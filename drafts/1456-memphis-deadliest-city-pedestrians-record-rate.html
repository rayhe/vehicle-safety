The Gap

Memphis Is the Deadliest Metro in America for Pedestrians. It Just Set the Record.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

Memphis is the worst place in America to cross the street. That is not an opinion, it is a measurement, and it just set a record. Smart Growth America's Dangerous by Design 2026 report puts Memphis at 5.5 pedestrian deaths per 100,000 residents for 2020 through 2024, the highest rate of any of the 101 largest metro areas in the country, and the highest the report has recorded in eleven years of ranking them.[1] In the first edition, back in 2009, no metro area in America scored above 3.52. This year the fourteen deadliest metros all clear that bar. Memphis clears it by more than half again.

Count the bodies and the record gets worse. Memphis buried 369 pedestrians in five years, up from 221 in the previous five, a 67% jump while the region's population stayed essentially flat.[4] Nearly 27% of everyone killed in a traffic crash in Memphis was on foot, against 18% nationally.[4][3] Memphis also carries the highest total traffic fatality rate of any big city in America, 30.77 deaths per 100,000 residents.[6] Walking there is not one risk among many, it is the risk.

Run the counterfactual, because the counterfactual is the whole story. Nationally, pedestrian deaths averaged 7,215 a year from 2020 through 2024, against an average population of about 334 million, a rate of roughly 2.16 per 100,000.[3] Apply that national rate to Memphis and the five-year toll comes out near 145 deaths, while Memphis recorded 369. That leaves roughly 225 people who would still be alive if walking in Memphis were as dangerous as walking in America generally, instead of two and a half times worse. Two hundred and twenty-five is not a statistical artifact, it is a small neighborhood, erased.

What makes the number sting is the direction of travel everywhere else. Pedestrian deaths fell nationally for a second straight year in 2024, to 7,080, down 3.9% from 2023.[3] Governors Highway Safety Association projects 6,732 for 2025, down another 7%, a third consecutive annual decline, with the national rate dropping below 2.0 per 100,000 for the first time since before the pandemic.[2] Tennessee itself is flat, 162 pedestrian deaths in 2024 and 162 projected for 2025, at a state rate of 2.26.[2] Memphis runs 2.4 times its own state's rate and 2.8 times the country's. The nation is getting safer for people on foot. Memphis is getting deadlier, and the gap is now a record.

Do not let the rankings fool you into thinking anyone is winning. Smart Growth America warns explicitly that a better ranking does not mean fewer deaths. Charleston, South Carolina moved from ninth to twelfth deadliest while burying more people. Tucson, Arizona moved from third to fourth while its death count rose by eighteen. Both "improved" only because other metros got worse faster.[1] Delaware cut its pedestrian fatality rate more than any other state in the country and still ranks tenth deadliest. Of the twenty deadliest states, nineteen showed no improvement at all or got more dangerous. A ranking is a sorting exercise, but the morgue is the measurement.

Some of the dead are not random: American Indian and Alaska Native pedestrians die at 3.7 times the national average. Black Americans die walking at 1.7 times the average. Together those two groups make up 16% of the population and 22% of pedestrian deaths from 2020 to 2024.[1] Smart Growth America puts it plainly: the more a community struggles financially, the more dangerous its roads tend to be. Memphis is a poor city with wide, fast roads running through poor neighborhoods, and the death toll follows the pavement, not the people.

And the pavement is only half the problem. The other half is what drives on it. SUVs and pickups account for more than half of pedestrian fatalities, and federal data shows tall, blunt front ends raise pedestrian fatality risk 43.6% compared with low, sloped ones.[2][5] Meanwhile the crashes themselves are turning more lethal: the share of pedestrian crashes that kill someone rose from 4% in 2015 to 10.1% in 2024, more than doubling, as vehicles got larger and heavier.[2] Bigger cars, same roads, worse odds. That is the entire American pedestrian story in one sentence, and Memphis is where it lands hardest.

None of this is mysterious: seventy-six percent of pedestrian deaths happen in the dark, seventy-three percent happen away from intersections, eighty-four percent happen in urban areas, and nearly one in four is a hit-and-run. Alcohol, driver or pedestrian, shows up in 43% of fatal pedestrian crashes.[3] Cross an expressway on foot and the fatality rate is 56.7%.[2] Every one of those numbers describes a road built to move cars quickly through places where people live, and a vehicle fleet built taller and heavier every year. Memphis did not invent any of that, it just stopped pretending it was working.

Fairness requires the counterweight, and it is real. Memphis is one metro of about 1.3 million people, and a five-year average can swing on a few dozen crashes. The national decline is partly a gift of comparison, since 2021 and 2022 were historic highs, and the projected 2025 total is still 5% above 2019. "Designed to kill" also overstates intent, because nobody voted to build a road that kills pedestrians. What got built was a system that treats moving cars fast as the goal and treats the people walking alongside as an afterthought, and the afterthought is where the bodies are. None of that changes the count: three hundred and sixty-nine people in five years, in one metro, at the highest rate ever measured, is not a rounding error, it is a verdict on the streets.

If you walk in Memphis, the data gives you the rules, and they are not complicated. Cross at signals, wear something reflective, and assume the driver cannot see you after dark, because three out of four deaths say that is usually true. If you drive an SUV or pickup, understand that your front end is the risk factor, and slow down on arterials, because speed is what decides whether a pedestrian crash is survivable at all. And if you live in Memphis, ask your council member a specific question: which proven countermeasures, narrower lanes, raised crosswalks, refuge islands, lower speed limits on arterials, are funded on the road nearest your house, and when do they get built? SGA's own math says that at the current pace of improvement, the country will not return to 2009 pedestrian death levels until 2042, and 96,615 more people will be killed walking before then.[1] Memphis cannot wait for the country, because the country is already leaving Memphis behind.
