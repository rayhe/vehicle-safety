# Research Notes — #845: Police Reports Undercount Pre-Crash Phone Use by 7x+

**News peg:** New IIHS study (published ~Sept 3-6, 2026), covered by Carscoops (Sept 6) and Auto Connected Car: telematics from safe-driving apps vs. police reports for ~17,000 crashes.

## Kill test
Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. IIHS ran the comparison safety researchers always wanted but couldn't: link real crash reports to phone telemetry from the same crashes. Result: **police reports recorded phone use in under 1% of crashes; the phones recorded it in 7-8%.** The official distracted-driving count is off by roughly an order of magnitude, and the category-level gaps are worse (hands-free calls: 87 to 1). ## Key numbers
- Sample: ~17,000 crashes (15,301 two-vehicle + 1,543 single-vehicle) in four states, 2021-2024, matched by time (15-min window) and location (1-mile radius) between CMT app telemetry and police reports.
- Phone activity within 30 seconds of impact: 7% of single-vehicle, 8% of two-vehicle crashes. Police reports for the same crashes: under 1%. At least a 7x undercount.
- Two-vehicle breakdown (telematics vs police, 30 sec): hand-held calls 19 vs 6; hands-free calls 87 vs 1; manipulation 590 vs 17; passive screen use 534 vs 15.
- Single-vehicle (telematics vs police): hand-held 3 vs 1; hands-free 3 vs 0; manipulation 49 vs 3; passive 52 vs 5.
- Both sources flagged phone use in the same crash but **agreed on the specific activity type in only 3 of ~17,000 crashes.**
- Narrowing the definition to active use only (hand-held/hands-free calls + manipulation, excluding passive screen) barely closed the gap.
- ~25% of drivers admit to watching video while driving at least once in the prior month (IIHS survey).

## Original calculations (novel contribution)
- Category-level ratios the coverage didn't headline: phone manipulation 590 vs 17 = **34.7x**; hands-free calls 87 vs 1 = **87x**; passive use 534 vs 15 = 35.6x. The undercount is worst precisely for the activity types officers can't see (nobody hears a hands-free call at a crash scene).
- National scale-up (rough, caveated): police report ~6.7M crashes/year in the US; 8% with pre-impact phone activity implies roughly **half a million crashes a year** with a phone active in the 30 seconds before impact, vs. the ~tens of thousands the official "distracted by phone" checkbox produces.
- The 3-in-17,000 agreement rate: when two independent systems do detect phone use, they disagree on what kind 99.98% of the time, which means even the "confirmed" phone crashes in FARS-era statistics are miscategorized.

## Strongest counterargument (must be stated at full strength)
Telematics "activity" is not distraction. An unlocked screen in the 30 seconds before impact includes turn-by-turn navigation, a podcast app, a music change, CarPlay-invisible use missed entirely. IIHS itself included passive screen use only "for a fuller comparison"; the study cannot say the phone caused the crash, who was at fault, or which app was open. And the sample is self-selected: drivers who voluntarily install safe-driving apps skew younger and possibly riskier than the general population. The 8% figure could overstate distraction while simultaneously understating phone use (CarPlay/Android Auto use never unlocks the screen). The truth is somewhere the study can't reach.

## Limitations
- Four states, 2021-2024; not nationally representative.
- CMT app users self-select; results may not generalize to all drivers.
- Matching is approximate (15-minute window, 1-mile radius); some links may be wrong.
- Cannot establish causation, fault, or which apps were in use; passive unlocks include navigation.
- Police reports aren't designed for this; officers arrive after the fact and drivers have every incentive to lie.

## Actionable takeaways
- If you're in a crash and suspect the other driver was on a phone: the police report is not proof they weren't. Phone records, subpoenaed, are. Ask your insurer or attorney early.
- If you use an insurance telematics app: your insurer already knows more about your pre-crash phone use than the police ever will. The discount has a witness attached.
- If you legislate: texting bans are aimed at a 2010 phone. Manipulation and passive screen use dominate the undercount; 21 states' hands-free laws (no holding the phone at all) are the only framework that matches what the data actually shows.
- If you drive: the two-second rule is the actionable bit. Eyes off road for two seconds doubles crash risk. The phone doesn't have to be "in use" to be the last thing you looked at.
