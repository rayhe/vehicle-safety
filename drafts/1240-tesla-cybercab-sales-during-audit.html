Tesla Will Sell You a Car With No Steering Wheel While NHTSA Decides Whether the Car Is Legal

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
December 4, 2027

On October 21, 2026, Tesla's engineering chief stood up on the Q3 earnings call and made a promise. The Cybercab, a gold two-seater with butterfly doors and no steering wheel, no pedals, and no mirrors, would be in customers' hands by the end of the year. "We're trying to get Cybercabs to customers by the end of this year, 2026," Lars Moravy told analysts, which is a bold thing to say about a vehicle whose federal paperwork is an open question.

9 days
Between Tesla's year-end sales promise and the deadline for its sworn answers to NHTSA

Hours after the Cybercab started carrying paying passengers in Austin on September 3, 2026, NHTSA opened Audit Query AQ26002 into how Tesla certified a vehicle without manual controls under safety standards written decades ago for human drivers. Tesla got a 30-day extension, and its answers are due October 30, 2026, which means the sales promise came nine days before the sworn paperwork.

Run that timeline again slowly, because the order is the story: a federal regulator has not decided whether this car was certifiable, and Tesla is already taking the product to market, which is either confidence or scheduling as a legal strategy.

Up to 1,000 Cybercabs fall under the probe, which examines whether Tesla correctly treated certain Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards as inapplicable to a car with no wheel and no pedals. Hundreds of the vehicles already appear in Texas DMV registries, and AI chief Ashok Elluswamy laid out the expansion plan on the same call: Dallas operations in November 2026, 24/7 robotaxi service in several cities by year-end. While the federal file stays open, the fleet keeps growing, and nobody at NHTSA can tap the brakes because there are no brakes to tap, metaphorically speaking.

There is a second docket too. On October 9, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened defect petition DP26010, alleging that about 1,000 Tesla robotaxis, Cybercabs and Model Ys, exceed posted speed limits in active school zones. The petition counts four incidents, and the tally of crashes, fires, injuries and fatalities sits at zero across the board. It is an evaluation step, not a finding, but consider the geometry: inside a Cybercab there is no driver and no pedal, so nobody is slowing down for the child you might want protected.

What actually changes with a private sale is the liability, not the hardware. A robotaxi in Tesla's own fleet is an operational asset with a safety chain somewhere above it. A titled Cybercab in a private buyer's name is a product sale, and you can never operate it, never seize control in an emergency, never grab a wheel that does not exist. Certification history moves with the title, and the audit is open while the titles are being cut. To be fair, Elon Musk said on the call that most Cybercabs will stay in Tesla's own transport-as-a-service fleet rather than being sold outright, so the private-buyer angle may be a footnote to a fleet story.

Underneath both dockets sits the regulatory asymmetry that made the car commercially possible. In most countries, a new model needs regulatory clearance before launch; in America, automakers put cars on public roads by self-certifying that they meet federal standards. The Cybercab exists in commercial service because of that asymmetry, not despite it. Even the government seems to think the rules are stale, since the Department of Transportation has proposed removing the manual-control requirements for vehicles designed to operate autonomously. A steering-wheel rule for a car with no driver is paperwork from a different century, and Tesla simply filled it out differently than everyone expected.

So no one has been hurt, no defect has been found, and the rules are probably wrong. And yet, if you are thinking about buying one, understand what you are actually purchasing: a ride, not a car. There is no control to grab, no pedal to stomp, no wheel to fight for. Read the AQ26002 docket at nhtsa.gov first. The federal question of whether this vehicle was certifiable is still open, with Tesla's sworn answers due October 30, and you would like your car to be legal before you pay for it.
