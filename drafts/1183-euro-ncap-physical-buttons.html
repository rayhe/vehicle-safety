Five Stars, Zero Buttons: Euro NCAP Started Grading Touchscreens, and Some Winners Flunked

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 15, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. Euro NCAP quietly added a new scored test to its 2026 protocol: cars now earn points for having physical buttons for the indicators, lights, wipers, washers, and mirror adjustment, the controls your hands reach for without thinking. The Leapmotor B05 scored 0 percent on key driving controls and 92 percent on crash protection, and Euro NCAP still handed it five stars.[1]

0%
The Leapmotor B05's score for key driving controls. It still earned five stars overall, with 92% for crash protection.

Here is the scoreboard from the 2026 tests so far. At the top: the BMW iX3 and Audi Q7, both at 100 percent for key driving controls. Cupra's Raval managed 80. Among Chinese brands, the Geely EX2 hit 80, while the Xpeng G9, Zeekr 7GT, and Aion UT scored 20 percent each, and the B05 scored zero. Euro NCAP itself notes "clear differences between European manufacturers and Chinese carmakers" on this front.[1] Audi's Q7 split its category score 100 percent on essential driving tasks and 80 percent on comfort and infotainment; the Xpeng G9, which runs its mirrors and lights through a touchscreen, scored 20 and 80.[1]

Why should a shopper care about a sub-score? Because the crash test grades the accident you may never have, while the button test grades the distraction you will have every single drive. Sweden's Vi Bilagare re-ran its cabin-distraction measurement this year: across ten new models, completing four basic tasks, bumping the temperature, switching the radio station, resetting the trip meter, dimming the interior lights, at 68 mph consumed an average 2,667 feet of road, a 7.5 percent regression from 2022.[2] Every one of those feet was traveled with eyes on a menu instead of the road. That is what the new Euro NCAP test is trying to price into the stars.

So here is the buying advice, free of charge. When you shortlist a car, look it up on euroncap.com and read the "Safe Driving" sub-score, not just the star count. On the test drive, try the wipers, the indicators, and the hazard lights without looking at a screen. If you cannot do all three by feel, you have found the zero percent. And if you shop in the United States, know that NHTSA's rating program does not score any of this, so the European page is the only public scoreboard, and only for models sold in Europe.[3]

Now the other side, stated fairly, because five stars did not become meaningless overnight. The star system is weighted toward the things that kill people: crash structure, automatic braking, post-crash rescue. That same Q7 just became the first production car to score 100 percent in Euro NCAP's post-crash safety category.[4] A B05 with 92 percent crash protection and zero percent buttons will still protect your body better in the crash that actually happens than a four-star car with perfect knobs. Buttons are a preference. Crumple zones are physics. That weighting is a judgment call, and you can argue it is the right one.

Limitations, because I am not a brochure. Euro NCAP tests European-market cars, and trims and equipment differ across markets; several of the lowest-scoring Chinese models are not sold in America at all. One slice, though: the controls score is only part of the overall rating, not the rating. Voice commands, steering-wheel shortcuts, and automation can cut eyes-off-road time without a single physical button, and Euro NCAP's scoring reflects an engineering opinion, not a measured crash-outcome study. What it does give you, for the first time, is a number for the thing you will touch ten thousand times before the airbags ever deploy.
