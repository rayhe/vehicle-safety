California Called Waymo an “Imminent Hazard” and Kicked It Off Every Freeway in the State

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 25, 2026

Freeways were supposed to be the easy part. No pedestrians, no cross traffic, no unprotected left turns, just lanes and signs and physics, the one environment where a computer should out-drive a human without breaking a sweat. On November 11, California’s Department of Motor Vehicles decided that a fleet of robotaxis could not be trusted with the easy part, and barred Waymo’s driverless cars from every freeway in the state, calling the operation an “imminent hazard to public safety.”[1] Within hours, Waymo pulled its cars off freeways in every market in America.

177 days
From the May 18 construction-zone incidents to the November 11 ban — a voluntary pause, a federal recall, a return to service, and a state ban, all over the same failure

Three Waymo vehicles drove into an active construction closure on U.S. 101 near Burlingame on November 5, with workers, vehicles and equipment in the zone.[2] Next day, a Waymo on Interstate 280 in San Francisco appeared to try to pass two California Highway Patrol cruisers running a traffic break, officers weaving across every lane with their lights on to slow traffic behind them, and the video went viral.[2] Nobody was hurt in either incident. That sentence is the whole problem, because regulators did not act because someone died; they acted because the same failure keeps happening in the one place where the people standing outside their cars have the least protection, and because it keeps happening after being fixed.

Run the timeline. Similar construction-zone incidents on May 18, 2026, a voluntary pause of freeway operations on May 21, and a June 18 NHTSA recall of 3,871 fifth-generation robotaxis because the software could enter a closed freeway construction zone and keep driving at speed, followed by a return to the freeways in July and a ban on November 11.[5] From the May incidents to the November ban is 177 days, and inside that span the same failure mode produced a voluntary pause, a federal recall, a return to service, and a state ban, while the June fix lasted about four months, which makes a remedy with a four-month shelf life a press release rather than a remedy.

Note the part that should worry every other operator. In May, Waymo pulled the plug itself, and in November the state did it, with a restriction that covers testing as well as rides, which means Waymo cannot even test its way back onto a California freeway without a human behind the wheel. California’s order applied only to California, yet Waymo grounded freeway rides in all five of its freeway markets, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Phoenix, Miami and Austin, because a construction-zone failure in Burlingame is not a California problem.[1][3] It is a Waymo problem, and it follows the cars wherever they drive at highway speed. Surface-street rides continue, and supervised freeway testing with trained specialists behind the wheel continues, but the driverless freeway, the thing the company spent a decade building toward, is closed until Waymo can show the DMV what it calls “effective corrective actions.”[1]

Now the case for the defense, stated at full strength, because it deserves to be heard before it is answered. Waymo’s own analysis of more than 270 million driverless miles shows 82 percent fewer injury-causing crashes and 95 percent fewer crashes resulting in serious injury or death than human drivers in the same cities, and freeways carry roughly half of all miles driven in California.[4] On those numbers, keeping the robotaxis off the freeways looks like a ban on the safer driver, and a regulator protecting the public by removing the vehicle least likely to crash. Fair enough. But the DMV’s answer is written into the order itself: an aggregate crash rate does not cover a specific failure, and a system that cannot read a construction zone or a police traffic break is dangerous in exactly the places where the humans around it are most exposed, because averages do not protect road workers and only reading the road does.

What this piece did not prove: nobody was hurt, so the hazard is prospective, and Waymo’s safety figures are the company’s own analysis of its own data, not an independent audit. NHTSA’s school-bus recall query, opened November 6, is a separate action and is not part of this ban. What to do with the rest: if you ride Waymo, expect surface streets and longer trips, especially to the airport, until the company satisfies the DMV. If you work in a highway construction zone, treat an approaching driverless car the way you would treat a distracted driver. And if you drive near one on a freeway, remember that a traffic break is a police maneuver, not a suggestion. The car that tried to pass the CHP cruisers is the reason the state acted.
