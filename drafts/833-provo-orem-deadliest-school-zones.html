The Gap

The Deadliest School Zones in America Are in Provo, Utah. The Speed Limit Signs Are Just Decoration.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 7, 2026

Before you sign the lease on the house "in a great school district," here is a number that will rearrange your priorities. A new analysis of twelve years of fatal-crash data names Provo-Orem, Utah the most dangerous metro in America for children walking or biking near schools: 5.01 deaths per 100,000 children, and all eight fatalities were pedestrians.[1] Not one was on a bike. Not one was inside the school zone's posted 25-mph limit's protection, because that protection turns out to be theoretical.

The study, published last month, ranked U.S. metros by child pedestrian and cyclist deaths (ages 5 to 18) near schools or on school-day commutes, adjusted for child population. Oxnard-Thousand Oaks-Ventura, California took second at 4.22 per 100,000, Des Moines third at 3.88, and Salt Lake City fourth at 3.66, with all nine of its deaths pedestrians too.[1] Las Vegas recorded the most deaths in raw numbers: thirteen, roughly one every year, within a half mile of a school. Utah appears twice in the top four, which is the kind of stat that should make the "family-friendly state" marketing team sweat.

Now the enforcement gap, which is the actual story. A Florida study of more than 25,000 vehicles in Palm Beach County school zones found over 70 percent of drivers speeding more than 20 mph over the posted limit during morning drop-off and afternoon pickup, with over 30 percent exceeding it by 30-plus.[4] Run the physics on that: a federal analysis puts the chance a struck pedestrian dies at roughly 5 percent at 20 mph or under, 40 percent at 30 mph, and 80 percent at 40 mph.[6] A school zone posted at 25 or lower, enforced by nobody, is a place where seven in ten drivers are traveling at speeds that turn a survivable impact into a 40-percent kill probability, and the third of drivers going 30-over reach 80 percent.[4] The sign is a suggestion. The study's authors put it more bluntly: half of American public schools have no crossing guard on surrounding streets, so the limit is self-enforced, and drivers routinely ignore it regardless of signage.[1]

Here is the part that should change how you parent, not just how you drive. NHTSA's decade of school-transportation crash data shows the school bus is the safest part of the trip. From 2014 to 2023, 209 school-age children died in school-transportation-related crashes, but only 38 were inside the school bus or van. Eighty-three were occupants of other vehicles. Seventy-nine were pedestrians.[2] Your kid's risk is not the yellow bus with the flashing reds and the stop arm. It is the walk to it, the crossing after it, and the pickup line around it. Two-thirds of those child pedestrians were killed somewhere other than an intersection, and more than half were between 5 and 10 years old, the age group most likely to dart into a driver's blind spot.[2] One law enforcement veteran quoted in the Florida coverage compared the sight lines of a consumer pickup to an Abrams tank, then added: these are little humans.[4]

A strange, useful finding: walking killed 2 to 3 times more kids than biking near schools in every top-ten metro, and in five of the ten, Provo, Salt Lake City, Colorado Springs, Milwaukee and Memphis, every single child killed was on foot.[1] Do not read that as "bikes are safe." Far more kids walk than bike, so the study can't give you per-trip risk, and it says so honestly. Read it instead as a map of where deaths cluster: at crossings, at drop-off and pickup points, along the arterial roads that border school property. The route is the risk factor, not the mode. A longer walk with zero mid-block arterial crossings beats a short one across a five-lane stroad every time.

Intellectual honesty corner, because the counterargument deserves airtime. Provo is a small metro; eight deaths over twelve years is a tiny numerator, and rankings built on tiny numerators are volatile. A metro can win this ranking the way a town wins the lottery: randomly, and once. Utah's double appearance suggests something structural rather than random, wide arterials plus dark winter mornings is my honest speculation, flagged as speculation, but a skeptic is entitled to note that per-capita rankings punish small denominators. Fair. Now consider the denominator-independent facts: 13 dead kids near Las Vegas schools in a decade, 79 child pedestrians dead nationally in school-transportation crashes over the same window, and 70 percent noncompliance with school zone limits measured in Florida.[1][2][4] No small-numerator argument survives those.

What to do with this, practically, since this column exists to tell you what to do. First, route selection beats mode selection: walk your kid's route yourself and count mid-block arterial crossings, because two-thirds of child pedestrians die away from intersections, then reroute or drive them on the bad days.[2] Second, teach the bus danger zone: stay ten feet from a stopped bus, take five giant steps before crossing in front, and make eye contact with the driver, which is NHTSA's actual guidance, not a slogan.[3] Third, ask your school and city the uncomfortable question: who enforces the school zone, and with what? If the answer is "a sign," you now know what that is worth. Automated enforcement works, and drivers themselves have pre-approved it: majorities tell pollsters they support speed cameras where crashes cluster.[7] Fourth, if you drive through a school zone, assume every kid is invisible to you and every sign is unenforced, because the data says both are true.
