Traffic Just Passed Gunfire as the Leading Killer of American Cops

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 5, 2026

NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison told state highway safety officials the kind of number that ends a speech. Traffic-related officer deaths are up 82% this year, he said, and now account for more than half of all line-of-duty officer fatalities, while total line-of-duty deaths fell 5%.[1] Fewer officers are dying overall, and more are dying in traffic: not gunfire, not a raid gone wrong, but the road itself, doing its ordinary business at 70 miles an hour while a cop stands next to it.

82%
Rise in traffic-related officer fatalities this year, per NHTSA

Preliminary figures from the National Law Enforcement Officers Memorial Fund count 68 officers dead through October 1, down from 84 in the same stretch of 2025, but 31 of those deaths were traffic-related, up from 21 a year earlier.[2] FBI data agrees from a different angle: 30 accidental officer deaths in the first nine months of 2026, ten more than the same period in 2025, and 25 of those 30 were traffic, 15 in crashes and 10 officers struck while outside their cars.[3] For scale, 2025 was the safest year for American police in 80 years: 111 deaths, 34 traffic-related.[4] At the 2026 pace, 31 traffic deaths in 274 days works out to roughly 41 for a full year, a reversal from a 23% decline in 2025 to a 48% rise through October 1.

Now the part that should make NHTSA uncomfortable. Its new national plan, Pathway to Safer Streets, unveiled in April, has one cornerstone above all others: putting officers back on traffic enforcement, vigorously, after years of pulling back.[1] Twenty million dollars in new grants will fund enforcement innovation. Civilian roads got safer meanwhile: 2025 traffic deaths fell 6.7% to 36,640, the lowest since 2019.[5] But the plan tracks civilian deaths, not the officers it sends into traffic, and a program that puts more people on the shoulder without counting how many the shoulder kills is grading its own homework with half the answers missing.

Fairness demands the strongest case against the alarm, and it is a good one. More enforcement means more exposure, so if enforcement hours rose faster than officer traffic deaths this year, the job got safer per hour worked while the body count rose, and no dataset here can separate those two facts. What exposure cannot explain is the struck-by column: officers are being hit while standing at someone else’s crash scene, directing traffic, or helping a disabled driver, and the national struck-by tracker lists exactly those activities as the moments officers get hit.[6] No enforcement-hours math turns that into a statistical artifact.

Before the advice, three caveats: every 2026 figure here is preliminary and will be revised; NHTSA’s 82% and the Memorial Fund’s 48% cover different windows and cannot be reconciled into one clean number; and FARS, the federal crash database this site lives in, does not record whether a victim wore a badge, so the deadliest dataset in American traffic safety is blind to this story. What you can do is unglamorous: move over a full lane for any stopped emergency vehicle, or slow down hard if you cannot, since observational research found 36% of drivers do neither.[7] If your car dies on the shoulder, get behind the guardrail. If you run an agency, the training exists, the grants exist, and the one metric NHTSA should publish, officer traffic deaths alongside civilian ones, does not yet. Ask for it.
