Your Backup Camera Runs on Your Radio. Your Radio Just Got Recalled.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 10, 2026
☕ 3 min read

I ran the numbers, then ran them again; they didn't get better. Stellantis is recalling 239,131 Ram 1500 pickups because a radio software error can replace the rearview camera image with a blue screen when the driver shifts into reverse, and the cause is not a camera failure or a wiring failure but a radio bug.[1] Read that again slowly, because the sentence that follows is the whole story: your federally mandated safety image is hosted on the entertainment computer, and the entertainment computer just blue-screened.

100%
FCA's estimated share of the 239,131 recalled Ram 1500s affected: not a bad batch of parts, but a bug in the code every truck shipped with.

Most recalls estimate a single-digit share of the population actually carries the defect. A 100 percent estimate is the company admitting the flaw is architectural: every truck runs the same buggy radio software, so every truck can lose its camera image at the exact moment the driver needs it.[1] I keep a mental regression of recall defect rates versus root causes, and the line is almost embarrassingly clean: hardware recalls cluster near zero, while software recalls cluster at one hundred.

FMVSS 111 has mandated rear visibility technology since 2018. Backover crashes kill about 210 people a year and injure 15,000; children under five account for 31 percent of the fatalities.[2] GAO estimated the rule would save 58 to 69 lives a year once the fleet turned over.[3] That entire benefit calculation assumed the image would be there. Nobody in the rulemaking modeled the failure mode where the display is fine, the camera is fine, and the radio in between decides today is a blue-screen day.

Here is the part that should bother you structurally. A compliant camera on a vehicle that already had a suitable display cost $43 to $45; a full standalone system cost $132 to $142.[3] The industry converged on sharing the infotainment head unit as the display because it made the mandate cheap, and now the safety function has no independent hardware path: if the head unit lags, crashes, or reboots mid-reverse, the federally mandated image dies with the playlist. The recall notice's owner guidance is admirably honest about this: stay aware of your surroundings and use your mirrors.[1] Translation: drive like it is 2017.

Fairness, then the counterweight, because I fit regressions to both sides. Zero crashes and zero injuries are attributed to this defect.[1] A blue screen announces itself; the driver knows the camera is gone and compensates with mirrors, exactly as drivers did for a century before the mandate. The fix is a free dealer software update, recall 83D, found through Stellantis's own investigation. Filed that way, this is a routine software recall and my architecture lecture is overthinking it.

Except the lecture survives the counterweight, because the mandate's benefit math never priced in a shared point of failure. If you drive a 2025 or 2026 Ram 1500, shift into reverse today and confirm the image appears; blue means get the free update now, not when the letter arrives, via Chrysler at 800-853-1403.[1] Until then, reverse with mirrors and a shoulder check, especially in driveways: the people FMVSS 111 was written for are disproportionately under five, and they are standing behind the truck, not in it. A 100-percent-affected recall is the one most worth a thirty-second VIN lookup at nhtsa.gov/recalls.
