The Gap

The Government Finally Graded Cars on Not Killing Pedestrians. GM and Stellantis Aren’t on the List.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. On September 22, the federal government did something it had never done in the nearly five decades of its 5-star safety ratings: it graded new cars on how gently they hit pedestrians.[1] One hundred and two models passed, and not one of them wears a Chevrolet, GMC, Buick, Cadillac, Jeep, Ram, Dodge, or Chrysler badge.

0
GM and Stellantis models that met NHTSA’s new pedestrian-protection criteria; Ford and Tesla managed one apiece.

Start with the brands that made it: Subaru, Toyota, Lexus, Nissan, Infiniti, Mitsubishi, Mazda, Honda, Acura, Hyundai, Kia.[2] Then Tesla with exactly one car, the Model 3, and Ford with exactly one, the gas Explorer, bringing the manufacturer count to ten. Count the countries of origin and you get three: Japan, Korea, and America, with America contributing precisely two nameplates. General Motors, the largest automaker in the country, contributed none, and neither did Stellantis.

29
Years Europe has graded cars on pedestrian protection; America’s first ratings arrived September 22, 2026.

The test itself is three acts of simulated violence: a child-sized and adult-sized headform hurled at the hood and windshield, an upper-leg impactor slammed into the hood’s leading edge, and a full lower-limb impactor driven into the bumper.[1] Engineers score the damage to the fake body parts the way coroners score real ones. A low, forgiving front end passes; a tall brick wall of a grille does not.

Europe has been doing this since 1997: Euro NCAP folded pedestrian protection into its star ratings in 2009, added pedestrian auto-braking in 2015, and NHTSA’s own final decision document cites Euro NCAP protocols from 2018 and 2020 as the methodology it is now implementing, nearly three decades late.[3][4] That lag has a body count attached to it, though precisely how many pedestrians died because American hoods stayed tall is a number nobody can compute, which is its own indictment.

Now ask why Detroit is missing, because the answer is structural, not conspiratorial. The test rewards low hood leading edges and forgiving front ends. GM and Stellantis sell their highest volumes in full-size pickups and tall SUVs, vehicles whose front ends are, by design, the opposite of forgiving. Subaru doesn’t sell a full-size pickup, and neither does Honda. Of course their hoods are lower; they have no 5,500-pound ladder-frame flagship to protect. Nobody designed this rating to punish trucks; physics did that, and the rating just finally wrote it down, with remarkably little fanfare.

This isn’t part of the star rating, and it won’t be on the window sticker. It lives in a separate category on NHTSA’s website, which is where safety information goes to be technically public and practically invisible.[5] NHTSA’s published plan also says the first model years are scored on data the manufacturers submit themselves.[3] Read that again: the inaugural federal pedestrian safety grades are, per the agency’s own final decision, computed from homework the automakers turned in. GM and Stellantis didn’t fail this test; they simply didn’t turn in homework that passed, or any homework at all.

Stated at full strength, the other side of this is that a forgiving hood is the consolation prize. The real prize is never hitting the person, which is a matter of auto-braking, speed, and road design, not hood sculpting. Pedestrian deaths also track how much people walk and how lethal the streets are, and a rating can’t rebuild a stroad. And 102 models is a fraction of the roughly 300 nameplates on sale, so most of the 2027 lot is still ungraded.

So here’s what you do. If you drive in a city, or your kids walk to school, check the pedestrian category on NHTSA.gov/ratings before you buy, because it’s there now, separate from the stars, and the dealer won’t mention it since it isn’t on the sticker. Cross-check Euro NCAP if the model sells overseas; they’ve been grading this for 29 years. And when you look at a car, look at the front end the way a pedestrian would: low and rounded beats tall and flat, every time. Your next car’s hood is a crumple zone for someone else’s head, so buy accordingly.
