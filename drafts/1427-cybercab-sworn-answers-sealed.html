Tesla Answered NHTSA's Sworn Cybercab Questions. Then It Asked to Seal the Answers.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

On October 30, Tesla did what the federal government asked. It answered 21 questions, under oath, explaining how a car with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no mirrors legally complies with American safety standards. Then it filed a second document, one page long, asking NHTSA to keep the first one secret. That letter reads, in full: “The entirety of this file is confidential business information.”[1] Fifty-seven days earlier, the car it describes had started carrying paying passengers on public roads in Austin.

Pages of Tesla's sworn safety case the public can read

Count what the public file actually holds, because the inventory is the story. Three Tesla documents sit in the docket for audit query AQ26002. First, the one-page letter quoted above. Second, a placeholder where the answer to Request 8 should be, the question that asked Tesla to explain, standard by standard, how it determined the Cybercab complies, which subpart it certified to, and whether it ran physical tests, simulations, or engineering analyses. Tesla requested confidentiality “for the entirety of each document” in that answer, too. Third, the only document with any content at all: a 30-page First Responder Interaction Plan, version 1.2, dated September 3.[1] Useful if you are a firefighter cutting someone out of a wreck. Useless if you want to know how the car passed its own safety exam. Two of three filings are empty. Zero pages of the safety case are public.

Rewind the tape, because the deadline is the tell. NHTSA opened the audit on September 3, the same day commercial Cybercab rides began in Austin, noting that Tesla had certified about 1,000 vehicles as compliant with all applicable federal standards despite their lack of conventional manual controls.[4] Seven days later the agency escalated, issuing a Special Order signed by its Chief Counsel, Peter Simshauser, with 21 detailed questions and answers under oath. Incomplete answers carry civil penalties of up to $139 million. False ones can bring criminal charges. Tesla asked on September 18 for eight more weeks, to November 20, citing the voluminous scope of the questionnaire. NHTSA granted 30 days instead, moving the deadline to October 30, and Tesla filed on time.[3] On time, under oath, and sealed. Every step of that sequence was public except the one step that matters, which is the answer itself.

What NHTSA asked is worth spelling out, because none of it sounds like a formality. How does a car with no foot controls meet FMVSS 135, the federal braking standard? How are turn-signal cancellation and mirror rules satisfied on a vehicle that has no mirrors? Did Tesla certify the car with temporary controls bolted on for testing and removed afterward? And why did it not seek a Part 555 exemption, the legal route for cars that cannot meet a standard as written?[2] Zoox took that route. In July, NHTSA approved its exemption petition for limited commercial deployment of steering-wheel-free robotaxis, a first for the industry, through a public process capped at 2,500 vehicles per manufacturer per year.[5] Tesla skipped the queue entirely, certified itself, and when the auditor came calling, handed over the homework with a cover sheet that says nobody may read it.

Run the arithmetic, because the numbers are small enough to hold in one hand. Divide the $139 million maximum penalty by the roughly 1,000 Cybercabs in the audit population and the price NHTSA put on a complete answer is $139,000 per car. Count the calendar: 57 days passed between the first paying ride and the sealed filing, 57 days of passengers riding in a vehicle whose federal safety case the public has never seen. Tesla, meanwhile, says it aims to get Cybercabs to customers by the end of 2026, with no published price and no ordering process, and no federal authorization to sell the car has been confirmed in public reporting.[7] Filing an answer does not authorize sales, and it does not close the audit. NHTSA can read the response, ask follow-up questions, or take further steps. What it cannot do, for now, is show the public what Tesla wrote.

Fairness demands the strongest case against this story, stated straight, because it is a good case. Confidentiality claims under 49 CFR Part 512 are routine, not sinister. Automakers file them constantly for engineering data, test procedures, and supplier information that genuinely is commercially sensitive, and a standard-by-standard map of how Tesla certified a car with no controls is exactly the kind of document its competitors would love to read for free. A blanket claim is the standard opening move, and NHTSA's Chief Counsel adjudicates it; if the claim is overbroad, the agency can order a public version released. Secrecy is not evidence of noncompliance, and NHTSA has explicitly not found the Cybercab noncompliant with any standard.[1] Tesla shares fell 10.8 percent on November 5, the day the filing surfaced, but that move coincided with broader market turbulence, and pinning it on one docket entry overstates the case.[6] Here is the honest version of the claim, narrower and harder to dismiss: a car carrying paying passengers on public roads was certified by its own manufacturer, the federal audit of that certification is now a sealed file, and the public's only window into the vehicle is a guide written for firefighters. That is the claim. Part 512 being routine is the limit of it, not the refutation.

What this piece did not prove, stated plainly. Electrek is the primary reporter of the filing's contents, and the docket PDFs were not independently re-read for this article, so the letter's wording is attributed to its reporting. NHTSA has not ruled on the confidentiality claim, and no outcome is predicted here. The $139 million figure is the maximum civil penalty for incomplete answers, not a fine imposed or threatened. The 10.8 percent share move is reported as coinciding with the filing's disclosure, not caused by it. And the roughly 1,000-vehicle population is NHTSA's estimate from the audit's opening resume; Texas state records counted 45 Cybercabs registered as of September 4.[5]

What you can actually do with this starts in Austin. If you ride in a Cybercab, know what you are riding in: a vehicle its manufacturer self-certified, not one that went through the public exemption process Zoox used, and the federal audit of that certification is open with its answers sealed. That is a fact about the ride, not a reason to refuse it, but it is worth knowing before you get in. If you follow the case, the AQ26002 docket is the place to watch. Confidentiality requests go to NHTSA's Chief Counsel under Part 512, and if a public version is ordered, it will appear there. And if you are waiting to buy one, Tesla has published no price and no order process, and no federal authorization to sell the Cybercab has been confirmed anywhere in public reporting. Do not put down a deposit on a car whose safety case is a sealed file.
