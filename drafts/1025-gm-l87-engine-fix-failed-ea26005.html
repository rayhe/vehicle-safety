GM's Fix for 600,000 Failing V8s Was Thicker Oil. 499 of Them Failed Anyway.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

Picture the engineering meeting where somebody said it: we have a machining defect in the rotating assembly of 600,000 V8 engines, and our repair for the ones that pass inspection is a different oil. Somebody nodded. On April 17, 2025, General Motors recalled 597,571 US trucks and SUVs over crankshaft and connecting-rod defects in the 6.2-liter L87 V8, defects that can spin a bearing, snap a rod, seize the engine, and strand a 6,000-pound vehicle without propulsion at speed.[3] Vehicles that failed a diagnostic scan for trouble code P0016 got new engines. The other 97% got an oil change. On August 20, 2026, NHTSA opened Engineering Analysis EA26005 into 997,743 GM vehicles, because 499 owners reported their engines failed after the official recall repair, and 26 of those had received full replacement engines.[1][5]

26
post-remedy failures involved engines GM had fully replaced, not just re-oiled

First, the defect itself, because it is genuinely ugly. GM traced the L87 failures to a supplier's machining: sediment left inside connecting rods and crankshaft oil galleries, chewing up rod bearings, plus crankshafts that arrived out of dimensional spec or with improper surface finish.[4] This is not a software glitch or a bad sensor. It is the rotating assembly, the heart of the engine, damaged at the factory. GM's own pre-recall file review found 28,102 field complaints; 14,332 alleged the vehicle lost propulsion while moving.[3] Twelve crashes, twelve injuries, and forty-two fires were flagged as possibly related.

Now the remedy, which is where my professional composure leaves the room. A thicker oil film does raise hydrodynamic bearing strength, and for marginal clearances it is a legitimate mitigation. But it cannot un-machine a contaminated crankshaft gallery. Roughly 3% of recalled vehicles were estimated to fail the P0016 scan and earn a new engine; everyone else drove home with 0W-40 where 0W-20 used to be, a new fill cap, a new filter, and an owner's manual insert.[5] For the large majority of owners, the fix for a bottom-end machining defect was a viscosity number.

Nobody else has run the undercount math, so I will. ODI received 499 post-remedy failure complaints against roughly 597,571 remedied US vehicles: about 0.084%. But GM itself reported receiving 6,953 complaints of post-remedy engine failure.[5] That is 1.16%, and a fourteen-fold gap between what the manufacturer heard and what the regulator logged. The original recall took more than 14,000 field complaints to happen.[3] The same reporting gap is now sitting on top of the remedy itself, halfway to the threshold that forced the first recall.

Two more numbers deserve their own paragraph, because each breaks a comforting assumption. The 26 failures in fully replaced engines mean the remedy's strongest branch did not hold everywhere. And 191 failures involve L87 engines built after the original recall's production window, which GM bounded at March 1, 2021 through May 31, 2024, with supplier process improvements claimed before June 2024.[1] If the defect was a closed supplier problem, fixed by mid-2024, what is failing in the later engines? Either the window is wider than stated, or the root-cause story was never the whole story.

Fairness requires the steel man, so here it is at full strength. Thicker oil is real mitigation, not placebo, and every engine that tripped the P0016 scan was replaced. A 0.05% complaint rate across nearly a million hard-worked truck engines could include plenty of unrelated wear, and NHTSA opens Engineering Analyses routinely, closing most without demanding new action.[1] Caveats for the honest ledger: VOQs are self-reported, not confirmed failures; nobody has published the remedy completion rate, so denominators are estimates; GM's 6,953 likely includes duplicates; and there is no public teardown data on the 26 replacement-engine failures or the 191 post-window ones, so root causes are unconfirmed. The agency has asked questions. It has not announced answers.

What to actually do. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for recall 25V-274, because this investigation is engine-specific, not model-specific: a Silverado with the 5.3L L84 is not involved. Confirm the L87 by the RPO sticker in your glovebox, not by guessing from the trim badge. Learn the warning signs from GM's own filing: knocking or banging, check-engine light, hesitation, abnormal shifting, reduced propulsion, no-start.[4] Keep every recall and repair document with dates and mileage. And if the V8 starts knocking on the highway, do not nurse it home to see if it clears up. A seized engine at 70 mph is a loss-of-propulsion event, which is exactly why this is a safety file instead of a warranty file.
