Congress Picked the One City It Controls to Kill Helmet Laws. DC's Motorcycle Deaths Just Tripled.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better. On August 6, 2026, two members of Congress who answer to voters in Wisconsin and Montana introduced bills to delete the motorcycle helmet law of a city whose residents cannot vote for either of them, in a year when DC motorcycle deaths tripled, from two in all of 2025 to six by late summer 2026, according to Fox 5's reporting.[2] Call it what the sponsor's own office calls it: a beachhead. Spokeswoman Grace Kim told the Washington Post that "D.C. is an important place to start" because changing policy there "opens the door to having a larger conversation about the federal government's role in helmet mandates across the country."[5] DC is not the target. DC is the pilot program.

42%
Median increase in motorcycle fatalities after states repealed universal helmet laws, across 20 study arms in the Community Guide systematic review

Mechanics first, because the bill is narrower than the slogan. H.R. 10067 in the House and S. 5274 in the Senate, both titled the Ride in Freedom Act, would bar the District from requiring protective helmets for operators and passengers age 18 and older.[3] Under-18 requirements would stay, which means DC's universal law becomes Wisconsin's law: helmets for kids, a choice for adults. Rep. Derrick Van Orden of Wisconsin, a Harley rider who rides to Capitol Hill votes and co-chairs the Congressional Motorcycle Caucus, says riders "should have the choice to wear a helmet or not" and calls a motorcycle "a symbol of freedom."[5] His House bill went to the Oversight Committee and, as of early September, had no cosponsors. A bill with no cosponsors is a press release with a number on it, except this press release names the exact city where it wants to run the experiment.

Now the prediction, because this experiment has already been run, repeatedly, in states. A Community Guide systematic review of universal helmet laws found that enacting one raises helmet use by a median 49 percentage points and cuts fatalities by a median 32 percent, while repealing one drops helmet use by a median 41 points and raises fatalities by a median 42 percent.[4] Texas repealed and watched helmet use fall 30 percent while fatalities jumped 21 percent. Kentucky saw deaths rise 50 percent after its 1998 repeal. Louisiana saw them double after its 1999 repeal. Syracuse University's Lerner Center tracked 21 years and found universal-law states carried a 33 percent lower age-adjusted head-fatality rate than everyone else, every single year, without exception.[6] When a policy's effect size shows up that consistently, across that many states and decades, calling the next repeal an open question is a choice, not an observation.

DC's own arithmetic makes the timing worse, because NHTSA's 2024 motorcycle data counts 6,228 riders killed nationally, with injuries up 5 percent, and IIHS puts motorcycle deaths up 23 percent since 2019.[1] Against that backdrop, DC's registered motorcycle fleet is microscopic, 4,610 vehicles in the 2023 tabulation, which means six deaths imply roughly 13 deaths per 10,000 registered motorcycles, about 1.8 times the 2023 national average of 7.1.[6] Small numbers swing wildly, one crash moves DC's rate by more than two points, and I will not pretend otherwise. But the direction is not noise: two deaths became six while the fleet stayed tiny, and Congress picked that moment to propose removing the one rule that makes those crashes survivable. NHTSA estimates helmets cut rider fatality risk by roughly 22 to 42 percent and brain-injury risk by 41 to 69 percent. Maryland and Virginia, DC's neighbors, both require helmets for all riders, so a repeal would carve a helmet-optional island into the middle of a universal-law region, exactly where commuters cross the line every morning.

History adds the irony, thickly, because in 1967 the federal government told states to pass universal helmet laws or lose highway safety money, and by 1975 all but three had complied. In 1976 Congress revoked its own penalty power, states started carving out adults, and the map has been bleeding ever since: 17 states plus DC still hold the universal line, 47 states have some form of helmet law, and only Illinois, Iowa, and New Hampshire have none.[3] For sixty years federal leverage pushed one direction. This bill uses the one jurisdiction where Congress still writes the law directly to push the other way, and its sponsor says so out loud. DC Council Member Charles Allen, who chairs the Council's Transportation Committee, called it "an unserious and unsafe proposal."[2] IIHS statistical chief Eric Teoh was blunter: "Weakening that will result in people dying."[5] Supporters, led by the Motorcycle Riders Foundation, answer that DC is where freedom of choice belongs "at the pinnacle," because visiting riders chafe at a rule their home states do not impose.

What to do: wear a DOT FMVSS 218 helmet no matter what any legislature says, because a repeal changes the ticket, not the physics. That standard means a hard shell, a thick rigid lining, a chin strap that stays put, and 105 degrees of peripheral vision per side. Beware novelty helmets carrying counterfeit DOT stickers; they meet no standard and fail exactly like wearing nothing, while Snell certification, marked inside the helmet, is the stricter voluntary check. If you ride into DC from Maryland or Virginia, treat the border as the trap: both neighbors require helmets for all riders, so the District would become the one stretch of your commute where the law stops requiring the thing that saves your life, so keep it on across the line. And if you live in DC, know where the decision actually sits: not with voters you can punish, but with the House Oversight Committee, where H.R. 10067 waits, and with a nonvoting delegate as your only voice in the room.

What this does not prove: the bill may never pass, and a bill in committee is a strategy, not a law. DC's six deaths are a small count, and one bad weekend moves the rate more than any policy could. Helmet-use status of the six 2026 DC victims is not public in the cited reporting, so nobody can honestly say a helmet would have saved any particular one of them. Repeal effects come from state-level studies, not a city-level repeal, and DC's commuter influx from two universal-law states muddies any clean transfer. National 2024 figures come from NHTSA's Annual Report File, which gets revised slightly with the next annual report. All of that is real, and none of it rescues the bill's logic, because the bill's own stated purpose is to start a national rollback, and the national evidence on rollbacks points one way.

Strongest case against this story, stated fairly: helmets do not prevent crashes, they only soften them, so a freedom-minded rider can reasonably say the state should police the crash, not the wardrobe, and DC's real killers might be speed and impairment rather than bare heads. Grant every word of that, because it still misses the mechanism. A repeal does not reduce crashes, it converts survivable crashes into fatal ones at a rate the literature has measured over and over, median 42 percent, and it does so in a city whose residents never got to vote on the trade. Freedom for a visiting rider is a funeral for a local one, and the sponsor's office has already told us DC is only the first stop.
