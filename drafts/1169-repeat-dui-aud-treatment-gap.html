84% of Repeat Drunk Drivers Have a Diagnosable Disease. Almost None Get Treated.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 23, 2027

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, America's drunk-driving problem is not really a driving problem. It is an untreated-disease problem that happens to own car keys. NHTSA's final 2024 count: 11,904 people killed in alcohol-impaired crashes, 30 percent of every traffic death in the country.[1] Of those, 8,097 died in crashes where a driver was at 0.15 BAC or higher, nearly twice the legal limit: not borderline cases, not one glass of wine with dinner, but the severe end of the distribution doing nearly all of the killing.

84%
Share of repeat drunk drivers who meet DSM-5 criteria for alcohol use disorder, per a new analysis of federal traffic, health, and enforcement data

That figure comes from an analysis by the law firm Siegfried & Jensen, published this month, and you should know the source because plaintiff lawyers have a financial interest in the disease framing.[6] But the federal datasets underneath it are not theirs. SAMHSA's 2024 survey found 27.9 million Americans met the clinical criteria for alcohol use disorder in the past year.[3] And SAMHSA's 2025 release reports that among people who needed substance-use treatment, roughly 85 percent never received it. FDA-approved medications for alcohol use disorder exist, yet they reached only 2.6 percent of the people who needed them.[4] Read that again, then consider what it means: we built an enforcement machine that detects this disease at a traffic stop, at scale, every night, and then declines to treat it.

$167.8B
Estimated comprehensive societal cost of 2024's alcohol-impaired deaths: 11,904 fatalities times NHTSA's $14.1M cost per fatality

That number is my own calculation, and the inputs are both federal: NHTSA's fatality count times the agency's comprehensive cost per traffic death, $14.1 million in 2024 dollars.[5] Now stack the treatment price next to it. A year of generic naltrexone, one of the FDA-approved options, runs roughly $500 to $1,500. The ratio of one prevented death to one year of medication is on the order of ten thousand to one. Somewhere in America tonight, a repeat offender will blow a 0.19 at a checkpoint, get booked, fined, and released back into the 85 percent. The system will spend six figures processing him and zero dollars treating him, which is not a policy so much as a very expensive ritual.

Now the case against everything I just wrote, at full strength, because the obvious objection is better than you think: treatment is not a silver bullet, and plenty of repeat offenders have already cycled through court-ordered programs with miserable completion rates. Ignition interlocks cut reoffending dramatically while installed, and Utah's 0.05 law moved even heavy drinkers. Nothing in the data proves the counterfactual, that treating these drivers would have prevented those 11,904 deaths, and coerced treatment underperforms voluntary treatment. And the 84 percent number arrives via a law firm that gets paid when drunk driving looks like a medical problem. All fair. What the counterargument misses anyway: nobody is proposing treatment instead of consequences, so interlock the car and treat the driver. Incapacitation plus medicine beats either one alone, and right now we are doing exactly one of those things.

So here is what to do with this. If someone you love has a second DUI, the numbers say the odds are about five in six that this is a diagnosable medical condition, not a willpower deficit. Ask whether anyone, court or clinician, has ever done an alcohol use disorder assessment. SAMHSA runs a treatment locator at findtreatment.gov. Ask a doctor about the medications; they exist, they are generic, and almost nobody who needs them gets them. And for the policy crowd: every state that mandates interlocks for convicted offenders should staple an AUD screening and a treatment referral to the interlock order, the way NHTSA's own behavioral research recommends. We currently pay around $168 billion a year for the arrangement we have. The alternative is cheaper by four orders of magnitude, which is not a moral argument but a box score.
