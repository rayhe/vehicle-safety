Sports Cars Are the Drunkest, Deadliest Class on the Road. Your Sports Car Probably Isn’t.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 2, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, sports cars have the most impaired drivers of any vehicle class in America. 22.5% of sports-car drivers in fatal crashes test positive for alcohol or drugs, across 14,061 drivers and a full decade of federal data.[1] Sports cars also post the highest fatality rate of any class: 1.95 deaths per 100 million miles, triple the SUV rate. Put those two facts together and the conclusion writes itself. It is also wrong.

−0.008
Correlation between a model’s fatality rate and its drivers’ impairment, across 262 models

Start with the chart that lies to you. Rank the five vehicle classes by fatality rate, then rank them by driver impairment, and the two lists match almost perfectly: sports cars first in both, vans last in both, everyone else in between. The correlation is 0.94, which in my line of work is the statistical equivalent of a slam dunk. Drunk drivers, deadly cars, case closed. Somebody print the bumper sticker.

Now run the same math one level down, model by model instead of class by class, and the slam dunk turns into an airball. Across 262 individual models, the correlation between how impaired a model’s drivers are and how deadly the model is comes out to negative 0.008. That is not a weak correlation. That is the mathematical signature of no relationship whatsoever, wearing a trench coat. Knowing a model’s toxicology tells you nothing about its body count. This desk ran that number once before and got the same nothing; I reran it on a bigger sample and the nothing got bigger.[1]

The sports-car class itself proves the point from the inside. Its deadliest member is the Hyundai Veloster, at 8.54 deaths per 100 million miles, and its drivers are among the soberest in the class at 17.4% impaired. Its drunkest member is the Chevrolet Corvette, at 26.2% impaired, sitting mid-pack on fatality rate at 1.52.[1] If booze were doing the killing, the Veloster’s drivers would be pickled and the Corvette’s would be sipping sparkling water. They are nearly the reverse. The Mustang (6.02 dead, 21.9% impaired) and the Camaro (3.44 dead, 23.0% impaired) fill out the middle like a bar tab nobody wants to claim.

What you are looking at has a name, and the name is the ecological fallacy: a correlation that exists between groups and evaporates inside them. Countries that eat more chocolate win more Nobel Prizes. Neighborhoods with more storks have more babies. Sports cars are the chocolate here. The class-level pattern is arithmetically real and practically useless, because no individual driver experiences the class average. You do not drive “sports cars.” You drive a Veloster or a Corvette, and those two vehicles have almost nothing in common except a marketing department.

Now the steelman, because somebody has to. Five data points is a joke sample for a correlation coefficient; drop any single class and the 0.94 could collapse like a bad alibi. And the class pattern might be pure demographics wearing a fender badge. Sports-car buyers skew young and male, the two demographics that independently crash more and drink more, so “sports cars are drunkest and deadliest” might just be “young men are drunkest and deadliest” with better styling. The model-level zero, built on 262 models, is the sturdier number. It says the car does not matter much. The driver does. Which, frankly, has always been this column’s position.

The honest accounting, then. FARS only records fatal crashes, so this is a census of the dead, not a survey of the driving. “Impaired” here means any detectable blood alcohol or any drug-positive toxicology, not necessarily over the legal limit or behaviorally impaired; cannabis metabolites linger for days. The fatality rates ride on estimated miles traveled, not odometer readings. And the class labels are the dataset’s own taxonomy, which is why a Hyundai Veloster counts as a sports car. Even with all that, the gap between the two correlations is too wide to be a rounding error. One of these numbers describes groups. The other describes cars. Only one of them can tell you anything about yours.

So here is the actionable part, and it is short. Do not shop by class stereotype. Do not set enforcement priorities by it. Do not let an insurer’s actuary do your thinking, even though the actuaries already know all of this and priced your Corvette accordingly. Look up the specific model’s numbers: its fatality rate, its toxicology profile, its crash-test scores. The class average is a story about other people’s cars. Yours has its own data. Read that instead.[2][3]
