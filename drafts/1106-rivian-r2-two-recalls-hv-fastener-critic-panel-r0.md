## Scores

### 1. General Editor — 9.0
Structure is textbook Crash Report: kicker, headline, lede with bolded stat, pull stat, four body paragraphs, action block, references, disclaimer. Hook lands in the first two sentences. The two-recall contrast sustains the piece; the close ("the software works, and the wrench needs watching") is quotable. Minor debit: "Both, probably." is a touch cute, but it reads as Rex shrugging, not as filler. Would publish.

### 2. Voice Coach — 9.0
Rex's deadpan noir is intact and non-transferable: "the robot needed a human to notice," "a bureaucracy cosplaying as one," "the only sensor that caught it had a pulse." No banned phrases, zero em dashes, rhythm gate passes with real variance (variance 2244, not clustered). Opinions are stated, not hedged ("Fourteen cars at a 100 percent defect rate means the automated station was not occasionally wrong"). Starts mid-thought, no throat-clearing. You could not swap Clara's byline onto this.

### 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0
Fair at full strength: the counterargument paragraph gives Rivian its best case (voluntary filing, week-long investigation, zero injuries, fastest fix in history) without strawmanning. No victim-blaming, no self-congratulation, no moralizing at owners. The action block is responsible advice (check VIN, confirm repair logged, treat OTA updates seriously). No ethical flags.

### 4. Social / Shareability — 8.5
Two strong pull quotes ("the only sensor that caught it had a pulse"; "Your car fixed itself overnight and then the company sent you a letter about it"). Headline is specific and surprising without being clickbait. Kicker taxonomy consistent. Debit: the story's natural audience is EV-interested readers; the pull-stat (14) needs its label for context, which the label provides. Solid, not viral.

### 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0
Every factual claim traces to a cited source: campaign numbers (26V597, 26V625), build windows, dates, delivery counts, pricing, and the TechCrunch-via-Autoblog "all 14 at service centers" detail, which is explicitly attributed as press-sourced rather than from the NHTSA filing. FMVSS 111 (rear visibility) and FMVSS 126 (stability control prompt) are described correctly. No invented URLs; all five links came from live search results. The disclaimer states exactly what is unknown (torque spec, shortfall magnitude, R2-specific delivery counts).

### 6. Research Rigor — 8.5
Original contribution is real: the four-month compression timeline (June 9 deliveries -> Sept 23 -> Oct 4) quantified as a rate; the inversion (biggest recall in company history functionally invisible, smallest recall the most old-fashioned); the human-caught-the-robot-miss angle; the paperwork-lag observation across both campaigns. Limitations are stated, not hand-waved (14-car population, 100% estimate applies only to that population, torque spec undisclosed). Counterargument given at full strength in the body, not buried. Debit: the Stellantis 26V531 (844,027 vehicles, same FMVSS 111 wave) appears in research but was cut from the article for length; acceptable, but the "industry-wide camera wave" claim is therefore unmade.

### 7. Data Presentation — 8.5
Pull stat (14) with a label that carries the contrast (98,828 vs 14). Campaign numbers, build windows, percentages (96% OTA, 100% defect estimate), and delivery figures are all inline with sources. The recall-population breakdown (62,259 / 34,088 / 2,481) lives in the references. Debit: a two-row campaign comparison table would have made the inversion visual, but the prose carries it and the 500-word budget is respected.

## Verdict
**SHIP** — all 7 critics ≥ 8.5 (average 8.79), all hard gates pass, round 0. No revision round needed.
