A Cracked Capacitor Can Kill Your Minivan's Stability Control. 2,017 Were Recalled.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 17, 2026

Inside the brake pedal assembly of 2,017 Chrysler minivans sits a capacitor smaller than a grain of rice. In roughly one in ten of them, that capacitor cracked. When it did, the minivan's electronic stability control switched itself off, and the only announcement was a dashboard light most drivers would never decode. Chrysler's own filing with federal regulators warns the failure can cause "a vehicle crash without prior warning."[1]

Let's talk about what the car lost, because the numbers are genuinely staggering. IIHS research finds electronic stability control cuts the risk of a fatal single-vehicle crash roughly in half. For rollovers the effect is even more dramatic: ESC reduces fatal single-vehicle rollover risk by 72 percent in cars and 75 percent in SUVs.[2] NHTSA credits the technology with saving an estimated 4,100 lives between 2010 and 2014, including 1,580 in 2014 alone.[2]

Now consider the vehicle. The Pacifica and Voyager are tall, heavy family haulers that seat up to eight people, which is exactly the body style where rollover protection matters most. A minivan loaded with kids and cargo carries a high center of gravity and a great deal of momentum, and ESC is the system that selectively brakes individual wheels to keep all of that mass pointed where the driver intended. Remove it and you have reverted a 2026 minivan to 1990s crash-avoidance technology, except the driver has no idea the reversion happened.

The defect is almost insulting in its modesty. A single capacitor inside the brake pedal position sensor, built by CTS Corporation of Elkhart, Indiana, developed a crack.[1] That is the entire failure chain. One cracked capacitor, part number 68236598AA, and the most effective crash-avoidance system ever mandated stops working. Federal standard FMVSS 126 requires stability control to be operational during every phase of driving, including acceleration, coasting, and deceleration, which is why a dead ESC is an automatic federal noncompliance even though nobody has reported a crash.[1]

Then there is the timeline, and it deserves scrutiny. Chrysler's safety organization opened its investigation on March 25, 2026.[1] Engineers spent the spring chasing root cause and the summer analyzing field records, and on August 5 the company recognized it had a build issue. The formal noncompliance determination did not arrive until August 26, five full months after the investigation began, while minivans built as early as June 2025 kept carrying families with potentially dead stability control.

In fairness, the defense deserves its full hearing. Only about 200 of the 2,017 minivans are estimated to actually carry the defect, no crashes or injuries have been reported, and the dashboard light does illuminate, so this is not a fully silent failure. Drivers also operated minivans safely for decades before stability control became mandatory for the 2012 model year. Chrysler investigated, verified the scope, and recalled the vehicles, which is the system working as designed. And the doubled-risk figure is IIHS fleet data extrapolated onto 200 specific minivans, not a measured body count.

What to do is straightforward. If you own a 2025 or 2026 Pacifica or Voyager built between June 28 and October 31, 2025, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, where affected vehicles have been searchable since September 9.[1] Owner letters mail September 29, and dealers will inspect and replace the sensor free of charge. More generally, treat an illuminated stability-control light as what it is: notice that the single most effective safety system in your car may have left the building. Call Chrysler at 1-800-853-1403 or get to a dealer, and until then drive like it is 1995.

Engineers call it a single point of failure, and they use the term as an insult. A federally mandated lifesaving system should never depend on one uncracked capacitor. In 2,017 minivans, it did.
