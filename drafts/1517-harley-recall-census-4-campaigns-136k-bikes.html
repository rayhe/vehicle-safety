By The Numbers

Harley Filed Four Recalls in 2026. One Blocked Breather Hole Was Two-Thirds of Them.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 15, 2026

Four times in 2026, Harley-Davidson walked into NHTSA with a new defect. Add up the four campaign populations and you get 136,077 motorcycles.[4] Now read that number again, because it is doing something sneaky: one recall is doing almost all the work. A blocked breather hole in an airbox baseplate, filed April 28, accounts for 88,039 of those bikes. Nearly two-thirds of Harley’s entire recall year came from a hole that was supposed to let air through and did not.

136,077
Campaign-vehicles across Harley-Davidson’s four 2026 NHTSA recalls. Populations overlap, so this counts recall actions, not unique motorcycles.

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again. They didn’t get better, but they did get clearer. February brought recall 0191, campaign 26V075: 9,401 Sportster S bikes from model years 2021 through 2026, with an upper triple clamp that may break under load and interfere with steering.[4] April brought two more in fifteen days. Recall 0192, campaign 26V234: 16,994 Softails, Low Rider ST and S, Street Bob, Heritage Classic, model years 2025 and 2026, with a rear brake line routed where it rubs against the body control module until it leaks brake fluid.[4] Recall 0193, campaign 26V270: 88,039 bikes in the US, Road Glide and Street Glide and Fat Boy and Breakout, model years 2024 through 2026, with a breather port in the airbox baseplate blocked by plastic material from a supplier manufacturing issue, so hot oil can escape when the dipstick is pulled.[5] November brought the last one, recall 0194, campaign 26V730: 21,643 brand-new 2026 touring bikes whose gear position sensor can tell the engine control module the bike is in neutral when a gear is engaged, so the bike can roll forward on its own.[1]

Shares first, because shares are the story. Airbox: 64.7 percent of the year’s total; gear sensor: 15.9 percent; brake line: 12.5 percent; triple clamp: 6.9 percent. One recall is bigger than the other three combined, 88,039 against 48,038, and the gap is not close enough to argue about. If you want to understand Harley’s 2026 in one sentence, it is this: a Milwaukee factory shipped a part with a blocked hole, and that hole became the year.

Now the number that made me put the spreadsheet down and stare at the wall. Harley’s own Part 573 filing for the gear sensor recall estimates that 0.1 percent of the 21,643 bikes actually carry the defect.[1] Do the multiplication: 21,643 times 0.001 is about 22 motorcycles. Harley recalled 21,643 bikes to fix roughly 22 of them, a ratio of about a thousand to one, and that is the precautionary recall system working exactly as designed. Except two injuries are already potentially related to the defect, as of October 27.[3] If the estimate is right, that is roughly one injury per eleven actually defective bikes, an absurdly high hit rate for a fault that requires a rider to start the bike in gear with the clutch out. If the estimate is wrong, the defective population is bigger than Harley admits. Both readings are bad, so pick the one you like least.

Two corrections to the tally, because a census that does not correct itself is just a press release with arithmetic. First, one of the four “2026 recalls” is not new at all. Campaign 26V075 extends the 2025 triple-clamp recall, 25V590, into the 2026 model year, so the count of genuinely new 2026 campaigns is three, not four, and anyone counting filings without reading chronologies is overstating the year.[4] Second, the 136,077 is a count of campaign populations, and the same motorcycle can sit in two of them. Four model codes, the 2026 FLHX, FLTRX, FLTRXL and FLHXL, appear in both the airbox recall and the gear sensor recall.[5] A 2026 Street Glide owner who runs the VIN finds two open recalls for two unrelated systems in the same calendar year. That is the lived experience the tally hides, and it is worse than the tally sounds.

Step back and look at the four defects as a set, and a pattern shows up that no single recall reveals. Three of the four are small physical manufacturing flaws, not engineering concepts gone wrong: plastic molding flash on a sensor, plastic material blocking a breather port, a brake line routed where it chafes against a module. Nobody designed a bad motorcycle four times. Somebody’s supplier shipped a burr, somebody’s routing missed a clearance, and the bikes came back one system at a time. A regression line fit to four points is a joke, and I know it, but the slope here points at the factory floor, not the drawing board.

Fairness requires the other side of the ledger, stated at full strength. Four recalls is a normal year for a major manufacturer, and the defect rates are tiny: 0.1 percent on the headline recall, no deaths anywhere in the four filings, no park-it order, no do-not-ride advisory, and every remedy is a free fix at any dealer. Most of the 88,039 airbox bikes never saw a drop of hot oil escape, and the brake-line recall is a chafe inspection with a cheap repair. Recalls are the safety system working, not failing. Harley found these defects, filed them, and pays for the repairs, which is precisely what the law asks a manufacturer to do. A reader who walks away thinking Harley builds dangerous motorcycles has misread the data; the tally is big, the danger per bike is small, and the remedy is simple.

Still, two injuries from about twenty-two defective bikes is a number that does not sit quietly in a spreadsheet, and a touring line that goes back to the dealer twice in one year for two different defects is a pattern a buyer deserves to know about before signing. Both of those sentences are true at the same time. That is usually the case with the numbers, which is why I keep running them.

If you own a 2026 Street Glide, Road Glide, or any of the nine models in recall 0194, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls or call Harley at 1-800-258-2464 and reference recall 0194. Do not wait for the owner letter, which is scheduled to mail November 16 through 23.[1] Check recall 0193, the airbox campaign, at the same time; four model codes sit in both. Until the sensor is replaced, start the bike with the clutch pulled in and a brake covered, every time, regardless of what the dash says, because the green N is advisory, not fact. Buying a used 2026 Harley touring bike? Confirm recalls 0194 and 0193 were completed, and ask for the service record instead of taking the seller’s word. Sportster S owners from 2021 through 2026 should check recall 0191 as well; if you skipped the 2025 triple-clamp recall, you are in the 2026 extension. And every rider, on any bike, should remember the lesson this census keeps teaching: a neutral indicator is a convenience display, not a safety system. The clutch lever is the interlock.
