Congress Wants to Repeal DC's Helmet Law. Every State That Tried It Buried More Riders.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 12, 2026

Six motorcyclists have died in the nation's capital this year, triple last year's two, under one of the strictest helmet laws in the country. Two congressmen looked at that number and decided the problem was the law.[2] Their bill, the "Ride in Freedom Act," would prohibit the District from requiring helmets for anyone over 18. The city currently requires them for everyone, no exceptions, like 17 other states.[5]

The sponsors are not coy about the playbook. Representative Derrick Van Orden of Wisconsin, a Harley rider who co-chairs the Congressional Motorcycle Caucus, and Senator Tim Sheehy of Montana introduced the Ride in Freedom Act in both chambers in early August, as bill 10067 in the House and 5274 in the Senate. Van Orden's spokeswoman, Grace Kim, told reporters the District was "an important place to start," adding that it "opens the door to having a larger conversation about the federal government's role in helmet mandates."[3] The capital is the beachhead, and the country is the objective.

Van Orden put it more simply: "It's nothing kooky. You should have the choice to wear a helmet or not."[4] Fine. But choices have body counts, and this one has already been counted, state by state, in the great American natural experiment nobody asked to run. Florida repealed its universal helmet law and its motorcyclist fatality rate rose 35 percent; Kentucky's rose 50 percent; Louisiana's doubled.[6] Nebraska's repeal took effect in 2024; preliminary numbers show fatalities up 32 percent against the five-year average. Missouri repealed in 2020 and got a 47 percent rise, culminating in its deadliest year on record, 174 dead riders in 2023.[6] Every state that bought freedom got a funeral bill.

Here is the part that should embarrass the sponsors. The states that ran the experiment are now legislating to undo it. In February, Missouri lawmakers introduced SB 990 to restore the all-rider helmet law their predecessors repealed.[6] In January, Hawaii introduced SB2226 to require helmets for all riders, after 27 motorcycle deaths in 2025 with 61 percent of the dead unhelmeted.[7] The data is moving toward helmets while the politics moves away. IIHS statistical director Eric Teoh put it plainly: universal helmet laws are "one of the most effective, common-sense rules of the road," and "weakening that will result in people dying."[3]

Nobody claims helmets prevent crashes. They prevent the part of the crash that kills the thinking organ, which is a distinction the American Motorcyclist Association would rather you forget when it calls mandates paternalism and argues that "helmet use alone is insufficient to ensure a motorcyclist's safety."[5] True enough, and helmets are also insufficient against lightning. Per mile traveled, motorcyclists are about 27 times more likely to die than car occupants, and five times more likely to be injured.[5] NHTSA's own advice: "If you're ever in a serious motorcycle crash, the best hope you have for protecting your brain is a motorcycle helmet." When 6,228 riders died nationally in 2024, the deadliest cohort on the road per mile, the best hope is not a footnote.[1]

Maryland and Virginia both require helmets, which means the bill would create a jurisdictional donut: helmet on, helmet off, helmet on, all within a single commute across the Potomac.[5] If you ride in the District, wear the helmet regardless of what Congress does; the morgue statistics do not grade on a curve for federalism. And if your state representative is one of the riders'-rights types pushing the rollback, ask them which experiment they plan to run differently than Florida, Kentucky, Louisiana, Nebraska, and Missouri, and to show their work. The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk.
