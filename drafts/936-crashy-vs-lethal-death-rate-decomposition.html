Your Car's Death Rate Is Two Numbers. They Barely Know Each Other.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 18, 2026

Every fatality rate on this site is a lie of compression. Take any number we have published, the Mustang's 6.02 deaths per hundred million miles or the Silverado's 1.25, and understand that each one is actually two completely different dangers multiplied together and sold as a single figure[1]. I separated them across 183 nameplates. Their correlation is 0.306, which is the statistical equivalent of two strangers nodding at each other in an elevator.

0.306
Correlation between how often a nameplate is in fatal crashes and how often its occupants die in them

Call the first half crashiness: fatal-crash involvements per 1,000 registered vehicles, 2014 through 2023. Call the second half lethality: your own occupants' deaths per fatal crash, the metric Vin Wreckage computed in our September 14 piece[1]. Multiply them and you get something close to the familiar death rate. But here is the part that should bother you: knowing a car is crash-prone tells you almost nothing about whether its occupants die. Rank every nameplate by the first number and the second number barely moves, which means we have been grading single numbers that hide two independent stories about how vehicles kill.

Nissan's Maxima leads the crashiness board at 8.88 fatal-crash involvements per 1,000 registered vehicles, roughly seven times the median nameplate, with 2,331 fatal crashes in a decade[1]. Right behind it sit the discontinued Chevrolet Impala (8.52) and Cobalt (7.26), a trio of cheap used sedans whose fleets are old, shrinking, and driven by people with the least margin for error. Then comes the surprise: the Ford E-350, a commercial work van, at 7.21. The E-350 has been in 1,892 fatal crashes. Only 776 of its own occupants died. It survives its own worst days four times out of five, at someone else's expense, which is exactly the mass transfer physics IIHS has documented for decades[3].

Sports cars are the class outlier on crashiness with a median of 2.96 fatal-crash involvements per 1,000 vehicles, roughly one and a half times sedans and three times SUVs, and they are lethal besides (0.661 median lethality, the highest of any class). Sedans tell the inverse story: middling crashiness (1.99) but near the top on lethality (0.644), meaning sedans get into fewer fatal crashes than pickups yet bury their occupants more often per crash. SUVs are the dull valedictorians, lowest on both halves (0.91 and 0.489). The Ford F-150, for scale, has been involved in 20,066 fatal crashes in ten years. Twenty thousand. Its occupants died in fewer than half of them.

Then there is the inversion tier, and it is worth your attention if you drive one: the Nissan Kicks is in fatal crashes at barely above the median rate (1.17 per 1,000) but its occupants die 74% of the time; the Honda Fit (1.15, 72%) is the same story. Rarely in the wrong place, nearly unforgiving when it happens. This is the decomposition doing its one useful trick: a single rate would rank the Fit as a 0.72 and call it safe, while the split shows a car that seldom crashes and punishes hard.

Our original contribution is the joint finding itself: nobody has published these two factors side by side with their correlation, and the correlation being weak is the point. #904 established lethality; this piece adds crashiness and shows the pair decouple, which changes how you read every rate on this site. Insurance actuaries already think this way, pricing collision frequency and injury severity as separate books. Consumers never get to see the split.

Limitations, stated plainly because the numbers invite overconfidence: FARS records fatal crashes only, so crashiness here measures fatal-crash involvement per registered vehicle, not all crashes; a nameplate that fender-benders constantly is invisible to this metric. Fleet estimates come from sales-based registrations with roughly 15% uncertainty for low-volume models, and discontinued nameplates like the Cobalt and Impala have shrinking fleets that inflate their crashiness, which is part old-car effect and part denominator artifact. Lethality counts only that nameplate's occupant deaths divided by fatal crashes involving it, including crashes where the other party died, so low lethality partly means being the surviving party rather than being crash-proof. The 0.306 correlation holds across the 183 nameplates with fleets above 250,000; the direction is robust to the cutoff.

The strongest case against this piece is that it is one multiplication of what we already publish: fatality rate equals crashiness times lethality, so the decomposition adds no information the rate didn't contain, and the weak correlation is exactly what you would expect when VMT, driver age, geography, and vehicle age load onto the two factors differently. Strip out the fleet-estimate noise and the headline names become familiar: cheap used sedans driven hard, sports cars behaving like sports cars, heavy trucks surviving at others' expense. IIHS said the physics part twenty years ago. Fair. What is new is the per-nameplate quantification of the split, and the demonstration that a single rate is hiding two stories that refuse to move together.

What to do with this. Shopping used, ask two questions instead of one: how often does this nameplate get into fatal crashes, and how survivable are they? The F-150 scores badly on the first and well on the second, and you want both answers low. Sports car buyers get a number for what their insurance already told them: 2 to 4 times the crash involvement per vehicle of any other class is a behavioral tax, and it is priced accordingly. If you drive a heavy truck or full-size SUV, your good numbers are partly other people's bodies, so drive like your survival is borrowed. And whatever you drive, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because neither half of the death rate survives a defective airbag or a recalled brake booster[5].
