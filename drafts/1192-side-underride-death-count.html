NHTSA Can't Count the Bodies. It Did the $1.2 Billion Math Anyway.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. In 1969, the U.S. Department of Transportation announced that after technical studies were complete, it would extend underride protection to the sides of large trucks.[1] Studies finished and guards got built; one design even passed IIHS crash testing. Fifty-seven years later, the sides of American trailers are guarded exactly as well as they were the year humans landed on the moon: they aren't.

Congress noticed. In early February 2026, lawmakers introduced the Stop Underrides Act 2.0, S. 3775 in the Senate and H.R. 7354 in the House, directing the Transportation Secretary to finalize a side-guard rule for new trailers, semitrailers, and single-unit trucks within 18 months of enactment.[2][3] Each guard would have to stop a passenger car striking at up to 40 miles per hour. Buried in the bill sits the provision that matters most: an order for NHTSA to go back through its Fatality Analysis Reporting System and correct every underride crash it misclassified, including crashes involving pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcyclists.[4] Read that again: Congress is ordering the federal government to recount the dead, because the current count is wrong.

17.2
lives per year NHTSA says side guards would save, at an annual cost of up to $1.2 billion

All that math comes from NHTSA's own April 2023 analysis. Agency analysts counted 89 light-vehicle occupant deaths and 409 serious injuries per year in crashes where a car strikes the side of a tractor-trailer and slides underneath.[5] Apply a 97 percent effectiveness rate for impacts at or below 40 mph, and the projection becomes 17.2 lives and 69 serious injuries saved annually, once the fleet is fully equipped.[6] Total annual cost: between $970 million and $1.2 billion, covering hardware, installation, and the lifetime fuel penalty of bolting 450 to 800 extra pounds onto every trailer.[6] That works out to $73.5 million to $103.7 million per equivalent life saved. NHTSA values a statistical life at $11.9 million. Six to nine times the value of a life, by the agency's own arithmetic.

Do one more division the agency didn't highlight. If 89 deaths at 97 percent effectiveness yields only 17.2 lives saved, then roughly one in five side-underride deaths happens at impact speeds the guards are even designed for. Four out of five victims die too fast for steel bars to matter. Meanwhile the agency has moved its own deadline for finishing the comment analysis three times: December 2024, then January 2026, then July 2026. It still sits at the "prerule stage," which is regulatory Latin for we'll get to it.[7]

57
years since DOT promised side underride guards, pending "technical studies"

Now the part where the universe winks at you. Every number in that cost-benefit analysis traces back to a body count that the government's own auditors say is fiction. A 2019 GAO review found FARS recorded between 189 and 253 underride deaths a year from 2008 through 2017, then spent pages explaining why the real number is higher: no uniform national collection, police forms with no underride field, officers documenting the same crash five different ways.[8] IIHS told the GAO it doesn't even use FARS for underride work. It uses general rear-impact data as a proxy, because FARS misses too many.[8] An independent researcher who filed comments on the rulemaking found something worse: FARS coding rules require the undercount. When the car's report codes an underride, the manual forbids the truck's report from coding one too, so the truck file must read "No Underride or Override Noted."[9] The database is programmed to lose bodies.

So let's do the math NHTSA can't, or won't. Suppose the true side-underride death toll is double the agency's 89, a modest correction given the GAO's warnings. Lives saved double to about 34 a year. Cost per life halves to roughly $37 million to $52 million. Still three to four times the value of a statistical life. Triple it: 267 dead, 52 saved, $25 million to $35 million per life. Still double or triple the bar. Even if you fix the count generously, the economics don't clear. Which means the honest case for this bill was never economic. It's the other number in the bill's findings: 31,500 dead since NHTSA's founding, with "no substantial progress."[3] You cannot cost-benefit your way out of that sentence. You can only decide whether 31,500 is a number you're comfortable with.

Nobody makes the case against this bill better than the people who'd pay for it. OOIDA, the Owner-Operator Independent Drivers Association, calls a side-guard mandate one of the costliest trucking rules in history: up to $1.2 billion a year, hundreds of millions in extra fuel, less freight per trailer, and steel bars hanging low enough to ground out on low-clearance railroad crossings.[10] Then there's the uncomfortable one. Plenty of side underrides start with a car drifting into the trailer, not the other way around, and one trade publication has called the bill a billion-dollar fix for a distraction problem.[6] Driver monitoring and lane-departure systems might save more lives per dollar than steel. Trailer manufacturers say they'll support guards once they're "justified and technologically feasible."[11] Nobody will tell you what "justified" means in units of dead people.

What this doesn't prove. There is no current verified national underride count, which is the scandal and also the caveat, because advocates can't prove the higher number either. That coding-rule study compared 16 FARS case listings against news reports: damning, but small and non-random. That 31,500 figure is the bill's finding, not an independent audit. The 97 percent effectiveness number describes impacts at or below 40 mph under test conditions; nobody knows the real-world figure because the guards barely exist in the wild. And my doubling-and-tripling exercise is arithmetic, not measurement. It shows the economics fail even on friendly assumptions, nothing more.

What to do with this. If you drive: the side gap is unguarded by federal law, so treat the space beside a trailer as somewhere you pass through, not somewhere you live. Pass decisively or fall back, and don't cruise in the blind zone. Side underrides happen during lane changes, turns, and drifts, the exact moments you're alongside. If you run a fleet: aftermarket side guards exist, one design is IIHS crash-tested, and they run about $2,500 a trailer.[8] If you care about the policy: the recount provision is the sleeper cell in this bill. Every future cost-benefit analysis will run on whatever FARS says after the correction. Docket NHTSA-2023-0012 on regulations.gov is where the missed deadlines go to be noticed. Fifty-seven years is a long time to wait for steel bars. Bodies, in the meantime, keep not being counted.
