## Angle (1-2 sentences)
The United States has spent more than two decades setting heavy-truck safety policy from a crash-causation study whose data was collected in 2001-2003. On November 3, 2026, FMCSA finally put money behind the replacement: up to $7 million for state and local agencies to collect detailed data on about 2,000 fatal heavy-truck crashes, with a final report and public database due in 2030.

## Verified facts
- **The announcement:** November 3, 2026. FMCSA offers up to $7 million to eligible state and local agencies to gather and report detailed crash information through CCFP. Awards capped at $500,000 each, about 30 awards. One-time offering. Applications due December 28, 2026. Grants.gov opportunity FM-CCFP-27-001.
- **The sample:** approximately 2,000 fatal crashes involving Class 7 and Class 8 trucks (GVWR 26,001 pounds or more) in about 30 states over two years. Full collection starts in 2027 after a pilot. The 2025 plan targeted 3,333 crashes to net 2,000 usable cases.
- **The timeline:** data collection and analysis through 2029, final report plus a publicly available anonymized database in 2030, per the November 2026 announcement. (An earlier 2025 plan said 2029 for the report; the newer announcement pushes the public database to 2030. Article uses the November 2026 timeline.)
- **The predecessor:** LTCCS fieldwork 2001-2003, 24 sites, 17 states, 967 crashes, up to 1,000 data elements per crash, report to Congress March 2006. Its headline finding: driver action or inaction was the critical reason in 88% of crashes.
- **The trend the study must explain:** fatal crashes involving large trucks and buses rose 26.4% from 2016 to 2022 (FMCSA, via Transport Topics).
- **The money, kept separate:** the $30 million figure in 2025 coverage is the overall study cost cited for the congressionally commissioned effort; the $7 million is the November 2026 CCFP funding slice for state and local collection costs. They are not the same pot, and the article must not add them together.
- **Who runs it:** FMCSA with the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (Office of Safety Data and Analysis leading research), plus state and local enforcement agencies, motor carriers, drivers, and other crash parties. BTS planned five hour-long interviews per crash.

## Original contribution (this article's novel findings)
1. **The gap, in years:** LTCCS fieldwork ended in 2003. The new study's fieldwork starts in 2027. That is a 24-year gap between the last comparable fieldwork and the next, and a 27-year gap between the old data and the new report in 2030. Every truck-safety rule written in between leaned, directly or not, on data collected before the iPhone existed.
2. **The scale-up, honestly stated:** 2,000 crashes is about double the LTCCS sample of 967, but it is still a sample, not a census. FMCSA estimated about 141,000 large trucks involved in fatal and injury crashes during the LTCCS period alone. The study's value is depth (up to 1,000 elements per crash in the old study; interviews per crash in the new one), not coverage.
3. **The 70% framing:** Class 7 and 8 trucks are roughly 70% of large trucks in fatal crashes, so the study's scope covers the bulk of the fatal-truck problem while excluding lighter commercial vehicles. That is a defensible scope choice, and the article should say so rather than treating the exclusion as a flaw.
4. **The deadline risk:** applications close December 28, 2026, and the funding is one-time. If too few of the 30 target states apply, or if state budgets cannot absorb the unfunded remainder, the nationally representative sample is the first thing that shrinks. The article names this as the thing to watch, not as a prediction.

## Strongest counterargument (at full strength)
The last study cost $18 million and five years and mostly confirmed what everyone already believed: drivers cause most crashes. Safety advocates called it underwhelming at the time, and a bigger sample does not guarantee a more surprising answer. The new study also arrives late by design: fieldwork in 2027-2028, answers in 2030, for a problem (fatal truck crashes up 26.4% in six years) that is getting worse now. And 2,000 crashes, however deep the interviews, cannot settle the questions regulators most want answered, like the real-world effect of any single technology, because the sample is too small to slice that finely. The article must carry this reading: the study is necessary infrastructure, not a rescue, and its payoff is a decade of better rules, not a headline in 2030.

## Limitations
- The November 2026 announcement's $7 million / 30-award / Dec 28 deadline figures come from trade-press reporting of the FMCSA announcement (The Trucker); the underlying Grants.gov listing (FM-CCFP-27-001) was not independently opened for this draft.
- The 2030 date for the final report and database comes from the November 2026 announcement as reported; a 2025 plan cited 2029. The discrepancy is flagged in the research and the article uses the newer date.
- No state-by-state list of the ~30 sampling states was verified; the article does not name participating states.
- LTCCS cost figures differ by source ($18 million over five years per Fleet Owner; $3M FY2000 + $5M/year FY2001-2003 authorized per the FMCSA report). The article uses the $18 million figure with the five-year framing, attributed to contemporary coverage.
