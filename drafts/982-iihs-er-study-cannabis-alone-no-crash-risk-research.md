# #982 Research: The IIHS-Paid Study That Says Weed Doesn't Crash Cars

**Journalist:** Dale Impactor III (toxicology/impairment beat)
**Kicker:** Sobriety Report
**Date:** 2026-09-24

## Angle
A nine-author team at OHSU, University of Colorado, UC Davis, Portland State, and the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety — funded by IIHS itself — drew blood and breathalyzed drivers at emergency departments in Denver, Portland, and Sacramento, then ran a case-crossover analysis comparing each driver's crash day against their own non-crash days. The result IIHS paid to find: **cannabis alone was not associated with higher crash odds** (OR ~0.45-0.47, confidence intervals crossing 1.0), while **alcohol alone raised crash odds ~4.6-5.2x** and **combined alcohol+cannabis raised odds ~2.6-3.3x**. The nine authors concluded THC levels are "a less-than-reliable indicator of driving risk" and that per se THC DUI limits lack scientific grounding. IIHS's other finding — that legalization raised injury crash rates 5.8% and fatal crash rates 4.1% (Farmer, JSAD Sept 2026, already covered in draft #840) — stands next to this one like two witnesses telling different stories. The reconciliation: legalization doesn't make cannabis more dangerous; it makes the combination more common, especially among infrequent cannabis users (combined-use OR 8.11 for infrequent users, Table A4).

## Kill test
Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. The funder (IIHS) is the industry body that has published the strongest evidence that cannabis legalization raises crashes — and it funded a peer-reviewed study that found no individual-level crash risk from cannabis alone. That institutional irony plus the per se-limit verdict is a genuine paradox with a novel mechanism. ## Key numbers (with arithmetic)
- Model 1 (closed vehicle), odds ratios vs sober baseline: cannabis only 0.47 (95% CI 0.21-1.03, p=0.060, not significant); alcohol only 4.57 (2.61-8.02, p<0.001); both 3.30 (1.23-8.82, p=0.017). Model 2: cannabis only 0.45 (0.20-1.01); alcohol only 5.15 (2.88-9.21); both 2.58 (0.91-7.34).
- Infrequent cannabis users (< weekly) who combined with alcohol: OR 8.11 (1.64-40.02, p=0.010) — the combo is 8x baseline for the casual user.
- "High self-reported acute cannabis use was associated with lower odds of MVC" — the heavier the reported use, the safer the odds, exactly backwards from dose-response intuition.
- Prior study cited by authors: daily cannabis consumers had 5x the blood THC of occasional users at 30 minutes, yet only occasional users showed driving-skill decrement in a simulator — tolerance decouples blood level from impairment.
- Congressional Research Service 2019: THC nanogram per se limits lack evidentiary basis; 2019 study: drivers at the legal THC limit (2-5 ng/mL) were statistically no more likely to crash than sober drivers.
- 10,832 alcohol-impaired deaths in 2024 = ~33 per day; ~30% of all U.S. traffic deaths.

## Original contribution
The funder-paradox framing: IIHS funded both the ecological study linking legalization to more crashes AND the individual-level study exonerating cannabis alone — both can be true only if the mechanism is combined use. Original arithmetic bridging the two: if legalization states see +4-6% crash rates but cannabis-only users have OR ~0.5, the entire ecological effect must ride on some other channel, and the paper's own Table A4 hands it over (infrequent-user combo OR 8.11). Second original point: the enforcement geometry — no roadside THC test can timestamp or quantify impairment, and the authors explicitly say strict cutoffs are scientifically indefensible; meanwhile House THUD report language still demands an "objective standard" that the science says doesn't exist. Third: the child-passenger datapoint (14%) recasts the risk from driver skill to third-party exposure — the people most hurt by cannabis-involved crashes are passengers who never chose.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
The study only enrolled drivers who agreed to participate — drivers who crashed while at fault, especially combined users facing DUI charges, have every reason to decline, which would bias the sample toward milder users and toward a weaker association. Self-reported cannabis use is also biased toward understatement for the same reason. The case-crossover design compares a driver's crash day to their own non-crash days, which controls for person-level confounds but not for day-level ones — a driver who drinks on Friday nights is being compared to their own sober Tuesdays, so "alcohol raises crash risk" partly restates "Friday nights are dangerous." The sample is three ERs in three legal states (Denver, Portland, Sacramento) — heavily urban, heavily cannabis-tolerant culture — and says nothing about states where use patterns differ. Blood THC cannot timestamp use, so "cannabis only" buckets contain people who smoked yesterday. And the paper's acute-illicit-drug OR (108.65, CI 14.55-811) shows the sample is thin where it matters most.

## Limitations (dedicated accounting)
- Observational case-crossover, not randomized; association, not causation.
- Consent-based ER enrollment → selection bias; at-fault impaired drivers underrepresented.
- Three ERs, three cannabis-legal metros → limited generalizability; no rural or prohibition-state data.
- Self-reported use likely understated; blood THC can't establish recency, so cannabis-only group includes stale users.
- NHTSA's trauma-center study (54% positive, 20% polydrug) measures presence in crash victims, not crash causation — high prevalence of a substance among crash victims does not prove it caused crashes.
- The 8.11 combined-use OR for infrequent users has a wide CI (1.64-40.02) — small-n estimate, treat as directionally strong, numerically soft.

## Actionable takeaways
- If you use cannabis and drink, the combination is the trap — an 8x crash-odds multiplier for infrequent cannabis users, per this study. This is the one impairment interaction that survives every statistical adjustment.
- No reliable roadside cannabis test exists, and the legal THC nanogram limits have no scientific basis per the authors — do not calibrate your safety to "under the legal limit" for weed; that number measures chemistry, not impairment.
- For parents and passengers: 14% of confirmed cannabis-involved crash drivers had a child in the car. The exposure isn't only yours.
- What to do: treat impairment like the study's authors do — judge by driving behavior and clinical signs, not blood chemistry. If you're behind the wheel and combining, you're the data point.

## Enforcement angle
House Appropriations THUD report language still demands an "objective standard to measure marijuana impairment and a related field sobriety test." This study — funded by the insurer-backed institute — says no such standard is scientifically available. The policy gap is structural: the law wants a breathalyzer for weed; the chemistry won't provide one.
