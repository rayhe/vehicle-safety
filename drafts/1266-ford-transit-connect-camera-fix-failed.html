Ford's Backup Camera Fix Was the Wrong Part. The Real Fix Is 14 Months Away.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
December 30, 2027

Ford recalled 506 delivery vans in October 2026 because a previous recall repair installed the wrong part. Every van in the new campaign carries the defect, Ford's filing says, because each one got the incorrect replacement camera under the earlier fix. Ford calls the campaign 26S73. Owners waiting for the corrected part will wait until December 2027.[1]

Start with what the driver sees. Affected cameras can show a distorted image, an inverted image, an intermittent image, or no image at all when the van is in reverse. A delivery van reverses dozens of times a day, in alleys and loading zones where children and low posts hide below the mirror line. An inverted image is worse than a blank one. A blank screen tells you it cannot help. An inverted screen looks usable while lying about direction.[1]

Ford found the problem by reading warranty claims. Its team reviewed claims on vans repaired under the earlier camera campaign, 25S89, and identified 506 that had received the incorrect replacement part. Ford has not said what was wrong with the part. Only that it was wrong. A wrong part that fits well enough to leave the dealer lot is a supply-chain failure wearing a service-bay uniform.[1]

Scale is where this story inverts. Ford's September 2025 camera expansion covered roughly 1.9 million vehicles, with more than 44,000 warranty claims and 18 accidents reported. Against that backdrop, 506 vans look like a rounding error. That is exactly why they matter: the smallest campaign in the family proves a bad remedy can ship, get installed, and sit in driveways until a later claims review catches it. Most recalls estimate a small fraction actually defective; Ford's largest 2026 campaign, 4,380,609 vehicles with a trailer-brake software glitch, carried a 1% estimate. This one carries 100%, because the population was defined by the mistake itself. If your van got the wrong part, your van is defective. Full stop.[2]

Then comes the wait. Interim owner letters went out in October 2026. The remedy itself is anticipated in December 2027. That is roughly 14 months of reversing a work van on a screen Ford has admitted cannot be trusted, for owners who did everything right: they answered the first recall, drove to the dealer, and accepted the repair. Their reward is a second recall letter and another year of checking the mirrors twice.[3]

Fairness demands the other side of this ledger, stated plainly. No accidents or injuries are tied to this 506-van group. A backup camera supplements mirrors and a turned head, and an intermittent image annoys before it endangers. December 2027 is a planning estimate, not a promise carved in stone, and parts could arrive sooner. A proportionate reader could call this a small paperwork-grade correction rather than a scandal: 506 vans, zero reported harm, free repair.[2]

What this piece did not prove: Ford's filing never says what made the replacement part incorrect, and no symptom count for the 506 has been published, so 100% describes vans carrying the wrong part, not vans already showing a bad image. Transit Connect FARS figures span all model years, untied to this defect. And the 14-month window is arithmetic on Ford's own schedule, not a guarantee it holds.

What to actually do with this

If you own a 2015 Transit Connect that had its backup camera replaced under the 2025 recall, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now: you may be one of the 506 and due a second, free camera replacement. Until the corrected camera is installed, treat the screen as untrustworthy in reverse and use mirrors plus a physical look behind the van every time, because an inverted image misleads where a blank one merely disappoints. Fleet operators should check fleet VINs in bulk, since delivery vans reverse more than any other vehicle class in daily service.
