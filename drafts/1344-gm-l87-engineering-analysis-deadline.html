NHTSA Asked GM What Warning You Get Before Your Engine Dies. The Deadline Was Yesterday.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 31, 2026

NHTSA sent General Motors six questions about the 6.2-liter L87 V8, and the sixth one should keep you up at night: explain what warning a driver gets before the engine destroys itself. The deadline for the answers was October 30. Yesterday.[1]

26
Engines that failed AFTER a complete replacement, out of 499 post-repair failures

This is chapter three of a saga The Crash Report has been following. Recall 25V-274 covered 597,571 trucks and SUVs in April 2025 for defective connecting rods and crankshafts, and the remedy was dealers inspecting each engine, with the ones that passed getting thicker 0W-40 oil, a new filter, and a new oil cap. A lubricant fix for a metallurgy problem, in other words. Failures kept arriving from engines that had already been fixed, so NHTSA opened recall query RQ26001 in January to ask the obvious question, then closed it and escalated to Engineering Analysis EA26005 on August 20, covering 997,743 vehicles from the 2021 through 2026 model years. That is the most serious level of defect investigation the agency conducts, and the last stage before it can formally press for a recall.[2]

Read the complaint math carefully, because it is all complaint math. Owners filed 499 reports of engines failing after the recall remedy, 473 after the oil change and 26 after a complete engine replacement, which means the remedy's maximum intervention failed 5.2 percent of the time among reported cases. GM itself reported 6,953 post-remedy complaints, about 1.16 percent of the recall population, for whatever a self-reported fraction is worth. Another 191 failure reports involve engines built after May 31, 2024, outside the recall window, which suggests the production dates were drawn too narrowly, and the investigation population is 400,172 vehicles larger than the recall, a 67 percent expansion, which makes these tallies, not a failure rate. A pattern this consistent does not need a denominator to make you uncomfortable.[1]

In fairness to GM, and it pains me to write it: complaints are self-selected by angry owners, and 6,953 angry voices are not 6,953 proven defects. Thicker oil is a legitimate engineering mitigation for marginal tolerances, not a sham, it just does not change the metal. The 997,743 figure is an investigation population, not a failure count, and NHTSA has attributed no defect, no crash, and no injury in the EA26005 opening summary. GM says manufacturing improvements by June 1, 2024 addressed the cause. All real, all on the record.[3]

But GM has also asked a federal judge to dismiss a related lawsuit, arguing the recall already provides the remedy the plaintiffs seek. That argument depends entirely on the remedy working, which is the precise question NHTSA just escalated to its most serious investigation level. Either the fix fixed it, in which case 26 engines should not have died after full replacement, or it did not, and the dismissal motion is wishful thinking. One replacement engine seized after 3,000 miles: a new engine, three thousand miles, gone.[2]

What this article did not prove: whether the post-repair failures share one root cause, whether 2025-2026 engines fail at the same rate, or what GM actually submitted by the deadline. An Engineering Analysis can end with a recall, a narrower action, or nothing at all. What is not uncertain is your to-do list. If you own a 2021-2026 Silverado, Sierra, Tahoe, Suburban, Yukon, or Escalade with the 6.2L, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and check for campaign 25V274. If the recall is incomplete, complete it, because the inspection path can still catch an engine that needs replacing. If yours was already fixed, listen for knocking or metallic noise and watch the oil pressure gauge, then file an NHTSA complaint if it fails, because 499 complaints are what built this investigation. And if you are shopping a used 2025 or 2026, understand it sits inside the investigation but outside the recall. The federal file is open, and the sixth question is still waiting for its answer.
