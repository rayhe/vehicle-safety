Nobody Died in a 2022 or 2023 Toyota Tundra. The Same Cannot Be Said for the F-150.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

Before you sign that lease on America's best-selling truck, you might want to see this. A study of the 50 best-selling vehicles in America, published November 2 and built on IIHS driver-death data, handed the large-pickup crown to a truck that sells about one-sixth as many units as the F-150. Zero driver deaths were recorded in the 2022 and 2023 Toyota Tundra. Not the lowest in its class. Zero, against a large-pickup average of 29 driver deaths per million registered vehicle years.[1] It is one of only six models in the entire IIHS dataset with a clean record.

0
Driver deaths per million registered vehicle years, 2022–2023 Tundra CrewMax 4WD (large-pickup average: 29)

Our own pull of NHTSA fatality data tells the same story from a second angle, and the second angle is the one that should make Ford and Ram uncomfortable. Counting every death in crashes involving model-year 2022 and 2023 vehicles, the Tundra recorded zero. In the identical window, the F-150 recorded 30, the Ram 1500 recorded 39, the Silverado 23, and the Tacoma 17.[2] Two different metrics, two different datasets, one direction. IIHS counts driver deaths only; FARS counts everyone killed in the crash. Both say nobody died in a new Tundra.

Run the arithmetic on what zero means, because a zero over two model years is not a small-sample shrug. Toyota sold about 229,000 Tundras across the 2022 and 2023 calendar years.[5] At the Tundra nameplate's own 2014–2023 average death rate, roughly 40 deaths would have been expected from that much driving. Zero showed up instead. That estimate is rough, model-year and calendar-year sales do not line up exactly, but the peer comparison needs no sales data at all: same window, same metric, same vehicle class, and the Tundra is the only one with nothing in the column.

Credit belongs to a specific truck, not a badge. The Tundra nameplate is not historically safe: 1,223 deaths across 2014–2023, a rate of 0.94 per 100 million miles, mid-pack among pickups.[2] Deaths by model year fell steadily as the old generation aged out, 43 for model year 2014 down to 8 for 2021, and hit zero the year the redesigned third generation arrived in 2022. Lab tests back up the real-world record. The 2026 Tundra crew cab earned an IIHS Top Safety Pick with Good ratings in small overlap front, moderate overlap front, and side tests, plus a five-star overall rating from NHTSA.[3][4] It is one of just two full-size pickups to earn a 2026 IIHS award, and a model-by-model comparison notes the 2026 F-150 earned none.[6]

Fairness demands the limits, stated straight. Zero covers two model years with at most two years of exposure in the FARS file, which ends in 2023. A single fatal crash involving a 2022 Tundra in 2024 data would end the zero, so read it as zero so far, not zero forever. And the study behind the headline number comes from a personal-injury law firm, not a peer-reviewed journal; its underlying driver-death figures are IIHS data, and our independent FARS pull confirms the direction, but the law-firm framing is not the evidence. One more caveat for shoppers: the IIHS zero applies to the CrewMax 4WD specifically, and NHTSA never assessed the Double Cab. Buy the crew cab.

What you can actually do with this is simple. Shopping for a full-size pickup: a 2022-or-newer Tundra is the only truck in the segment with a zero in real-world death data and a current IIHS award at the same time, starting around $41,260.[1] Already own one: nothing to do, and that is the point. Own a 2021-or-older Tundra: understand the generational break, because the old truck's record, 43 deaths for model year 2014 alone, is not the new truck's record. And if you are cross-shopping the F-150, ask the dealer one question before you sign: how many people died in the 2022 and 2023 models. You already know the answer. It is 30.
