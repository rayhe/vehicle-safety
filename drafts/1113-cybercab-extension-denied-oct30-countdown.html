Tesla Asked for Eight Weeks to Explain the Cybercab. NHTSA Gave It Thirty Days.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 9, 2026

Twenty-one days from now, somebody at Tesla has to swear under oath that a car with no pedals complies with a federal rule requiring a foot-operated brake. The answers are due October 30. Tesla asked for until November 20 and got half that.

30 of 60
Days NHTSA granted of the 60 Tesla requested to answer 21 sworn certification questions

The paper trail reads like a manila folder in a noir film, except every date is real. September 3: NHTSA opens Audit Query AQ26002 the afternoon Tesla starts charging for Cybercab rides in Austin, roughly 1,000 vehicles in scope.[1] September 10: Chief Counsel Peter Simshauser signs a Special Order, 21 requests, sworn affidavit from a responsible officer.[2] September 18: Tesla asks for eight more weeks, citing "the voluminous nature of the request."[3] September 22: NHTSA grants 30 days. September 29: a memo to file makes the exchange public.

"Review material for responsiveness and privilege" means lawyers are deciding what NHTSA gets to see, which is normal and also the sentence you read twice.

All 21 questions go straight at the driverless design: every standard Tesla certified to, which ones it decided do not apply, and whether temporary human controls were bolted on for certification testing and removed afterward, plus mirrors, self-canceling turn signals, and touchscreen driving. Then the brake question, the sharpest in the file: FMVSS 135 says the service brake "shall be activated by means of a foot control," and the order restates the agency's view that a vehicle without one "could not certify."[4] Tesla must now explain how its pedal-less car complies anyway, plus why it never sought a Part 555 exemption, the Zoox route that took about four years and caps at 2,500 vehicles a year.

Why 30 days instead of 60, nobody outside the agency knows: the memo doesn't say, and Tesla's request isn't public beyond the quoted sentence. But agencies split differences for a reason, and the reason is usually impatience.

Meanwhile the fleet keeps growing: Cybercab #67 joined Austin on September 21, eleven days after the Special Order landed, and gold Cybercabs are filling the Dallas lot. The deadline lands nine days after Tesla's third-quarter earnings on October 21: the earnings call gets the questions, the affidavit gets the answers, and only one of those is given under penalty of $27,874 a day, capped at $139,356,994.

In fairness: self-certification is the lawful process every car in America uses, and "inapplicable" determinations are routine engineering judgments. NHTSA audits manufacturers as a matter of course; 30 days instead of 60 is ordinary case management, not a verdict. If the Cybercab is genuinely safe, and weeks of paid rides with no reported crash suggest it might be, then the paperwork fight is theater punishing the company moving fastest while Zoox spent four years in the exemption line.

What this article does not prove, because the evidence doesn't exist yet: that the Cybercab violates any standard. An audit is not a finding, and a Special Order is a demand for paper, not a recall. The "impatience" read is inference, labeled as such. Tesla's answers aren't due until October 30, so this is a story about the clock, not the verdict, and "no reported crash" across a weeks-old fleet is an absence of reporting, not a safety rating.

If you rode a Cybercab in Austin, you rode in a vehicle whose legal right to the road is under sworn federal examination. That doesn't make the ride unsafe; it makes the compliance story unresolved, and whatever Tesla swears to on October 30 will set the template for every wheel-less vehicle after it, so watch the affidavit, not the earnings call.
