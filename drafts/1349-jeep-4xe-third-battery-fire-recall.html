Jeep's Battery Fires Survived Two Recalls. The Third One Catches 320,065.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 31, 2026

Chrysler's latest recall filing for its plug-in hybrid Jeeps contains a sentence every software-fix skeptic should frame: the company's own chronology admits the 2024 remedy was "ineffective in detecting certain abnormalities" in the high-voltage battery.[1][2] Nine of the 19 known battery fires happened in Jeeps that had already received that fix.[3] That is not a recall; it is a delay with paperwork.

Read the timeline as a confession in three acts: in November 2023, recall 23V787 pulled in the 2021-2023 Wrangler 4xe for batteries that could fail internally and burn the car, parked or driven; in September 2024, recall 24V-720 expanded the net to roughly 154,000 Wrangler and Grand Cherokee 4xes, prescribing a software update to watch the batteries; and in October 2025, recall 25V741 admitted the watcher was blind.[4][3][1] Suspect cells have physical separator damage, and no software update can see torn plastic inside a lithium-ion cell.[1][2] Chrysler estimates 5% of the 320,065 vehicles carry the defect, which works out to roughly 16,000 vehicles whose early-warning system spent a year watching for the wrong thing.[2]

FCA's safety office opened the investigation behind the third recall on July 3, 2025, after fires appeared in two places the software story said were impossible: vehicles outside the old recall's scope, and vehicles that had already been fixed.[2] End of debate.

Drive a 2020-2025 Wrangler 4xe or a 2022-2026 Grand Cherokee 4xe? Do the unglamorous part: run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, park outside and away from anything flammable, and stop charging the battery until Chrysler's 68C remedy is done, because a plug-in hybrid you cannot charge is a very expensive driveway ornament, and a burned garage is worse.[1][5] That 2024 software update does not count as compliance; vehicles that got it must come back for the new remedy, which adds physical battery replacement where testing flags it.[1][3] If your dealer shrugs, call Chrysler at 800-853-1403.[3]

What this article did not prove: the true fire count is almost certainly higher than 19, since NHTSA fire reports are self-reported by owners and manufacturers, and underreporting is routine. Chrysler's 5% defect estimate is its own engineering guess, unverified by any regulator, and issued by the same company that guessed software would fix hardware. And nothing here tells you whether your specific VIN has a damaged separator; the battery test under the new remedy is the only way to know.

Fair point, stated plainly: a bigger third recall can be read as the system working. Chrysler found fires outside the old scope, expanded the population from 154,000 to 320,065, and replaced a software-only remedy with one that includes physical battery replacement.[1][3] Ten of the 19 fires were in unremedied vehicles, so the old software was never proven useless, merely proven insufficient. It is a fair defense. It is also the defense of an automaker on its third try.
