# Research #1189 — IIHS First-Ever Front Crash Prevention Ratings for Commercial Vehicles

**Journalist:** Mia Crumplezone (safety tech beat — AEB sensor/design failures)
**Angle:** IIHS just graded the auto-braking systems of America's work trucks and cargo vans for the first time, and the results are grim: 9 of 11 substandard for vehicle-to-vehicle, zero Class 3 pickups reaching "good" in either test, and the two bestselling full-size vans of the last 30 years (Chevy Express, GMC Savana) have no AEB at all. The two "good" pedestrian scores belong to battery-electric newcomer vans (BrightDrop, Rivian). Federal AEB mandates don't cover these classes, so IIHS is doing the government's job.

## Primary facts

### The program
- IIHS announced the commercial vehicle ratings program in March 2026, framing it as addressing "gaps in the U.S. vehicle safety system" (VP of Vehicle Research David Kidd).
- Launched June 2026 with driver protection checks (standard front/side airbags, advanced seatbelt tech, effective seatbelt reminders). Among cargo vans, only the 2025 Ram ProMaster 2500 and the electric 2025 Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 had effective driver seatbelt reminders. Among pickups, the Silverado 3500HD and F-350 passed; the Ram 3500 did not.
- First vehicle-to-vehicle and pedestrian front crash prevention ratings released **October 8, 2026**.
- Same good-acceptable-marginal-poor scale and protocols as passenger vehicles. Next up: headlight evaluations, then assessments of Class 4-6 trucks.

### The ratings (11 vehicles)
Class 3 pickups (GVWR 10,001-14,000 lb):
| Vehicle | Vehicle-to-vehicle | Pedestrian |
|---|---|---|
| 2025-26 Chevrolet Silverado 3500HD | Poor | Marginal |
| 2025 Ford F-350 | Poor | Acceptable |
| 2025-26 GMC Sierra 3500HD | Poor | Marginal |
| 2026-27 Ram 3500 | Marginal | Acceptable |

Cargo vans:
| Vehicle | Vehicle-to-vehicle | Pedestrian |
|---|---|---|
| 2025-26 Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 | Acceptable | **Good** |
| 2025-27 Chevrolet Express 2500 | Poor | Poor (no AEB) |
| 2025 Ford Transit T250 | Poor | Marginal |
| 2025-27 GMC Savana 2500 | Poor | Poor (no AEB) |
| 2025 Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 | **Good** | Acceptable |
| 2025-27 Ram ProMaster 2500 | Poor | Marginal |
| 2025 Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 | Marginal | **Good** |

### Standout details
- 9 of 11 rated substandard (marginal or poor) for vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention: 7 poor, 2 marginal.
- The F-350's AEB could only avoid the passenger-car target at 31 mph; it failed all four tests above that speed, and in a 43-mph test failed to reduce speed at all.
- The Silverado 3500HD failed to avoid the passenger car in all six tests.
- Motorcycles were the worst detection category across the fleet: most systems failed to warn or slow; none warned early enough for IIHS. The Transit's pre-collision assist "failed to produce a timely warning regardless of speed and position."
- Transit pedestrian warnings came 0.6-1.6 seconds before impact: enough to scrub speed, not enough to avoid the hit.
- IIHS noted some trucks "lost sight of child-sized pedestrians as they approached" (carscoops).
- Express/Savana results attributed by IIHS to missing automatic emergency braking entirely.

### The body count
- IIHS, citing NHTSA FARS: **6,215 people died in 2024 in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans = 16% of all U.S. roadway deaths.**
- Kidd to Detroit Free Press: "There are a lot of gaps between those things that are required for passenger vehicles and commercial vehicles around the airbags, advanced restraints and even towing regulations. For example, you have to have [AEB] that detects vehicles as well as pedestrians, but that's not coming for commercial vehicles... When there are large crashes, those vehicles are usually doling out the harm."

### The wake-up call quote
- IIHS President David Harkey: "These results are a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers. Front crash prevention is especially important for these larger, heavier vans and trucks because of the danger they pose to other road users."

## Novel angle (kill test: does this clear it?)
- YES. First-ever ratings for a vehicle class that represents 16% of roadway deaths — IIHS is publicly backfilling a federal regulatory gap. The Express/Savana is a platform that dates to 1996 and is still sold new in 2026 with no AEB. The inverse correlation: the best pedestrian scores come from the two EV-native delivery vans (Rivian RCV-Delivery 500, BrightDrop 400), i.e., the newest entrants see pedestrians best while the legacy heavy pickups can't.

## Original contribution (for the article)
- Regulatory-gap cross-tab: NHTSA's AEB rule covers passenger vehicles up to 10,000 lb GVWR; Class 3 (10,001-14,000 lb) sits above it, which is exactly the band where the biggest consumer-accessible pickups live. The F-350 you can buy on Saturday morning at any Ford dealer is tested on a looser standard than the Escape next to it in the showroom.
- The "same money, different eyes" comparison: a buyer shopping an F-350 vs a Sprinter van is choosing between Poor and Good in the test that predicts whether the truck stops before it hits a car — but no window sticker is required to tell them that. IIHS is now that window sticker.

## Counterargument (steelman)
- These are protocol tests against dummies, not real-world crash outcome data; fleet buyers argue AEB tuning on 14,000-lb vehicles must account for load, trailer dynamics, and false-positive risk (a 7-ton vehicle emergency-braking on a false positive is its own hazard). Commercial vehicles operate differently — more urban delivery miles, professional drivers. Real-world FARS death-rate per VMT by model isn't available for these classes from FARS the same way it is for passenger cars, so "poor rating" does not equal "most deadly on the road."

## Limitations
- No fleet mileage exposure data; ratings are protocol performance, not crash outcomes.
- Child-pedestrian "lost sight" claim is a protocol observation (dummy position vs. hood height), not a field study of front-blind-zone incidents.
- Manufacturer responses: as of Oct 12 reporting, no manufacturer had announced a fix (Automotive Files). GM discontinued BrightDrop 400 production — per eletric-vehicles.com, GM no longer builds it.
- The 6,215 figure covers medium/heavy-duty vehicles AND light vans, broader than the 11 tested Class 3 vehicles; it establishes the stakes, not a causal claim about the tested models.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- Fleet buyers: the Sprinter 2500 is the only Good vehicle-to-vehicle score; BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 lead pedestrian detection. If your route runs through neighborhoods with kids, the Express and Savana are the last thing you should spec.
- Consumers: an F-350 can't reliably auto-brake before hitting a car at highway-adjacent speeds (43-mph test: zero speed reduction). Give heavy pickups and full-size vans the following distance you'd give a truck with no electronic help, because the sensors may be decorative.
- Check NHTSA's AEB rulemaking: the rule that protects you from a Camry's AEB doesn't extend above 10,000 lb. IIHS ratings are currently the only public scoreboard.
