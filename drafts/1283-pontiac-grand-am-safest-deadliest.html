The Gap

The Forgettable Pontiac Was the Safest of Its Siblings. Its Last Year Was Its Worst.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 26, 2026

Pontiac has been dead for sixteen years. Its cars have not stopped killing people. Between 2014 and 2023, the Pontiac Grand Am, the cheap sedan nobody remembers fondly, was involved in 921 fatal crashes and 713 people died, at a rate of 1.18 deaths per 100 million miles traveled.[1] That is the lowest rate among Pontiac's three high-volume sedans in federal crash data, lower than the Grand Prix and lower than the G6. The forgettable one was the safe one.

1.18
Pontiac Grand Am deaths per 100 million miles, 2014-2023, vs 2.14 for the Grand Prix

Start with the excuse that usually works: drunk drivers. It fails here, completely. Grand Am drivers in fatal crashes tested impaired 21.6% of the time, against 21.5% for Grand Prix drivers and 22.1% for G6 drivers.[2] A six-tenths-of-a-point spread in impairment cannot carry an 81% gap in death rate between the Grand Am and the Grand Prix, or a 39% gap against the G6. Whatever separates these three sedans, it is not who was behind the wheel. Scope note: smaller Pontiac nameplates rate lower, but on fewer than 140 deaths each, too thin to rank against sedans with 700-plus deaths.

Nor is it a General Motors thing, since Buick's sedans from the same era rate more than double the Grand Am: LeSabre 2.67, Century 2.41.[3] Same corporate parent, same era, same sedan job, twice the death rate. Size and weight explain some of any sedan-versus-sedan gap in multi-vehicle crashes, but they cannot explain a gap between cars this close in class.[4]

Then comes the twist that ruins the feel-good version. The Grand Am's deadliest model year is 2004, its last full year of production: 148 deaths, more than a fifth of the car's entire ten-year toll from a single year.[1] Fair warning, though: model-year counts are counts, not rates, and 2004 was a big sales year, so part of that peak is just more cars on the road. Old cheap cars also skew toward younger, poorer, less-belted drivers on worse roads, and this dataset cannot fully separate the car from its driver and its roads. Granted. But the impairment parity across the three Pontiac sedans takes out the most common version of that excuse, and the Grand Am's fleet is the largest of the three, not the smallest.

So if you are shopping at the bottom of the used market, let model year beat badge loyalty. A Grand Am is not a safety rating, and neither is a dead brand's reputation. Skip the 2004 cars specifically, check IIHS ratings for the exact year you are buying, and run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands. If you already drive one, treat it like what the numbers say it is: an old car from a dead brand with no free fix coming. Belt every seat, keep the tires and restraints maintained, and do not let familiarity pass for safety.
