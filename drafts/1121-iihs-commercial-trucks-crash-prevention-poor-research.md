# Research Notes — #1121: IIHS First Commercial-Vehicle Crash Prevention Ratings

**Slug:** 1121-iihs-commercial-trucks-crash-prevention-poor
**Journalist:** Rex Driverton (Investigation)
**Topic:** IIHS's first-ever front crash prevention ratings for commercial vehicles — cargo vans and Class 3 pickups (released Oct 8, 2026). Most scored Poor; motorcycles the blind spot; two workhorse vans have no AEB at all.

## Verified facts
1. **First of its kind:** IIHS ran its first commercial-vehicle crash-prevention evaluation series (vehicle-to-vehicle + pedestrian), 11 vehicles, at the Vehicle Research Center in Ruckersville, Virginia. Announced Oct 8, 2026. (freep.com, Oct 8 2026)
2. **Poor in vehicle-to-vehicle (7 of 11):** 2025-2027 Chevrolet Express 2500, 2025 Ford Transit T250, 2025-2027 GMC Savana 2500, 2025-2027 Ram ProMaster 2500, 2025-2026 Chevrolet Silverado 3500 HD, 2025 Ford F-350, 2025-2026 GMC Sierra 3500. Not one pickup managed a Good in either crash-prevention test. (freep.com; autoblog, Oct 7-8 2026)
3. **Good ratings (3 total):** Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 — Good in vehicle-to-vehicle. Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 (discontinued EV van) and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 (Amazon delivery van platform) — Good in pedestrian crash avoidance. (fleetmanagementweekly; fordauthority, Oct 9 2026)
4. **Motorcycles are the worst case:** the motorcycle target was the biggest challenge; several systems "did not issue warnings or slow at all" in most motorcycle scenarios. The F-350's system could only avoid a passenger-car target at 31 mph; at 43 mph it did not reduce speed at all. (freep.com; autoblog)
5. **Two vans have no AEB at all:** Express 2500 and Savana 2500 scored Poor in pedestrian tests because they are not equipped with automatic emergency braking — only optional forward-collision warning. These are 1996-era platforms still on sale today. (freep.com)
6. **Children "disappear":** even the Acceptable-rated F-350 and Ram 3500 could avoid the adult dummy at night but NOT the child-size dummy in broad daylight — at close distances children vanish from the driver's and sensors' line of sight behind tall front ends. (freep.com)
7. **Body count:** 6,215 people died in 2024 in crashes involving medium/heavy-duty vehicles or light vans — 16% of all US roadway fatalities (FARS 2024). (freep.com)
8. **The regulatory gap:** IIHS VP David Kidd — fewer government standards for delivery vans, box trucks, tractor-trailers and larger work pickups than passenger cars; AEB that detects vehicles and pedestrians is required for passenger cars but "not coming for commercial vehicles." His line: "When there are large crashes, those vehicles are usually doling out the harm." (freep.com)
9. **NHTSA's own math (June 2023):** a federal heavy-vehicle AEB requirement would save ~155 lives, prevent 8,814 injuries and 19,000+ crashes per year, ~$2B/year. Rule still not finalized. (Advocates for Highway and Auto Safety via freep.com)
10. **AEB works:** AEB is proven to cut rear-end crash rates by 50% in passenger vehicles (Kidd). Passenger-car glare in IIHS testing fell from 21% (2017) to 3% (2025 models). (freep.com; autoconnectedcar)

## Contrast / context
- Passenger vehicles: NHTSA's light-vehicle AEB rule (finalized April 2024, passenger cars and light trucks) has a 2029 compliance target; Kidd notes the equivalent for commercial vehicles is "not coming." The vehicles responsible for 6,215 deaths — 16% of all fatalities — face weaker AEB requirements than a passenger sedan.
- The good performers are the newcomers: Sprinter (European safety culture), BrightDrop (discontinued EV), Rivian RCV (Amazon EV van). The worst performers are legacy domestic platforms — Express/Savana ride on an architecture launched in 1996.
- Motorcycle vulnerability: #862 (Motorcycle Deaths Fell 2.2% in 2024; young riders rose) — this rating series shows why detection, not rider behavior, is the gap for the heaviest vehicles.

## Novel contribution (original framing)
The regulatory asymmetry calculation: the vehicles responsible for 16% of all US road deaths face a weaker AEB mandate than a Honda Civic. And the age gap: the two AEB-less vans (Express/Savana) are sold on a 30-year-old platform — the crash-avoidance tech gap is a platform-age gap. Kidd's "doling out the harm" line maps to the FARS 16% figure: these vehicles don't primarily kill their occupants; they kill everyone else.

## Kill test
Passes: genuinely fresh (ratings released Oct 8, 2026 — 2 days ago), first-ever commercial-vehicle ratings from IIHS, novel angle (the deadliest vehicles to everyone else have the weakest brakes-by-default), actionable (fleet buyers, parents, motorcyclists, pedestrian guidance), and a regulator-grade number (155 lives/year from NHTSA itself).

## Counterargument (strongest)
Commercial vehicles are driven by professionals who log massive miles; per-mile crash rates may still be low, and fleet buyers buy on total cost, not IIHS ratings. The motorcycle test target is a new methodology — manufacturers had no chance to tune for it, so Poor ratings partly reflect a test nobody designed against. Also: driver inattention, not missing AEB, causes most of these crashes; AEB is a backstop, not a fix.

## Limitations
- IIHS ratings measure crash-avoidance systems, not crashworthiness; a Poor prevention score says nothing about occupant survival in a crash that happens.
- Motorcycle test methodology is brand new — no historical baseline; ratings will shift as manufacturers tune for it.
- 6,215 deaths involve medium/heavy vehicles OR light vans — mixed vehicle classes; do not attribute all to the 11 tested models.
- Class 4-6 trucks (the truly heavy ones) are not yet tested; these results understate the problem.
- Express/Savana platform age (1996) is established automotive history, but their lack of AEB is per the tested model years (2025-2027), not all model years ever sold.

## Actionable takeaways
- Fleet buyer: specify the Sprinter 2500 or an AEB-equipped van for any vehicle that enters neighborhoods; refuse AEB-less vans (Express/Savana) for new orders — the IIHS ratings give you leverage with your dealer.
- Parent: the child-dummy finding is the actionable horror — never assume a tall work truck's driver (or its sensors) can see a child close to the front bumper. Hold hands in parking lots around work vehicles.
- Motorcyclist: assume every box truck and heavy pickup cannot detect you at all — several systems literally did not warn or brake in motorcycle scenarios.
- Driver behind one of these: leave extra space; its crash-avoidance is worse than the car you're driving.
