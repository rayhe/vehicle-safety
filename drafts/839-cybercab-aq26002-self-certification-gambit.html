Tesla Certified a Car With No Steering Wheel as Compliant. NHTSA Wants to See the Math.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 7, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Somewhere in Austin this week, a gold two-seater with no steering wheel, no brake pedal, and no mirrors has been carrying paying passengers on public roads, because Tesla filed paperwork saying the car complies with every federal safety standard that applies to it. Four days ago, the federal agency that writes those standards opened an audit to examine, in its words, the extent to which Tesla's certification "depended on determinations that certain FMVSS are inapplicable to the Cybercab."[1] Read that sentence again. Slowly. It is a regulator asking a car company to show its work on the theory that the rules don't apply.

50×
Tesla's installed Cybercab production capacity (125,000/yr) divided by the federal exemption path's annual cap (2,500/yr)

Nobody at NHTSA approved the Cybercab before it hit the streets, because in the United States nobody approves anything. Automakers self-certify. American car regulation has a ritual, and it goes like this: you build the car, you declare that it meets all applicable Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards, and you start selling, while the agency gets to audit your homework later, after the cars are already among us, if it ever gets around to it.[2] It got around to it on September 3, 2026, the same week Tesla launched commercial rides through its Robotaxi app in Austin, opening Audit Query AQ26002, an investigation "prompted by public information" covering an estimated 1,000 vehicles.[3]

Compliance by declared inapplicability is a beautiful legal machine. Picture it. A safety standard assumes a human driver sits behind a steering wheel, reaches a brake pedal, and checks a side mirror, because the standard was written in an era when that was the only kind of car anyone could imagine. You build a car with none of those things. Then you certify that you comply with all applicable standards, having quietly decided, internally, that the standards requiring a steering wheel do not apply to your car because your car has no steering wheel. Claim and noncompliance arrive in the same sentence. It is the legal equivalent of grading your own exam and awarding yourself full marks on the sections you didn't take.

We know exactly how this exam goes, because Amazon's Zoox already took it. In June 2022, Zoox self-certified its purpose-built robotaxi, a rectangular four-seat pod with no driver controls, arguing the relevant standards didn't apply. NHTSA opened Audit Query AQ23001 in March 2023, inspectors found multiple apparent FMVSS noncompliances, and in August 2025 the agency closed the probe by granting a demonstration exemption on one condition: Zoox had to remove or cover every public statement claiming its vehicles complied with federal safety standards.[4] Imagine being ordered by the federal government to stop saying your car is legal. Then, on July 30, 2026, NHTSA granted Zoox a commercial exemption: up to 2,500 vehicles a year, no heavy rain or snow, speed limits of 45 mph or less, enhanced crash reporting.[5] Four years of federal scrutiny bought the right to operate a slow toaster in dry weather.

4 years
From Zoox's self-certification (June 2022) to commercial clearance (July 2026), ending in a 45-mph, no-rain cap

Tesla looked at that outcome and chose the same path at fifty times the scale. NHTSA says Tesla never applied for the Part 555 temporary-exemption process at all, and engineering chief Lars Moravy has said the Cybercab "will not be subject to that cap," the 2,500-vehicle annual limit.[6] Why skip the legal door? Because the door is too small. Under the exemption path you are capped at 2,500 vehicles a year, and Tesla has installed manufacturing capacity for more than 125,000 Cybercabs a year at a target price under $30,000.[2] The legal route physically cannot accommodate the business plan. Self-certification was not a reading of the law so much as a scheduling decision: the only path that doesn't put the factory on ice.

Meanwhile, and this is my favorite part, the regulator auditing Tesla for acting like the brake-pedal rule is obsolete has itself proposed making the brake-pedal rule obsolete. In June 2026, NHTSA proposed ending the requirement for manual brake pedals in self-driving vehicles, part of a package to speed autonomous deployment; the changes are not yet in effect.[6] The agency is simultaneously arguing the rule is outdated and auditing a company for behaving as if it is. The universe has a sense of humor. It is just darker than most.

None of this is about whether the Cybercab is safe. No crash, injury, or defect has been alleged. The audit targets the certification process, not the vehicle, and an audit query is an investigative step, not a finding of anything.[3] If you want the sober version of the worry, Bryant Walker Smith of the University of South Carolina supplied it: "No public information about Tesla's capabilities suggests that Tesla is anywhere close to being able to safely and reliably deploy an automated driving system over the wide range of conditions that would be required for a vehicle without conventional controls."[6] NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison's version was more diplomatic: the agency supports AV development but "we need to ensure that all of our laws are followed."[7] And three former senior NHTSA officials told Reuters the clash could end up in court, adding, ominously, that "NHTSA, importantly, has not always won these cases."[6]

Consider the fullest version of the other side, because it deserves its day. NHTSA's own June proposal signals the agency believes the legacy rules are outdated for purpose-built AVs, so Tesla's underlying reading may be vindicated by rulemaking within months. Self-certification is the lawful process every automaker uses, and AQ26002 is an audit, not a verdict; Tesla's paperwork could be immaculate. The company that skipped the exemption queue might simply be early to a compliance regime the agency is already building. If the rules are getting rewritten anyway, being first is just efficient.

What the audit cannot tell us is bounded, and the bounds are wide. Tesla's actual certification filing is not public, so nobody outside the agency has seen the FMVSS-by-FMVSS applicability analysis being questioned. The Zoox audit took more than two years to close, and Tesla's version is bigger, louder, and politically hotter, so do not hold your breath for a verdict. The 125,000-a-year figure is Tesla's stated capacity, not verified output; as of last week, Texas had 45 Cybercabs registered.[2] And nothing in AQ26002 measures whether the car crashes more or less than the thing it replaces, which is, in the end, the only question that will matter to anyone in its path.

What to actually do: if you hail a driverless ride, know which legal door the operator walked through. Zoox's exemption shows what the regulated version looks like: 45 mph, no heavy rain or snow, enhanced crash reporting. A company that self-certified instead has traded years of legal exposure for speed, and the legal exposure has now begun. Track AQ26002 in NHTSA's defect-investigation database for updates; the Zoox precedent suggests the next milestone is measured in years, not weeks. And the next time a company tells you its product "meets all applicable standards," pause, and ask who decided which standards were applicable. Sometimes the answer is the company; four days later, it isn't.
