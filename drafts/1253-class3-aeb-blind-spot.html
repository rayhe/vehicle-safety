The Gap

America's Heaviest Pickups Just Flunked the Brake Test the Law Doesn't Require

By Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 23, 2026

IIHS finally asked America's biggest pickups a simple question: can you stop yourself before you hit something? Not one earned so much as an Acceptable, when the something was another vehicle. The Ford F-350, Chevrolet Silverado 3500HD, and GMC Sierra 3500 all rated Poor in the vehicle-to-vehicle crash-avoidance test; the Ram 3500 managed only Marginal. These are the first front crash prevention ratings IIHS has ever issued for Class 3 pickups, trucks with a gross vehicle weight rating of 10,001 to 14,000 pounds.[1]

6,215
People killed in 2024 in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans: 16% of all U.S. roadway deaths

Motorcycles were the worst part. Several systems didn't warn the driver or brake at all in most of the motorcycle trials. When the target wore a windshield, the truck brakes stayed asleep. IIHS President David Harkey called the results "a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers," which is the polite way of saying the biggest, heaviest vehicles sharing your lane have the worst electronic reflexes.[1]

Only one bright spot among the vans: the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned Good in vehicle-to-vehicle, and the Chevy BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 earned Good for pedestrians. Everyone else landed at Marginal or worse, and two cargo vans still on sale (the 2025–27 Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500) rated Poor in both tests for the simplest possible reason: they don't have automatic emergency braking. Just an optional forward-collision warning. In 2026.[1]

Now the physics, because physics doesn't do marketing. Kinetic energy is 1/2 m v². At identical speed, the energy a vehicle must shed scales with its mass. A 14,000-pound Class 3 pickup at 62 mph carries about 2.44 megajoules of kinetic energy; a 3,500-pound car at the same speed carries about 0.61. That's a 4-to-1 ratio of stopping energy, at identical speed. The vehicles that demand the most from their brakes are the ones whose brakes were just measured for the first time. Ever.

And here's where the rulebook gets embarrassing. NHTSA's FMVSS No. 127, finalized in 2024, requires new passenger cars and light trucks to avoid contact with a lead vehicle up to 62 mph, brake automatically up to 90 mph, and detect pedestrians by September 2029. It applies to vehicles up to 10,000 pounds GVWR. Class 3 pickups start at 10,001 pounds.[2] The mandate stops one pound short of the class that needs it most. Nobody in this month's coverage noticed the overlap, so here it is: the regulatory gap and the performance gap land on the exact same weight line.

One more finding, and it's the creepy one. The test sensors sit near the rearview mirror, which on a Class 3 truck is somewhere around your second-story window. With the child dummy, several trucks detected it at distance, then lost it as they got closer — the dummy "disappeared" beneath the sightline of the tall front end. Some systems struggled more with a child in daylight than with an adult at night. If you park a work truck with the nose pointed at a school crosswalk, the machine cannot reliably see what a human standing at the bumper could.[1]

What should you actually do? If you buy trucks for a fleet, pull up IIHS's commercial vehicle ratings before the next order cycle, and cross the Express 2500 and Savana 2500 off the list unless a dealer can spec real AEB into them. If you drive a Class 3 pickup for a living, drive it like your radar is ornamental. In the motorcycle trials, sometimes it was. If you ride a motorcycle, give work trucks the space they cannot give themselves. And if you write federal regulation, maybe reconsider a cutoff that exempts the heaviest vehicles on the commuter's road.

What this doesn't prove: FARS 2014–2023 predates every vehicle IIHS tested, so these ratings can't be tied to real-world fatality counts yet. The motorcycle and child trials used dummies on a closed track, and performance swung between low and high beams. The 4-to-1 kinetic-energy ratio uses loaded GVWR against a light car (a more typical curb-weight comparison is roughly 2-to-1, and the KE growth that matters most is with speed, not load). Also: heavy-duty pickups have some of the lowest per-VMT fatality rates in FARS (F-350: 0.15), because they're mostly driven by professionals; the risk they pose falls on everyone around them, not their drivers. The 6,215-death figure includes box trucks and tractor-trailers, not just Class 3 pickups and cargo vans.
