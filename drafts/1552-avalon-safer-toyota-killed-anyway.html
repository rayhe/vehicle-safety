Toyota Killed the Avalon. The Data Says It Was the Safer Toyota.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

Before you sign for a used Camry, you might want to see this. Toyota stopped selling the Avalon after the 2022 model year. No farewell tour, no press release with a tear in its eye, just a quiet page on Toyota's website pointing buyers toward the Crown. What Toyota did not put on that page: the car it killed was the safer Toyota. Between 2014 and 2023, 469 people died in Avalons, at 1.33 deaths per 100 million miles. Over the same decade the Camry, the car Toyota kept, killed 6,328 people at 2.03. Same company, same platform family, and one of them kills a third slower, the one you can no longer buy new.[1][4]

1.33
Avalon deaths per 100 million miles, 2014–2023, against 2.03 for the Camry and 3.07 for the Accord.

Blame the drivers first, because that is the usual excuse, and watch it fail. Avalon drivers in fatal crashes tested impaired 21.7 percent of the time, alcohol-positive 16.2 percent, the drunkest Toyota drivers in the sedan comparison and the third-highest impairment rate in the whole table. Camry drivers tested impaired 19.2 percent of the time. Sober drivers, faster death rate; drunker drivers, slower death rate. Impairment explains nothing here, in either direction, which is exactly the point: when the rate spread runs 7.7x across sedans while impairment barely moves, the gap is the vehicle, not the driver. And this time the vehicle doing the protecting is the one with the worse drivers in it.[1]

What is different is about 120 pounds, for a start. A 2022 Avalon carries a curb weight of 3,570 pounds against the Camry's 3,450, on a stretched version of the same platform, and the fifth-generation car runs up to 3,715 pounds. IIHS has documented the size-and-weight mechanism for years: in a two-car crash, the heavier car wins, and every extra pound is a small transfer of risk from your cabin to theirs. That is not the whole story, because buyer age, highway trip mix, and standard Toyota Safety Sense 2.5 on the final generation are all tangled up with the weight, and this file cannot untangle them. But the lab record points the same way the road record does, which is more than the Camry can say. A final-generation Avalon earned IIHS Top Safety Pick with Good in every crashworthiness test and Superior front crash prevention, plus a five-star NHTSA overall rating.[9] Meanwhile the Camry holds the higher award, Top Safety Pick+, and still kills faster per mile. The award gap runs one way and the death-rate gap runs the other.[2][5][6][8]

Age helps too, and not in the direction you would guess. Adjust each model year for its years on the road inside the window, and newer Avalons kill at roughly half the annual rate of the 2000s cars: the 2015 through 2020 cohort runs about 1.2 to 1.6 deaths per exposure-year, against 2.1 to 3.9 for the 2005 through 2008 cohort. Peak single model year is 2006, at 39 deaths. Two-thirds of all Avalon deaths sit in 2000s model years. Put plainly, the safest Avalon ever built is the 2019 to 2022 fifth-generation car on the TNGA-K platform, and it is the one Toyota stopped selling.[1]

Fairness check, because the headline is doing a lot of work. Avalon buyers skew older and wealthier, drive fewer late-night miles, and take more highway trips, all of which lower fatality rates without the car doing anything. This file measures none of that directly, so some of the 34 percent is almost certainly the buyer, not the bumper. And here is the number that keeps the Avalon honest: when an Avalon is in a fatal crash, 0.684 people die per crash, against 0.593 for the Camry. Once the crash is fatal, the Avalon is the harsher car to be in, consistent with older occupants and fuller cabins. Its advantage is entirely in staying out of the file, not in softening it. Add the small-fleet caveat, 306,250 cars against the Camry's 2.7 million, which widens the uncertainty band around 1.33, though not by enough to close a 34 percent gap. None of that rescues the Camry's rate, because a rate can be mid-pack and still be a public-health problem when the pack is this big.

If you are shopping a used full-size sedan, the buy is a 2019 to 2022 Avalon, roughly $23,000 to $36,000 depending on trim and mileage, with Toyota Safety Sense 2.5 standard and the IIHS award in hand. Check the headlights before you check anything else: only the Limited, Touring, and Hybrid Limited trims carry the Good-rated LED projectors, while the XLE and XSE trims carry Poor-rated LED reflectors, which is what kept the Avalon from Top Safety Pick+. If you already own a 2005 to 2008 Avalon, it kills at roughly double the annual rate of the newer car, and the upgrade that matters is the 2019 redesign, not a newer Camry. Either way, check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands. Toyota killed the safer Toyota. The used market is the only place left to buy it.
