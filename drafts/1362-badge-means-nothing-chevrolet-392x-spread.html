The Gap

Chevrolet's Safest Car Is 392 Times Safer Than Its Deadliest. Same Badge.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 2, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. A Chevrolet Prizm posts 0.02 deaths per 100 million miles traveled. Chevrolet's Tracker: 7.83. Same bowtie on the grille, 392 times the death rate. I ran the numbers for every major automaker in the federal crash database, and the pattern holds everywhere: the badge tells you almost nothing, while your model choice tells you almost everything.

392x
Chevrolet's deadliest model vs. its safest, per FARS 2014–2023

Toyota is supposed to be the safe choice, and the RAV4 backs that up at 0.19. But Toyota's deadliest model, the Land Cruiser, posts 6.27, worse than a Chevrolet Impala and a 314-fold spread from Toyota's safest car, the Matrix. Ford runs 120-fold from Bronco (0.05) to Mustang (6.02), and Hyundai 142-fold from Palisade (0.06) to Veloster (8.54). That "just buy a Toyota" heuristic fails precisely where the money is: big, expensive halo vehicles that the reputation was built on.[1]

So what separates the survivors from the statistics? Sometimes it is engineering, honestly: modern crossovers like the RAV4, Traverse, Bronco, and Palisade cluster at the safe end of every brand's range because they were designed after stability control and side-curtain airbags became standard equipment.[3] But much of the gap is who buys the car and what they do with it: Mustangs get bought by young men and driven accordingly, Land Cruisers get taken off-road and deep into rural territory far from a trauma center, and Trackers are cheap beaters driven hard and maintained never, same badge and different species behind the wheel.

Fair warning, because the data deserves honesty: the extremes are noisy, since the Prizm died in 2002 and only 87,500 of them were still generating miles in this window, which means a handful of crashes can swing its rate wildly. FARS counts fatalities only, never injuries, and these rates divide by estimated miles rather than odometer readings. And the badge deserves its strongest defense here: it is not meaningless, because brands that rolled safety tech across their whole lineup early genuinely lifted their entire range. Call it a weak signal, not a dead one.

But a weak signal is a lousy way to buy a car, so when a model-level number exists, and IIHS publishes driver death rates by make and model, updated and free, choosing by brand reputation means choosing to know less for no reason at all.[2] Check the specific model before you buy, especially used, where the spread runs widest, then run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. And if anyone tells you some brand is "the safe one," ask them which model. Watch the confidence drain from their face.
