The Gap

The 14,000-Pound Truck Behind You Can’t Stop Itself. Nobody Required It To.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 9, 2026

On October 8, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety did something it had never done before: it tested the stopping ability of work trucks and cargo vans, using the same front crash prevention protocols it applies to passenger cars.[1] Eleven vehicles went through the vehicle-to-vehicle and pedestrian tests. Nine of the eleven rated Marginal or Poor at avoiding a crash with the vehicle ahead. One van earned Good, one earned Acceptable, and every heavy-duty pickup in America failed.

Work trucks and cargo vans rated Marginal or Poor in IIHS vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention, October 2026

Start with the vans, because the spread tells the whole story in one table. A Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned the only Good rating for vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention. Chevrolet’s BrightDrop 400 was Acceptable, and it earned Good for pedestrians, alongside Rivian’s RCV-Delivery 500. Then the floor falls out, and Ford’s Transit T250 and Ram’s ProMaster 2500 both rated Poor at avoiding the vehicle ahead. Chevrolet’s Express 2500 and the GMC Savana 2500 rated Poor in both tests, and the reason is not a bad sensor or a cheap camera. Those two vans have no automatic emergency braking at all. A 2025, 2026, or 2027 model-year van, sold new today, offers an optional forward collision warning and nothing else, a beep where a brake pedal should be.[1]

Pickups did worse, which takes some doing. Not one of the four Class 3 pickups earned Good in either test. Ford’s F-350, Chevrolet’s Silverado 3500HD, and the GMC Sierra 3500 all rated Poor at vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention. Ram’s 3500 managed Marginal. On pedestrian tests, the F-350 and Ram 3500 reached Acceptable while the two GM trucks stayed Marginal. Motorcycle detection was the biggest failure across the whole group. Several systems issued no warning and did not slow at all in most motorcycle trials.[1] And then there is the child dummy, which several trucks detected at a distance and then lost as they closed in, because the sensors sit up near the rearview mirror and the child is short and the truck’s front end is tall, which means a child can disappear from a work truck’s sensors by getting closer, a sentence worth reading twice.

None of this is an accident of engineering. It is an accident of arithmetic, and the arithmetic lives in a federal rule. FMVSS 127, finalized in April 2024, requires automatic emergency braking and pedestrian braking on all passenger cars and light trucks, by September 2029, with systems that must stop for a lead vehicle at speeds up to 62 mph and detect pedestrians day and night.[2] It applies to vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating of 10,000 pounds or less. Class 3 pickups start at 10,001 pounds. One pound of rated capacity is the entire difference between a vehicle that must stop itself and a vehicle that may not even warn you. NHTSA and the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration proposed a separate heavy-vehicle AEB rule in June 2023. It is still not final.[2] Meanwhile FMVSS 127 itself sits in legal limbo, its D.C. Circuit appeal held in abeyance since February 2025 while the September 2029 deadline stands on paper and nobody in Washington will say what happens next.[5] In 2024, 6,215 people died in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans, 16 percent of every traffic death in the country, about 17 people a day.[1] Those are the vehicles with the weakest crash-avoidance technology ever measured, and the gap is not a secret. It is a weight cutoff.

So what do you do with it? If you buy for a fleet, the IIHS ratings are now a procurement document, and the math is blunt: an on-the-job crash costs an employer more than $55,000 on average, before anyone gets hurt.[3] A Sprinter 2500, a BrightDrop 400, or a Rivian delivery van against an Express or Savana is a spec-sheet decision with a five-figure price tag on the wrong answer, and you should demand the IIHS rating before you sign the order, not after the first rear-end claim. If you are shopping for a van yourself, know that no Express or Savana at any price includes automatic braking. If you tow with an F-350 or a Silverado 3500HD, drive like your truck’s crash prevention is decorative, because IIHS just showed that it mostly is. And if you are everyone else on the road, the move is simpler: leave the gap. A 14,000-pound truck behind you in traffic is statistically less likely to stop itself than the sedan next to it, and the driver may be excellent while the software is not.

Fairness demands the narrower version, because the headline version overreaches. IIHS ratings are laboratory protocols, not real-world crash rates, and the trucks were tested unloaded or as incomplete builds, which changes braking performance in ways that cut both directions. A Poor rating means the system failed IIHS’s specific scenarios at specific speeds against specific targets, not that the truck has been proven deadly in service. And the 6,215 deaths count crashes involving these vehicle classes, not crashes caused by them, so a delivery van rear-ended at a red light counts the same as a van that plowed into stopped traffic. Fleet buyers who chose the Express chose price, too, and the market produced part of this outcome alongside the regulator. What survives all of that is still the story: the exemption left a measurable capability gap, IIHS measured it, the gap is widest exactly where the vehicles are heaviest, and one of the two vans that earned a Good pedestrian rating, the BrightDrop 400, is a vehicle GM stopped building in October 2025.[4] The good van is already dead. The vans that can’t stop are still on sale.
