The Gap

Nissan's Entry-Level Crossover Just Beat the Audi Q3. The Catch Is Stamped in the Door Jamb.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2026

Six models went through IIHS's October 2026 ratings round. Exactly one came out with a Top Safety Pick: the Nissan Kicks, the entry-level crossover in the group.[1] A redesigned Audi Q3 failed. Both versions of the redesigned Toyota RAV4 failed. Chevrolet's Trailblazer failed, and so did the Volkswagen Jetta. One test did almost all of the damage, and it was not the test most shoppers think about when they picture a crash.

Models in IIHS's October 2026 ratings round that earned a Top Safety Pick award

Credit the back seat. IIHS revised its moderate overlap front test in 2022 so a second-row dummy's injuries count heavily, a change meant to catch exactly the kind of harm that hides behind a good driver-side score, and the Kicks is the car that finally learned the lesson in public.[2] When this generation debuted for 2025, it scored Poor in that test. Nissan then changed the rear seat belt load limiters, first for 2026 models and again for cars built after June 2026, and the retest came back Good across the board except for two Acceptable marks in the rear.[2] No new body structure and no redesigned frame: belt tuning moved a car from the bottom of the scale to the top of it, which tells you what this test actually measures: not how much steel surrounds you, but how gently the belts catch the person sitting behind you.

Now look at who lost, because the pattern is the story. Audi's redesigned Q3 scored Good in every other evaluation and Poor in moderate overlap, which alone disqualified it, since a Good in that test is a requirement for the award.[3] Toyota's redesigned RAV4 did the same dance twice: Acceptable with the standard hybrid powertrain, Marginal as a plug-in hybrid, Good everywhere else, no award either way.[3] Chevrolet's Trailblazer scored Poor in moderate overlap and added weak headlights and front crash prevention to the pile.[3] Five failures, and four of them trace to one rear-occupant test. Shoppers who compare badges and sticker prices are comparing the wrong things; shoppers who compare rear dummy scores are comparing the thing that actually decided this round.

And then comes the trap, because the fine print on the Kicks award covers only cars built after June 2026.[1] Same model year, same badge, same showroom, yet two different crash ratings separated by a factory calendar line that no window sticker discloses.

Check the label, because IIHS says a specific car's build date sits on the certification label, usually on the driver door or the adjacent B-pillar, and that month and year is the entire difference between buying the Good-rated car and buying the Poor-rated one.[2] A used listing that says "2026 Kicks" tells you nothing. A dealer who says "it's the new one" is guessing. Open the driver door, read the label, confirm a build date after June 2026, and only then believe the award. Anyone shopping a 2026 Kicks without doing that is paying award-winning money for a car that flunked the test that matters.

Fairness demands the other half of this, and it starts with a plain fact: Audi and Toyota did not build unsafe cars. Both earned Good in every evaluation except the one rear-weighted test, and a household that never puts anyone in the back seat could reasonably prefer the Q3's otherwise clean sheet.[3] Nissan's own prize is the lower tier, too: Top Safety Pick, not Top Safety Pick+, which means its front crash prevention ratings did not clear the higher bar.[1] An honest version of this headline reads narrower than the one above it: on one specific back-seat-weighted test, the cheap car passed and the expensive ones did not, and the cheap car's award stops one rung short of the top.

Two more limits before you act on any of this. IIHS crashworthiness is not a real-world fatality rate, and a Good here says nothing about how a model performs in police-reported crashes, which is a different dataset with different biases.[2] Award tiers matter as well: Top Safety Pick and Top Safety Pick+ sound like synonyms, but the plus demands stronger front crash prevention, so a badge alone never tells you which tier a car cleared. Check the tier, the build date, and the rear scores. Those three checks take about ninety seconds, and in this round they were worth more than the badge on the grille.

Before you sign anything, run the door-jamb check on any 2026 or 2027 Kicks, new or used, and treat a pre-July-2026 build as a different car for safety purposes, because IIHS does. If you are cross-shopping the Q3, RAV4, or Trailblazer instead, ask specifically about rear-occupant protection in a moderate overlap crash, not about safety in general, since general safety is where those cars already shine. Salespeople will happily discuss the badge, so make them discuss the back seat instead.
