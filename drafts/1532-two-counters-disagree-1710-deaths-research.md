# Research — #1532: The Two Official Counters Disagree by 1,710 Deaths

**Slug:** `1532-two-counters-disagree-1710-deaths`
**Journalist:** Axle McScatter (Data Visualization Editor — statistical roundups, methodology beat)
**Kicker:** By The Numbers
**Date researched:** November 16, 2026

## Angle
America has two official traffic-death counters, and they disagree by 1,710 lives for the same six months. NHTSA says 16,750 people died on the roads in the first half of 2026. The National Safety Council says 18,460. Both numbers were published as the news of the year. Both are "official." The gap between them is four times larger than the improvement both are celebrating. The 425 fewer deaths NHTSA is touting sits entirely inside a 1,710-death definitional fog.

## Kill test
- **Newsworthy:** Yes. Both halves of the comparison landed within the last ten weeks: NSC on September 9, NHTSA on November 10. The two releases have been quoted separately all over the press; nobody has put them head-to-head.
- **Novel angle:** Yes. The site has covered NHTSA's number (#1458, #1494) and NSC's number (#1402, #1353, #1053) as separate stories. No article has run the gap arithmetic: gap vs. improvement (4.02x), gap per day (9.4), the annualized gap vs. NSC's own decomposition of the difference (3,420 vs. a 1,700–2,900 ceiling), and the stability of the gap across halves (1,671 → 1,710). The story is not that one counter is wrong. It is that the most-quoted safety statistic in America has two answers, and the distance between the answers is bigger than the news.

No queue title, slug, or note mentions the 1,710 gap, the two-counters comparison, or NSC's methodology page. No prior article cites injuryfacts.nsc.org/comparison-of-nsc-and-nhtsa-estimates. ## Verified facts (3+ primary sources)

1. **NHTSA early estimate, H1 2026 (Crash Stats DOT HS 813 875, November 2026, released November 10):** An estimated 16,750 people died in motor-vehicle traffic crashes in January–June 2026, down about 2.5% from the 17,175 projected for H1 2025. VMT rose about 12.9 billion miles (+0.8%). Fatality rate 1.02 per 100 million VMT, down from 1.05, the second-lowest first-half rate on record (1.01 in H1 2014). Quarterly split: Q1 7,715 (−5.0%), Q2 9,035 (−0.2%). Monthly: every month fell year-over-year except May and June, which rose. 8 of 10 NHTSA regions projected decreases; 35 states and Puerto Rico projected decreases, 15 states and DC projected increases. Estimates to be refined when the 9-month projections arrive in late December and when FARS files land.
2. **NSC preliminary estimate, H1 2026 (September 9, 2026):** An estimated 18,460 lives lost in motor-vehicle crashes in the first six months of 2026, down 2% from the revised 18,846 for H1 2025. Mileage up 0.8%. Estimated mileage death rate 1.12 per 100 million VMT, down 3.4% from 2025 and down 10.4% from 2024. Thirteen states fell more than 10% (Hawaii −30%, Illinois −24%, West Virginia −24%). NSC notes estimates are "subject to slight fluctuations as data matures."
3. **Why the two counters differ (NSC, Injury Facts comparison page; NSC press release):** NSC's preliminary estimates count deaths occurring within 100 days of the crash, on public *and* private roadways (parking lots, driveways), using National Center for Health Statistics data. NSC's final counts use a one-year window and NCHS death-certificate data minus intentional fatalities. NHTSA's FARS counts only traffic fatalities — crashes on public roads — in which someone died within 30 days of the crash. NSC's comparison page quantifies the two known gaps: FARS omits 800–1,000 motor-vehicle-related deaths a year that occur more than 30 days after the crash, and omits 900–1,900 nontraffic deaths a year. Combined published range: 1,700–2,900 deaths a year.
4. **The gap is structural, not a one-off (NSC Injury Facts preliminary-estimates page):** The most recent NCHS final estimate shows 20,278 deaths in the first half of 2024, against NHTSA's 18,681 for the same half — a gap of 1,597. NCHS provisional estimates show 18,846 deaths in H1 2025, matching NSC's revised H1 2025 figure. H1 2025 gap: 18,846 − 17,175 = 1,671. H1 2026 gap: 18,460 − 16,750 = 1,710. The gap moved by 39 deaths while both counters fell by hundreds.
5. **The 30-day rule's own defense (Streetsblog USA, June 24, 2024):** NHTSA says an estimated 98% of people who die in crashes succumb within 30 days, and cites the need to "maintain uniformity for those who report, analyze, classify, and otherwise use traffic crash data." The Center for Auto Safety's "Missing in FARS" campaign has documented for 15 years that NSC historically counts about 2,000 more motor-vehicle deaths a year than FARS, largely from non-traffic incidents and deaths beyond 30 days.

## Original contribution: original math

All calculations from the figures above:

- **The gap:** 18,460 − 16,750 = **1,710 deaths**, or **10.2%** of NHTSA's count. NSC's toll is a tenth higher for the identical six months.
- **Gap vs. improvement:** NHTSA's celebrated improvement is 17,175 − 16,750 = 425 deaths. 1,710 ÷ 425 = **4.02x**. The disagreement between the counters is four times the decline being reported as news.
- **Gap per day:** 1,710 ÷ 181 days (Jan 1–Jun 30) = **9.4 deaths a day** that one counter counts and the other does not. The improvement works out to 425 ÷ 181 = **2.3 a day**. Four phantom funerals a day for every one life actually saved, on the counters' own arithmetic.
- **Rate gap:** 1.12 − 1.02 = **0.10 deaths per 100M VMT**; NSC's rate runs 9.8% above NHTSA's. Both rates are "the" fatality rate, depending on which press release you read.
- **Annualized gap vs. NSC's own decomposition:** 1,710 × 2 = **3,420 a year**, against NSC's published components of 800–1,000 (late deaths) + 900–1,900 (nontraffic) = **1,700–2,900 a year**. The half-year gap annualizes about 520 deaths *above* the top of NSC's own explanation. Caveat, stated in the article: the preliminary NSC estimate uses a 100-day window, narrower than the one-year final window the decomposition describes, which should make the late-death component *smaller*, not larger — so the overshoot is a genuine open question, not a rounding artifact. Either the preliminary estimate runs hot, or a third difference nobody has itemized is in the mix.
- **Stability check:** H1 2024 gap (NCHS final vs. NHTSA) 1,597; H1 2025 gap 1,671; H1 2026 gap 1,710. Three halves, three gaps, all within 113 of each other. The fog is not noise. It is a fixed feature of the system.
- **What both agree on:** direction. NSC −2%, NHTSA −2.5%; both put VMT at +0.8%. The trend survives the disagreement. The level does not.

## Strongest counterargument
The gap is not a scandal and not an error. Both counters publish their definitions openly, both are estimates subject to revision, and the difference is definitional: different windows (30 days vs. 100 days vs. one year), different territory (public roads vs. anywhere a car can kill you). NHTSA's 30-day rule exists so every state reports the same way, and 98% of crash deaths happen inside it anyway. A policymaker who needs a consistent trend line is right to use NHTSA; a public-health researcher who wants every death is right to use NSC. The article's point is narrower and stands: when the headline improvement (425) is a quarter of the definitional gap (1,710), quoting either number to the digit is false precision, and celebrating the decline without naming the fog is incomplete reporting.

## Limitations
- Both figures are estimates, not counts. NHTSA's will be revised when the FARS 2025 Annual Reporting File and 2026 ARF arrive; NSC's preliminary will move when NCHS final mortality data lands (about a year lag). The 1,710 gap is a snapshot of two preliminary numbers, not a final census.
- NSC's preliminary uses a 100-day window; its final uses one year. The H1 2026 NSC figure could rise slightly on finalization as deaths between day 101 and day 365 are added, which would widen the gap further, not narrow it.
- Annualizing a six-month gap assumes the second half mirrors the first. H2 2026 could differ; the 9-month projections arrive in late December.
- No state-level head-to-head is possible from the published releases: NSC publishes state estimates, NHTSA publishes state estimates, but the two use different allocation methods and the releases do not line up state by state.
- The 800–1,000 and 900–1,900 component ranges come from NSC's comparison page and are annual, approximate, and undated; treating them as exact bounds would overstate the puzzle.

## Actionable insights
- Never quote a single traffic-death toll as gospel. If a politician, a press release, or a news story says "16,750 people died," ask which counter. The other official answer is 18,460.
- If you work in safety policy, pick your counter on purpose: NHTSA for trend consistency across years and states, NSC (or NCHS) for the fullest count of lives lost.
- The 30-day rule has a human consequence: if a crash victim survives more than 30 days in a hospital and then dies, the federal count does not include them. Families should know the official toll excludes those deaths.
- Watch the December 9-month projections: if Q3 repeats Q2's near-flat −0.2%, the "second-lowest rate ever" headline will rest on a first quarter that is already nine months old.
