The Gap

A 14,000-Pound Truck That Can't See a Motorcycle

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
Monday, November 8, 2027

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds after a forward-collision sensor decides something is in the way. On October 8, IIHS published its first-ever crash-avoidance ratings for commercial vehicles, and for most of America's biggest trucks the answer is: nothing happens. Three of the four Class 3 pickups tested, the Silverado 3500HD, the F-350, and the Sierra 3500, earned a Poor rating for vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention, the lowest score IIHS gives.[1] The fourth, the Ram 3500, managed only Marginal.

IIHS ran the same protocol it uses on passenger cars: passenger-car target, motorcycle target, semitrailer, multiple speeds, warning plus automatic braking, and motorcycles broke the systems. Several issued no warning and did not slow at all in most motorcycle trials; others braked well but warned too late to matter.[1] A sensor that sees the obstacle and stays silent is arguably worse than a blind one, though at least the blind one isn't pretending.

Then came the children: IIHS rolls a child-sized dummy across the road in daylight, and several trucks detected the child at a distance, then lost it as they closed in. IIHS points at packaging, not software: the forward sensors sit near the rearview mirror, and on a truck with a hood like a billboard, the detection cone's lower edge climbs above a child's head height as the distance shrinks. The child drives straight under the sensor's gaze, through a blind wedge that grows wider with every foot the truck closes, which is the kind of failure mode you catch in an afternoon with a tape measure and a step ladder if anyone had bothered to look. Somebody ported a passenger-car sensor layout onto a vehicle two feet taller and never re-aimed the geometry. Somebody drew that bracket, chose that mounting point, signed the drawing, and moved on to the next program milestone, and that signature is the whole story of how a child becomes invisible to a fourteen-thousand-pound machine.

Now the physics, because physics does not negotiate. In one 43-mph trial, the F-350 failed to reduce speed at all against the passenger-car target. It avoided the same target at 31 mph; twelve miles per hour faster, and the system contributed nothing.[2] Run the kinetic energy: half m v squared. A 14,000-pound truck at 43 mph carries roughly 1.17 megajoules. A 3,000-pound car at the same speed carries about 250 kilojoules. Do the division. The truck brings 4.7 times the destructive energy to the impact, and it is the truck IIHS just rated Poor. Sit with that. Call it the inverse safety law: the more energy a vehicle carries into a crash, the worse its electronic eyes. Nobody designed it that way; it emerged from where the R&D money goes, which is passenger cars, because that is where the volume lives, where the ratings pressure bites, and where a five-star badge moves metal off the lot.

IIHS launched its commercial-vehicle program in June, starting with driver protection: airbags, belts, reminders. Read that again slowly: in 2026, the institute had to begin checking whether work trucks even have airbags, because many federal passenger-vehicle standards, airbag requirements included, simply do not apply to delivery vans, box trucks, tractor-trailers, and larger work pickups.[1] The automatic-braking mandate trajectory is a passenger-car story. These trucks live in a different regulatory county, one where the airbag rule never arrived and the automatic-braking mandate is still a rumor, which leaves IIHS ratings as the only force currently pushing them anywhere at all.

Bright spots exist, thin as they are: the Sprinter 2500 earned the only Good in vehicle-to-vehicle prevention, while the BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 earned Good for pedestrians.[1] And the detail that curdles it: GM ended BrightDrop production in October 2025, per secondary reporting.[5] The van that sees pedestrians best is already dead. Read that twice. Meanwhile the Express 2500 and Savana 2500, ancient platforms still sold new, have no automatic emergency braking at all, only optional forward collision warning. Poor and Poor.

Full strength for the other side, because it deserves it. None of this makes a contractor's F-350 a death machine. Commercial drivers are often trained professionals, and work trucks spend their lives at job-site speeds where automatic braking is a footnote. Stopping 14,000 pounds is a physics problem no software update fully solves. The motorcycle miss is genuinely hard perception physics: small radar cross-section, lateral motion, clutter. The Express and Savana are end-of-life platforms, and rating them is like crash-testing a fax machine, which is fair criticism. But the child dummy did not disappear because of physics; it disappeared because of a bracket, and that one is on the drawing board, not in the laws of motion.

What this does not prove matters too: IIHS trials measure behavior under a controlled protocol, not real-world crash or injury rates, and the release offers no exposure denominator, no crashes per mile to anchor the ratings to. Scores attach to tested model years and trims, and sensor suites vary by options package, so the truck on the dealer lot with the base work-truck spec sheet may carry older or decontented hardware relative to what IIHS evaluated. A Poor rating is poor test performance under a fixed protocol, not a proven defect in your driveway, and the field failure rates that would settle the argument are unknown and will stay unknown until somebody funds the exposure study.

If you buy trucks for a living, the IIHS ratings page just became a procurement tool: spec the Sprinter 2500 for vehicle-to-vehicle performance, or the BrightDrop and Rivian vans where pedestrian exposure is high. Do not put new Express or Savana 2500s on downtown delivery routes; they have no automatic braking, full stop. Watch the 2027 ratings, when IIHS adds headlights and rear automatic braking for Class 3 and starts phasing in Class 4 through 6.[3] The inverse safety law holds only until somebody decides to spend money on it.
