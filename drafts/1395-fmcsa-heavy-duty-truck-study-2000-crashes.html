America Has Been Setting Truck Safety Policy Off a 2003 Study. The Replacement Arrives in 2030.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 4, 2026

Federal truck safety policy has been running on fieldwork collected before the iPhone existed. On November 3, 2026, the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration finally funded the replacement: up to $7 million for state and local agencies to gather detailed data on about 2,000 fatal heavy-truck crashes, with a final report and a public database due in 2030.[1] Until then, every rule about the trucks that kill the most people on American highways leans on a study whose investigators finished their fieldwork in 2003.

2,000
Fatal heavy-truck crashes FMCSA will investigate in detail for its first truck crash-causation study since 2001-2003 fieldwork

That predecessor, the Large Truck Crash Causation Study, examined 967 crashes at 24 sites in 17 states between 2001 and 2003, collecting up to 1,000 data elements per crash.[2] Its report reached Congress in March 2006, and its headline finding still shapes the debate: driver action or inaction was the critical reason in 88 percent of crashes.[5] Meanwhile the problem it measured got worse. Fatal crashes involving large trucks and buses rose 26.4 percent from 2016 to 2022.[3]

New study, bigger net. Investigators will cover Class 7 and Class 8 trucks, the rigs rated at 26,001 pounds or more, which account for roughly 70 percent of large trucks in fatal crashes.[1] Fieldwork starts in 2027 after a pilot, runs two years across about 30 states, and pairs crash reconstruction with interviews: the Bureau of Transportation Statistics planned five hour-long interviews per crash.[3] FMCSA's 2025 plan even oversampled on purpose, targeting 3,333 crashes to net 2,000 usable cases.[4]

Skeptics have a point, and it is worth stating plainly. Last study cost $18 million over five years and mostly confirmed what everyone already believed, which is why safety advocates called it underwhelming at the time.[5] A bigger sample does not guarantee a more surprising answer, and answers due in 2030 do nothing for anyone driving next to a semi in 2027. Still, depth is the point: 2,000 crashes investigated in interview-level detail beats counting crashes from police reports alone, and FMCSA estimated about 141,000 large trucks were involved in fatal and injury crashes during the old study's period.[2] Watch one date before any of that matters: December 28, 2026, when state applications for this one-time funding close. If too few states apply, the nationally representative sample shrinks first. For everyone else, the actionable part is boring and real: the next decade of truck rules will be argued from this dataset, so carriers, insurers, and safety groups that want a say should be pushing their states to apply now, not complaining about the report in 2030.
