## Story in one line
On June 12, 2026, a Broward County jury awarded $603 million to the family of Destiny Marie Byassee, 22, a mother of two killed in a minor crash when the counterfeit airbag in her 2020 Chevrolet Malibu exploded and severed her throat — and the company the jury held liable, China's DTN, filed a denial and then did not bother to show up for its own trial.

## Key verified facts
- **The verdict:** June 12, 2026, Broward County, Florida. $243M compensatory + $360M punitive = $603M, against DTN only as reported. DTN retained counsel, filed a response denying the allegations, then failed to attend the trial.
- **The victim:** Destiny Marie Byassee, 22, mother of two young children. Survived by her children, sister, and grandmother.
- **The crash:** June 11, 2023, minor frontal collision in a 2020 Chevrolet Malibu. She was belted. The counterfeit driver airbag exploded instead of inflating; a metal fragment severed her throat; she bled to death. NHTSA's language for these crashes: "otherwise survivable."
- **The car before she bought it:** September 2022 collision (as an Enterprise rental) deployed the driver airbag and the seat-belt pretensioner. The suit says the Malibu should have been totaled, salvage-titled, and removed from service. Instead: Enterprise → Manheim auction → DriveTime → Jumbo Automotive "repair" → sold to Byassee.
- **The repair:** Jumbo allegedly installed counterfeit DTN airbag components and, instead of replacing the deployed pretensioner, cut its wires. Two safety systems disabled in one repair visit.
- **The company:** DTN says it does not do business in the United States and its products are prohibited from sale there; it told NHTSA the inflators may be counterfeits of its own products. NHTSA's position (per #1233): it does not matter who made them — every DTN60DB inflator known to have been in a crash has ruptured.
- **The wider litigation:** Morgan & Morgan filed three wrongful-death suits in three states and was investigating at least three more fatality cases as of June 9, 2026. Byassee's is the first to reach a verdict.
- **The body count since:** Byassee died June 2023, before NHTSA opened its DTN investigation (October 2025) and before the April 2026 ban. The count now stands at 11 deaths in 14 crashes.

## Original contribution (Rex's math and synthesis)
1. **Five companies, five chances to stop it.** The suit's chain of custody is the story no DTN piece has told: Enterprise (should have totaled the car), Manheim (auctioned a wrecked car with deployed safety equipment), DriveTime (sold it to the public), Jumbo (installed the fake bag and cut the pretensioner wires). Each handoff was a point where someone could have asked what was behind the steering wheel. Nobody did. The counterfeit airbag is the proximate cause; the used-car supply chain is the system that delivered it to a 22-year-old mother.
2. **$603 million against an empty chair.** DTN denied the allegations in writing, then did not show up. The jury's number is a message to an absent defendant that says it never sold a part in America. Morgan & Morgan says collectible assets exist and it will pursue them; whether a dollar ever reaches Byassee's children is unreported. A verdict is not a payment, and the gap between the two is the real measure of what civil justice can do to a Chinese manufacturer that denies the parts are even its own.
3. **Two dead safety systems, one repair.** The pretensioner detail matters: Byassee was wearing her seat belt, and the belt's pretensioner — the device that yanks the belt tight in the first milliseconds of a crash — had its wires cut instead of being replaced. The fake airbag killed her; the cut pretensioner meant the one system that could have held her back from the exploding bag was not working either. One repair visit disabled both.
4. **She died before anyone knew the inflators existed.** Byassee's crash was June 11, 2023. NHTSA did not open its DTN investigation until October 2025 — more than two years later. She is counted among the 11 DTN deaths only in retrospect. The first crash in NHTSA's file is May 30, 2023, twelve days before hers. For two years, the people who knew these inflators were killing drivers were the families.

## Limitations
- The $603M figure and trial account come from plaintiff's counsel (Morgan & Morgan) as reported by trade press; I have not read the verdict form or docket. DTN's side is its filed denial and its public statement; it did not present a case at trial.
- The verdict as reported is against DTN. The status of the claims against Enterprise, Manheim, DriveTime, and Jumbo — pending, settled, dismissed — is not reported in any source found. Do not claim they were found liable.
- Whether any of the $603M has been or will be collected is unknown. Morgan & Morgan's "collectible assets" claim is the firm's assertion, not a finding.
- FARS Malibu figures aggregate all model years and measure the nameplate's crash record, not this car's risk; they frame the story (an ordinary sedan, a survivable crash), they do not measure the counterfeit-inflator risk.
- The June 2023 crash predates NHTSA's DTN investigation; the attribution of Byassee's death to a DTN inflator rests on the lawsuit's allegations and the jury's verdict, not on a NHTSA crash listing I have independently verified.

## Strongest counterargument
The honest case against this story: the verdict proves less than the number suggests. DTN did not attend trial, so the jury heard one side. DTN says the inflators are counterfeits of its products and that it never sold into the US — if true, the jury held the wrong company liable for someone else's crime, and the $603M is uncollectable theater that punishes a name on a label rather than the importer who brought the parts in and the shop that installed them. The other four defendants, the ones with US assets and US addresses, have not been found liable in any reporting. The counter is that the jury heard DTN's written denial — the company chose to file a response and then not defend it — and that NHTSA's own finding is that it does not matter who made the inflators, because every one that has been in a crash has ruptured; the name on the part is DTN's either way. And the verdict's value may not be the money: it is the first time a jury has priced a counterfeit-airbag death, and Morgan & Morgan has two more suits already filed.

## Actionable takeaways
- If you bought a used car that was in a prior crash where an airbag deployed, have the airbag inspected by a franchised dealer — not the shop that sold you the car. NHTSA's guidance: any car with a post-2020 airbag deployment repaired outside a dealership should be inspected.
- A VIN recall lookup cannot detect a counterfeit replacement part. These inflators were never factory equipment and are not tied to any VIN. The only detection is a history report showing an airbag deployment plus non-dealer repair, followed by a physical inspection — the inflators are marked DTN60DB.
- Check the title history before buying a used car: a salvage or rebuilt title plus a fresh steering wheel is the pattern in the DTN deaths. Most of the crashed cars had salvage or rebuilt titles.
- If a shop offers you a cheap replacement airbag, or your car comes back from a repair with a pretensioner warning light on, stop. A deployed pretensioner must be replaced, never bypassed — cut wires are a tell that the repair was not a repair.
