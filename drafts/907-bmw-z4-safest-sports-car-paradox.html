The Gap

The Safest Sports Car in America Is a BMW Roadster. It Is Safer Per Mile Than a Camry.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 15, 2026

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. Across a decade of federal crash data, the BMW Z4, a two-seat roadster with no roof and nothing to prove, killed at a rate of 0.46 deaths per 100 million miles driven.[1] Meanwhile the Toyota Camry, the default answer to every nervous parent's car question, killed at 2.03. Somebody's intuition about which shape is safe just filed for divorce.

How much deadlier per mile a Ford Mustang is than a BMW Z4, same vehicle class, same federal dataset

It gets worse for the conventional wisdom. Civics sit at 2.25 deaths per 100M miles and Accords at 3.07, which means every one of America's sensible sedans out-kills the little Bavarian convertible per mile, a sentence that should bother you slightly more each time you reread it. You could drive a Z4 four times as far as a Camry, through the same intersections in the same weather, and still be playing the same odds, which is the kind of sentence that gets a person uninvited from dinner parties.

Read that again.

Inside the sports car class, the spread is obscene, the kind of number that makes you check whether somebody misplaced a decimal point before you check anything else. Anchoring the bottom: the Hyundai Veloster at 8.54 deaths per 100M VMT, which makes it 18.6 times deadlier per mile than the Z4. Ford's Mustang, 2,739 deaths over ten years, lands at 6.02. Thirteen to one: same vehicle class, same federal dataset, different universe. The Corvette (1.52) and the Camaro (3.44) fill out the middle, which is to say even the mullet wagon doubles the roadster. Ouch.

What is the Z4 doing right? Probably nothing mechanical, which is precisely the point. Z4 buyers skew older, wealthier, and seasonal, the kind of people whose second car sleeps in a garage from November to April. Garage-kept roadsters do not rack up midnight miles in the rain, and their owners are not the demographic filling out the 2 a.m. single-vehicle crash reports that keep the FARS coders employed. Mustang buyers skew young, and 21.9 percent of Mustang drivers in fatal crashes tested positive for alcohol or drugs.[1] IIHS said the quiet part out loud in July: sports cars post high death rates because of how they are driven.[2] Same phenomenon in reverse here. It is not a safe car; it is a car driven by safe drivers, which is a distinction with a difference, because one of those things you can buy and the other you have to be.

Steel yourself for the caveats, because there are several: sixteen Z4 deaths is a thin sample and the mileage estimates for low-volume roadsters carry wide error bars; FARS only counts fatal crashes, so a car that maims gracefully looks identical to one that kills cleanly; and nothing in this dataset measures driver age, which means the demographics argument is inference wearing a lab coat. Even at the pessimistic edge of the Z4's confidence interval, though, it stays well clear of the Camry, and the inversion survives the error bars.

What do you actually do with this? You stop shopping by silhouette and start shopping by driver pool. A roadster shape tells you nothing, and a sedan shape tells you less, and the sooner car shoppers internalize that sheet metal is a fashion statement rather than a safety rating, the fewer surprises the actuarial tables will hold for them. If you want a sports car, the badge's typical driver pool predicts your risk better than the spec sheet ever will. Check the IIHS driver death rates, refreshed this July, before you sign anything.[2] And if you are handing a Mustang to a nineteen-year-old, understand you are handing him the thirteen-x end of the stick.
