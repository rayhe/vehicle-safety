## Proposed angle
The Commercial Vehicle Safety Alliance ran its annual Operation Safe Driver Week July 12-18, 2026, across 36 states, seven Canadian provinces and Mexico, with the stated focus on reckless, careless and dangerous driving. Results released October 5, 2026: officers interacted with 8,083 drivers and issued 2,604 citations and 3,968 warnings. The focus offense produced 33 citations, 1.3 percent of the total. Speeding, which was not the focus, produced 691. And the enforcement split tells a second story: commercial drivers got warnings, passenger-car drivers got tickets, even though truckers were the overwhelming majority of stops.

## The numbers (primary source: CVSA release, Oct 5, 2026)
1. **Scale:** 8,083 drivers interacted with; 3,968 warnings; 2,604 tickets/citations. 36 US states, 7 Canadian provinces, Mexico. Campaign July 12-18, 2026.
2. **By driver type:** CMV drivers: 3,409 warnings, 1,956 citations. Passenger vehicle drivers: 559 warnings, 648 citations.
3. **Speeding (top violation):** 1,003 warnings, 691 citations. CMV: 772 warnings, 342 citations. PV: 231 warnings, 349 citations.
4. **Seat belt:** 292 warnings, 311 citations. CMV: 267 warnings, 270 citations. PV: 25 warnings, 41 citations.
5. **Texting/handheld:** 135 warnings, 181 citations. CMV: 106 warnings, 136 citations. PV: 29 warnings, 45 citations.
6. **Drugs/alcohol possession, use or impairment:** 17 warnings, 26 citations. CMV: 12 warnings, 22 citations. PV: 5 warnings, 4 citations.
7. **Reckless, careless and dangerous driving (the week's stated focus):** 83 warnings, 33 citations. CMV: 50 warnings, 22 citations. PV: 33 warnings, 11 citations.
8. **Failure to obey traffic control device:** 676 warnings, 344 citations. CMV: 652 warnings, 319 citations. PV: 24 warnings, 25 citations.
9. **Other violations (CVSA table):** failure to yield 13 warnings/9 citations; following too closely 65/28; improper lane change 102/45; improper passing 43/25; improper turn 15/3; CMV ill/fatigued 9/6; radar/laser detector 16/10; railroad grade crossing 1/2.
10. **The no-paperwork third:** more than 2,880 contacts, over a third of the week's total, ended with neither a citation nor a warning. Officers used the stop to talk. CVSA also distributed 64,450 postcards and ran a Paramount/CBS digital campaign with 16.4 million impressions. 974 human trafficking awareness activities.
11. **The stakes (CVSA citing NHTSA):** speeding contributed to 11,288 US traffic deaths in 2024, 29 percent of all fatalities. Distracted driving killed 3,208 in 2024 (8 percent). Alcohol-impaired driving killed 11,904 in 2024. 36,640 total US traffic deaths in 2025.

## Original calculations (show the math in the article)
12. **The focus flopped:** reckless/careless/dangerous citations were 33 of 2,604 total citations = 1.27 percent. Its warnings were 83 of 3,968 = 2.09 percent. The week's named target produced about one citation in every 79 written. Speeding produced 691 citations = 26.5 percent of all citations, more than twenty times the focus offense.
13. **Truckers get warned, car drivers get ticketed:** CMV enforcement actions were 3,409 warnings + 1,956 citations = 5,365; warnings were 63.5 percent of them. PV actions were 559 + 648 = 1,207; citations were 53.7 percent of them. A commercial driver stopped during the week was more likely to leave with a warning; a passenger driver was more likely to leave with a ticket.
14. **Passenger cars out-sped the trucks:** PV drivers received 349 of 691 speeding citations = 50.5 percent, despite accounting for only 1,207 of 6,572 total enforcement actions = 18.4 percent. The week aimed at commercial drivers caught more speeders in passenger cars than in trucks (349 vs 342).
15. **Seat belts are a trucker problem in this data:** CMV drivers received 270 of 311 seat-belt citations = 86.8 percent. Traffic-control-device warnings were even more lopsided: 652 of 676 = 96.4 percent CMV.
16. **The impairment footnote:** drugs/alcohol produced 26 citations = 1.0 percent of all citations, against a national toll of 11,904 alcohol-impaired deaths in 2024. A week of roadside enforcement barely touched the behavior behind roughly a third of US road deaths. Say plainly that a one-week blitz cannot catch impairment the way it catches speeding; do not present 26 as evidence impairment is rare.

## FARS context (original cross-tab)
17. **FARS 2014-2023, heavy pickups and vans:** the workhorse vehicles in this story are not in the FARS model file as commercial trucks, but their passenger-vehicle cousins are. Ford F-350: 164 deaths, rate 0.15 per 100M VMT, among the lowest in the file. Ford E-350 van: 776 deaths, rate 2.51, roughly seventeen times the F-350's rate. Same manufacturer, same weight class family, wildly different outcomes. The E-350's 776 deaths over ten years are the relevant scale: the vans CVSA stops are the vehicles ordinary drivers share the road with, and the van's fatality rate says the risk is not theoretical.
18. **Do not overclaim the FARS link:** FARS covers fatal crashes, not enforcement stops, and the E-350 in FARS is mostly passenger and shuttle use, not the CMV fleet CVSA inspects. Use it as scale, not as proof about CMV drivers.

## Limitations (must appear in the article)
- One week of targeted enforcement is not a representative sample of American driving. Officers choose where to stand and whom to stop; the numbers describe the blitz, not the roads.
- Warnings vs citations reflect officer discretion and jurisdiction policy, not just driver behavior. Some states warn where others ticket.
- The focus-offense count is low partly because reckless/careless/dangerous driving is hard to prove roadside and easy to write up as speeding instead. A speeder doing 30 over is often also driving recklessly; the officer picks the charge that sticks.
- CVSA's counts cover the US, Canada and Mexico combined; the NHTSA death tolls are US-only. Do not divide one by the other as if they share a denominator.
- The 2,880 no-paperwork contacts are CVSA's "more than" figure, not an exact count.

## Strongest counterargument (must appear at full strength)
The honest reply is that this article mistakes a press release for a finding. Operation Safe Driver Week is a deterrence and outreach exercise, not a study: 8,083 stops in a week when Americans drive roughly 60 billion miles is a rounding error, the 33 reckless-driving citations say more about how hard the charge is to prove than about how rarely it happens, and the warning/citation split says more about commercial-enforcement culture (educate the professional, ticket the amateur) than about who drives worse. The speeding numbers are the least surprising fact in road safety: speeding is the most common violation because it is the easiest to measure with a radar gun. Say all of that, then answer it: the article's claim is not that the blitz measured American driving. The claim is that the program's own scorecard shows its focus missing by a factor of twenty, its warnings landing on professionals and its tickets on amateurs, and its impairment enforcement touching 26 drivers in a week when alcohol kills 33 people a day. Those are the program's numbers, and they are worth reading as written.

## What NOT to claim
- That reckless driving is rare because only 33 citations were written. The charge is hard to prove; speeding is the easier citation for the same behavior.
- That truckers drive better or worse than car drivers. The stop population is selected, not random.
- That the 26 drug/alcohol citations mean impairment is a 1 percent problem. It means roadside blitzes rarely catch it.
- That CVSA's 8,083 stops represent North American driving. They represent one enforcement week.
- Any state-level breakdown. CVSA did not publish one.

## Actionable takeaways (HARD GATE — must include)
1. If you drive a passenger car near trucks, the blitz data says you are the one getting the speeding ticket: PV drivers took half the speeding citations on 18 percent of the stops. Slow down around CMV enforcement; the radar is pointed at you too.
2. Seat-belt enforcement during the week was overwhelmingly aimed at commercial drivers, but the belt is what saves car drivers: 14,955 lives saved by belts in 2017 per NHTSA, 2,549 more could have been saved. Buckle up on every trip, including the short ones.
3. A warning is not a pass: CVSA's 3,968 warnings are logged contacts. If you are a commercial driver, warnings accumulate in enforcement records; treat one as seriously as a ticket.
4. Check whether your state participated and what its focus was: 36 states took part, which means 14 did not. Enforcement intensity varies by state and by week.
