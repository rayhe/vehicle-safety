The Deadliest Car in America Aces Its Crash Tests

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 24, 2026

It passed every test in the lab. The Kia Rio failed the one on the road. In July, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety published its latest driver death rates, and the cheapest car in America sits at the top of the list: 170 driver deaths per million registered vehicle years, the worst figure recorded for any vehicle on the road.[1]

170
Driver deaths per million registered vehicle years for the Kia Rio, 2021-2024 — the highest of any vehicle evaluated, 4.6x the all-vehicle average of 37.

Now for the contradiction: its own institute crash-tested the Rio and handed it Good ratings in the moderate overlap, the driver-side small overlap, and the side impact test.[2] Good, Good, Good. In the lab, the car protects you; the morgue says otherwise.

Crash tests are graded on a curve: every IIHS rating compares a vehicle against its own weight class, which is a polite way of saying the Rio only had to beat other 2,700-pound runabouts. Out on real asphalt nobody curves grades: the Rio shares the road with Tahoes, F-150s, and Ram 3500s, and in those meetings the lighter car always loses. IIHS president David Harkey put it plainly: even the safest small cars don't fare well in crashes with large pickups and heavy SUVs.[1]

Blame the drivers if you like. Some of it sticks: 21.5 percent of Rio drivers in fatal crashes were impaired, above the national average.[3] But the IIHS adjusts its death rates for driver age and gender, and the Rio still finished first. Strip out the demographics and the little Kia remains the deadliest thing on four wheels with a factory warranty.

Then there is what the car doesn't have. On the 2023 Rio, forward collision warning was optional, automatic emergency braking was optional, and blind-spot monitoring wasn't offered at any price.[4] Crash tests measure how well a car protects you during the crash. Nothing on the window sticker measured whether the car could help you avoid it. At $14,450, the Rio was the cheapest new car sold in America that year, and the data suggests the discount came with a body count.

A fair detective reads the alibi too: IIHS counts deaths per registered vehicle year, not per mile driven, and the Rio is a low-mileage commuter car, which inflates per-year figures against per-mile ones. By the federal FARS yardstick of deaths per 100 million miles, the Rio's 1.07 looks merely bad, and the Chevy Spark's 1.28 looks worse.[3] Likely both are true: the Rio is genuinely dangerous for its drivers, and the number-one ranking is partly an artifact of the denominator. Denominators have ended more reputations than airbags ever saved.

What this means for you: if you drive a 2018-2023 Rio, know that its Good ratings are real but narrow: the structure holds up in the crashes it was designed for. Danger lives in the mismatch, so drive like the smallest thing on the road, because it is. If you're shopping used subcompacts, the math favors spending a little more: a Forte or Elantra with standard automatic emergency braking buys the crash-avoidance tech the Rio made you pay extra to skip.
