Seven Cars Entered the IIHS Crash Lab. Only Two Left With an Award

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 19, 2026

Two out of seven: that is the scorecard the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety handed down this month after crash-testing seven 2026 and 2027 models, and the pass list should end the idea that a luxury badge is a safety guarantee. The winners are a Kia Telluride and a Tesla Model Y.[1] BMW, Lexus, Toyota, and GMC sent five cars into the same lab. None of them made the award list.

of tested models earned IIHS's highest award, Top Safety Pick+ (2 of 7)

Here is the detail that should stop a Telluride buyer mid-signature. Kia's 2027 Telluride carries its Top Safety Pick+ rating only on vehicles built after June 2026, because Kia quietly upgraded the headlights from Marginal to Good mid-model-year and IIHS drew its line at the fix.[1][4] Two Tellurides can sit on the same dealer lot, same model year, same sticker price. One is among the safest vehicles on sale. The other does not appear on the award list at all.

These failures are not random. Four of the five misses trace back to the same two tests: the updated moderate-overlap front crash test and the new front crash-prevention standard.[1] In the updated moderate-overlap test, the dummy in the second row is now the one most likely to absorb the consequences, and that is exactly where the Lexus IS failed: rear-seat passengers fared badly enough to sink its score.[1] Crash prevention is the nastier gate. BMW's i4 rated Poor at vehicle-to-vehicle automatic emergency braking.[1] Poor does not mean the system is a little slow. It means the technology that is supposed to stop the car before it hits the car ahead cannot be trusted at higher approach speeds.

Notice what did not fail. Kia's $36,000 Telluride aced every crash test and both crash-prevention evaluations.[1][4] Tesla's Model Y did the same, taking the plus rating while carrying a Marginal seat-belt reminder, because reminder quality is not a TSP+ criterion and IIHS ranks preventing the crash above nagging you about the belt,[2] and badges are marketing while ratings are receipts.

So the next time a salesperson leans on the price to imply the safety, remember this round: a BMW with an MSRP north of $55,000 flunked automatic braking while the Kia parked next to it passed everything.[1] When you buy the badge you are not buying safety; you are buying leather.

Do three things before you sign. If you are shopping the 2027 Telluride, photograph the build-date sticker on the driver's door jamb and buy a truck built after June 2026. If you are cross-shopping any of the five misses, look up the current ratings at iihs.org/ratings before you negotiate, because the moderate-overlap and crash-prevention columns are where these cars lose.[5] And when the brochure says the price reflects the engineering, smile and check the receipt yourself.

What this article did not prove: all seven vehicles meet federal safety standards; missing an IIHS award is not the same as failing FMVSS, and IIHS ratings grade beyond the legal minimum. FARS 2014–2023 contains no fatality data for these 2026/2027 redesigns, so no fatality-rate cross-tabulation is possible; every vehicle-specific claim here traces to IIHS crash-test and crash-prevention ratings, not observed death rates. Unit counts for the 2027 Telluride build-date split are not publicly reported, so the affected share of early builds is unknown.

Strongest case against this article: the 2026 test bar is partly arbitrary, and a Marginal moderate-overlap rating can reflect rear-dummy kinematics under a tougher protocol rather than real-world injury risk that would show up in fatality data. BMW and Toyota would argue their cars are as safe as ever in the crashes that actually kill people, and the “Poor” labels read worse than they drive. That defense has a hole in it, though: the crash-prevention failures measure whether the car avoids the collision at all, and a Poor on vehicle-to-vehicle braking is the kind of failure that shows up in police reports, not just lab scores.
