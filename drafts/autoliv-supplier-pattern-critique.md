### 6. 🔬 Research Rigor — Original contribution, limitations, counterarguments, methodology

**Score: 9.0 / 10**

**Original contribution:** Clearly identified — the NHTSA recall database's automaker-centric indexing creates a structural blind spot for supplier-level patterns. This is a genuine, testable insight that readers can verify themselves (go to nhtsa.gov/recalls, search "Autoliv," get nothing). The Takata parallel is deployed as a historical precedent for the same architectural failure, not merely as a scare comparison.

**Limitations:** Stated explicitly and honestly:
- No fatalities reported (unlike Takata)
- Different failure modes may indicate isolated problems, not systemic rot
- Scale makes some recall volume statistically inevitable
- The disclaimer notes the 2.5M figure aggregates recalls with "distinct failure modes"

**Strongest counterargument:** Presented at full strength — a company producing hundreds of millions of components annually will have some recall rate; four different defects across ten years at that volume may be statistical noise. The article doesn't dismiss this; it lets it stand and then introduces the criminal record as additional context.

**Methodology transparency:** The 2.5M+ aggregation is clearly sourced (sum of four recalls across three automakers over 10 years). Revenue and market share figures are from the 2024 annual report. The article distinguishes between global recall numbers (Volvo 2.18M) and U.S. numbers where applicable.

**One improvement area:** The article could calculate what "expected" recall rate would look like for a company with 44% market share — e.g., if X% of all vehicle recalls industry-wide involve seat belts, what share would Autoliv statistically account for? This would let the reader judge whether 2.5M is anomalous or proportional.
