NHTSA Wants Driverless Cars to Glow Cyan. It Still Has No Rules for the Driving.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 28, 2026

Washington has picked a color for robot cars. It still has no rules for how they drive. NHTSA's draft update to FMVSS 108, the federal lighting standard, would let driverless cars wear steady cyan marker lamps, and the proposal reached the Federal Register on October 26, 2026, opening a 30-day comment period that closes in late November.[1][2]

0
Cyan lamps the draft actually requires on any driverless car

The spec is precise to the inch. Lamps burn steady, never flashing, and every lamp on the car switches on and off in unison. They light only while the automated system drives and no human is expected to watch the road, then switch off the moment supervision is required again, which limits them to true driverless operation rather than supervised driver-assist.[1][2] Placement runs one or two lamps at the front, the same on each side, and one or two at the rear, four to eight in total, each at least 15 inches off the ground.[1][2]

This color did not come from nowhere. Mercedes-Benz won the first American approvals for turquoise automated-driving marker lights in California and Nevada in December 2023, fitting them into the headlights, taillights, and mirror housings of DRIVE PILOT cars.[5] Federal filings credit Mercedes with a two-year, five-car California validation from 2023 to 2025, and the company picked turquoise because test studies rated it highly visible and hard to confuse with traffic or emergency lighting, consistent with industry practice SAE J3134.[4][5]

Now the catch. Fitting the lamps is optional. A driverless car with no cyan anywhere remains fully compliant, so two identical robotaxis could pass each other at night, one glowing and one dark, and both would satisfy the same rule.[1][2] A pedestrian who learns that cyan means robot will still meet compliant robot cars wearing nothing at all. Standardizing a message while making sending it voluntary is a strange way to build a safety signal.

Read the rest of the draft and the pattern sharpens. Physical turn-signal, hazard, and high-beam controls would be required only on vehicles with manual driving controls, and the draft drops the 12.8-volt test standard for integrated headlamps, following NHTSA's earlier decision to scrap the manual brake pedal rule for cars driven exclusively by an automated system.[1][2] Administrator Jonathan Morrison framed the package as easing "unnecessary regulatory burdens" while setting "clear performance standards for automated vehicle marker lamps."[1][3] Meanwhile the agency's Cybercab certification inquiry, AQ26002, stays open, more than 300 Cybercabs already carry passengers in Austin, and as of October 2026 no federal safety standard governs automated driving itself, according to analyst Sam Abuelsamid.[2][6]

The defense deserves a fair hearing. Standardizing a signal before mandating it lets early adopters converge on one color instead of fragmenting, and five validation cars look thin until you remember how few eligible cars exist. But that defense concedes the core point: a signal half the fleet may skip cannot carry the safety case being made for it. What you can do is concrete. File a comment before the window closes in late November if you want the lamps mandatory, and treat the street accordingly: cyan reliably means a robot is driving, while no cyan means nothing at all.
