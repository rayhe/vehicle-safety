The Gap

Half of Recalled Cars Never Get Fixed. The Other Half Fix Themselves at 2 AM.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
August 16, 2026

NHTSA logged 670 recall campaigns covering 28 million vehicles in 2025, and by February 2026, the overall recall completion rate had sunk to 50.3 percent, leaving an estimated 14 million recalled vehicles on American roads with known safety defects that nobody has touched.[1] That 50.3 percent sounds bad enough on its own, but it conceals a structural fracture that makes the real picture far uglier for anyone driving something built before the age of connected cars.

50.3%
Overall recall completion rate as of Feb. 2026, per NHTSA. The lowest figure in years.

Of those 670 campaigns, 119 targeted software and electronics defects across 8.19 million vehicles.[1] Many of those fixes travel over the air. In December 2023, Tesla pushed a recall patch to two million Model S, X, 3, and Y vehicles while their owners slept, updating Autopilot safeguards without a single dealer visit, a single appointment slot, a single replacement part pulled from a shelf.[2] BMW, Mercedes, Ford, and GM all ship OTA safety updates now, and for a connected vehicle that receives one of those overnight downloads, the effective completion rate approaches 100 percent because the owner doesn't need to do anything at all.

Then there are the remaining 551 campaigns. Powertrain bearing failures. Corroding airbag clocksprings. Overheating seat motors that melt wiring beneath your thigh. Suspension links that snap without warning at highway speed. Every one of these needs a human technician, a dealer bay, and a physical replacement part, and that part has a 44 percent chance of being manufactured outside the United States, where it now carries a 25 percent tariff.[3] OEM parts delivery averaged nine days in January 2026, nearly triple the three days that aftermarket alternatives require, according to PartsTrader's Spring 2026 collision industry trends report.[4] Post-tariff OEM parts inflation hit 4.3 percent while aftermarket stayed below one. S&P Global Mobility tallied $22.4 billion in new aftermarket parts duties, with 47 percent of U.S. parts imports originating in Mexico alone.[5]

So two tiers have emerged, and the gap between them is not a rounding error but a structural divergence in how safety defects get resolved. Tier one: newer, connected vehicles whose software defects vanish silently, often before the owner even learns a recall was issued. Tier two: older, disconnected vehicles whose mechanical defects persist for months or years because the component that would fix them costs more, arrives slower, and competes for allocation with every other repair order in the pipeline.

NHTSA's own age-stratified completion data draws the dividing line with brutal clarity. Vehicles under three years old, which are far more likely to have OTA capability and factory warranty coverage, reach 90 percent recall completion; vehicles aged 16 to 20 years, which almost never have OTA capability and almost always need physical parts, manage only 38 percent.[6] Between those poles, the falloff is steep and monotonic: 60 percent for ages 5 to 10, 52 percent for 11 to 15.

Run the intersection and the picture sharpens: 14 million unrepaired vehicles, overwhelmingly older and overwhelmingly parts-dependent, carrying defects whose fixes now cost 4.3 percent more and take three times longer to arrive than aftermarket alternatives. Meanwhile, a 2025 Tesla with a steering software glitch gets patched while charging in the garage, its recall letter already obsolete before it arrives in the mail. Same regulatory system, same recall database, same NHTSA letter format, but wildly different outcomes determined entirely by whether your defect lives in a microcontroller or a wiring harness.

NHTSA does not currently disaggregate completion rates by OTA versus physical repair, which means that clean 50.3 percent is averaging two populations that share almost nothing in common except a regulatory label. One of those populations is above 95 percent and climbing; the other is below 50 and sinking, weighed down by tariff-inflated parts costs, a nine-day supply chain, and the plain economics of an owner deciding a dealer visit isn't worth the afternoon off work. "Keep the dent and pay the rent," as PartsTrader's report put it[4] — except it isn't a dent but a fire-risk wiring harness or a suspension link designed to hold a two-ton vehicle to the road at 70 miles per hour.

What to do: Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. If your recall remedy says "software update" or "OTA," it may already be applied, so verify with your dealer or the vehicle's update log. If it says "dealer replacement" and you have been putting it off, know that the parts situation is deteriorating, not improving, and the longer you wait, the higher the chance your component sits behind a tariff wall or warehouse queue while you drive with the defect.
