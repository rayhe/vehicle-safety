The Gap

9% of People Don't Buckle Up. They Account for Half the Deaths.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2026

Nine percent of American adults skip the seat belt. They account for nearly half of traffic deaths, according to a September 2026 AAA Foundation study built on six years of federal fatal-crash data.[1] That is not a rounding error or a sampling quirk. It is the single most lopsided split in American road safety, and it has survived every attempt to explain it away.

of passenger-vehicle occupants killed in 2022 were unrestrained, versus 14% of survivors in the same crashes (NHTSA, known restraint use)

AAA's researchers did two things at once: they surveyed more than 2,800 adults about belt habits, then matched those answers against Fatality Analysis Reporting System records from 2017 through 2022.[2] Inside fatal crashes, belt use was just 72%, against 91% observed use among adult front-seat occupants in the general population. Passengers did worse than drivers, 31% unbelted versus 27%, and more than a quarter of drivers and passengers involved in a fatal crash in the past 30 days were unbelted, the 28% figure AAA headlines.[1]

Run the crude math yourself, because AAA stops short of it. Nine percent of occupants produce roughly 50% of occupant deaths. Divide each group's death share by its population share and an unbelted occupant dies at about 10 times the per-person rate of a belted one: (50/9) against (50/91). Two cross-checks from the same sources land in the same direction with smaller magnitudes, 28% against 9% in the fatal-crash sample, and 50% of the dead against 14% of survivors. Treat 10x as an illustration, not a causal estimate, because NHTSA's controlled numbers are far more modest: belts cut fatal injury risk 45% for front-seat car occupants and 60% for light-truck occupants once crash severity is accounted for.[3] Somewhere between the crude ratio and the controlled estimate sits the truth, and the distance between them is confounding.

Ask who the 9% are and the profile stops looking random. Men, younger adults, rear-seat passengers, pickup occupants, nighttime drivers, rural-road travelers, people already drinking or using drugs: AAA derived nine road-user profiles from the survey and crash data, and every one of them reads like a different excuse for the same decision.[1] Asked why they skip the belt, 30 to 50% of sometimes-or-never users gave the same three answers: only driving a short distance, forgot to put it on, traveling on low-speed roads. Short trips and slow roads are precisely where a belt feels most optional, which is why they dominate the excuses and, presumably, the deaths.

Steel-man the other side before dismissing it, because it is strong. Belt use correlates with everything else that kills: speed, impairment, rural roads, older vehicles, risk tolerance. Nobody believes buckling up a drunk driver at 2 a.m. on a county road converts his crash into a fender-bender, and AAA's profiles prove the point by showing non-use clustering with the very behaviors that cause crashes. If every American buckled up tomorrow, deaths would fall, but not by half, because the deaths and the non-use share common causes. What the belt does change, inside a crash already happening, is ejection: 1% of belted occupants in fatal crashes were totally ejected in 2022, against 26% of unrestrained occupants, and 83% of everyone totally ejected died.[3]

What this analysis did not prove deserves its own paragraph. FARS records fatal crashes only, so it says nothing about injury-only crashes, where belt effectiveness differs. NHTSA's 50%-versus-14% split covers known restraint use only, excluding cases where belt status was never recorded. Comparing the 72% fatal-crash sample against 91% general-population use mixes samples, all occupants against adult front-seat occupants, so the comparison is directional rather than exact. Survey answers carry their own bias: 70% of drivers say they always buckle up while only 57% of passengers say the same, and "always" is a stricter claim than being observed belted on one trip.[1] None of that erases the inversion. All of it shrinks the precision with which anyone can quote it.

So what do you actually do with a 9% problem? Buckle up on short trips and slow roads first, because those are the excuses doing the damage, not highway speeding. If you drive, make rear-seat belts non-negotiable before the car moves: passengers are the worst belt users in the fatal-crash data, and their excuses are yours once the car rolls. And if you are a young man in a pickup heading out at night on a rural road, congratulations, you are AAA's highest-risk profile on four separate axes at once. A belt will not fix the rest of your risk profile. It is still the cheapest, fastest intervention in the entire vehicle, and unlike every other safety system in your car, it works only when you choose it.
