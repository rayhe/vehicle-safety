Pedestrian Deaths Dropped 7% Last Year. Distraction Nearly Doubled Since 2019. Do the Math.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 20, 2026

The Governors Highway Safety Association just reported that drivers killed 6,732 pedestrians in the United States last year.[1] That is 7% fewer than in 2024, the third straight annual decline, and the largest single-year drop of the streak. Celebration is tempting, but resist it for about ninety seconds, because the math underneath this good news is not good news at all.

18.4%
Pedestrians’ share of all U.S. traffic deaths in 2025 — identical to 2024, and up from 18.2% in 2023

Start with the calculation nobody put in the press release. In 2023, pedestrians accounted for 7,472 of 41,025 total traffic deaths: 18.2%.[1][3] In 2024, they were 7,237 of 39,254: 18.4%.[1][2] Last year, they were 6,732 of an estimated 36,640: 18.4%.[1][2] Three years of falling death tolls, and the pedestrian slice of the pie has not moved a tenth of a point. Pedestrians did not get safer; everyone got safer, and pedestrians merely rode along.

Now look at what the tide had to fight. In 2024, 21.7% of pedestrian deaths involved a distracted driver, up from 15.6% in 2019.[1] In absolute terms (applying GHSA’s percentages to its death counts), that is roughly 1,570 deaths with a phone in the causal chain, versus about 1,000 at the 2019 rate. Pedestrians nearly doubled their own distraction share over the same period, from 15.3% to 29.2%.[1] Both parties in the crash are looking at screens more than at any point in history, and the death count still fell. Whatever is saving pedestrians is working against a headwind, not with a tailwind.

And the vehicles themselves did not get friendlier. Just over half of all pedestrian deaths, 50.4%, came from light trucks: SUVs, pickups, and vans, the tallest front ends on the road.[1] More than three-quarters happened after dark, whether or not the street was lit.[1] Those are the two design problems that matter most, and neither has fundamentally changed: tall, blunt front ends meeting human bodies, and headlights that cannot reveal a crossing pedestrian in time. IIHS now grades pedestrian front crash prevention and headlight performance, and those two ratings are the single most useful thing a buyer can consult for everyone outside the car.[6]

One state suggests the decline is not just the tide. New Mexico, which had the worst pedestrian death rate in the country in 2023 and spiked even higher in 2024, cut its first-half deaths nearly in half last year, from 53 to 27, after deploying flashing warning lights that activate when a pedestrian steps into a crosswalk.[4] Alabama, California, Maryland, New Mexico, and New York together drove more than two-thirds of the national first-half decline.[4] Drops that concentrated, in a handful of states, look like interventions. Not luck.

What to do with this

If you are shopping for a car, the two ratings that protect everyone outside it are the IIHS pedestrian front crash prevention grade and the headlight grade, and the headlight grade matters more than most buyers realize: three-quarters of pedestrian deaths happen after dark.[1][6] Check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because pedestrian automatic emergency braking improvements increasingly ship as software updates.[7] Pedestrians: the distraction share nearly doubled since 2019, and the phone in your hand at the crosswalk is doing exactly what you think it is.[1] And if your city council is debating flashing crosswalk beacons, New Mexico just ran the pilot program for you.[4]

What this article did not prove: the 2025 figures are GHSA preliminary projections from state highway safety offices, not FARS final data, and will be revised when the FARS Final File for 2024 and the Annual Report File for 2025 publish in early 2027. Distraction is systematically undercounted in crash data — officers code what is visible and phones are not always examined — so 21.7% is a floor, not a ceiling. The 50.4% light-truck share is not exposure-adjusted; light trucks are a large share of the registered fleet, so this does not prove they kill more pedestrians per mile. New Mexico’s crosswalk lights are correlation with a plausible mechanism, not a controlled experiment. The 2019 pedestrian baseline is implied by GHSA’s “5% above 2019” statement (about 6,411 deaths), not independently sourced.

Strongest case against this article: 740 fewer dead pedestrians is 740 fewer funerals, and the absolute number is what matters to the families left behind. That is true, and the streak deserves its moment. But causes determine prescriptions: if pedestrians were getting disproportionately safer, the right move would be doubling down on pedestrian-specific fixes. They are not; they are floating on a general tide: fifteen consecutive quarters of declining traffic deaths across every NHTSA region. Misread the cause and you fund the wrong cure — money for crosswalks when the real lever was enforcement, or applause for vehicle design that has not actually changed.
