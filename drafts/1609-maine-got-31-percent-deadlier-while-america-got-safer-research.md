# Research — #1609: America's Roads Got 4% Safer in 2024. Maine Got 31% Deadlier.

**Journalist:** Axle McScatter (statistical roundup / national trends)
**Kicker:** By The Numbers
**Slug:** 1609-maine-got-31-percent-deadlier-while-america-got-safer
**Date:** November 27, 2026

## Angle
The national headline from NHTSA's final 2024 FARS data is good news: 39,254 deaths, down 4.3%, rate 1.19. The state table underneath it tells the opposite story in 16 jurisdictions. Maine's death toll jumped 31% in a single year while the country improved. And the per-capita gap between the safest state (Rhode Island, 4.7 per 100,000) and the deadliest (Mississippi, 25.6) is 5.4x — wider than the gap between the safest and deadliest car models this site usually covers. The national average is a blend of two Americas that barely share a road-safety reality.

## Kill test
Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. No prior Crash Report article has done a state-level FARS 2024 analysis with per-capita and per-mile rankings. #1565 covered counties; #1469 and #1053 covered the national 2025 estimates. The Maine +31% hook, the 16-states-worse count, and the 5.4x per-capita gap are all original calculations from the primary tables. Novel angle on fresh final data.

## Verified numbers
- **National 2024:** 39,254 deaths (-4.3% vs 41,025 in 2023); rate 1.19/100M VMT (was 1.26); 11.5 per 100,000 people; 36,297 fatal crashes; 2,422,195 people injured; 6,180,241 police-reported crashes (+0.7%, not statistically significant).
- **States that got worse (16 of 51 jurisdictions):** Maine 177 vs 135 (+31%), Minnesota 477 vs 402 (+19%), Alaska 70 vs 61 (+15%), Nebraska 251 vs 227 (+11%), New Jersey 670 vs 605 (+11%), Hawaii 102 vs 93 (+10%), DC 47 vs 44 (+7%), Massachusetts 363 vs 342 (+6%), New Hampshire 133 vs 127 (+5%), South Dakota 146 vs 140 (+4%), Mississippi 753 vs 733 (+3%), Nevada 417 vs 406 (+3%), North Carolina 1,619 vs 1,577 (+3%), Wisconsin 595 vs 586 (+2%), Arkansas 603 vs 596 (+1%), Connecticut 310 vs 308 (+1%).
- **Flat (2):** Michigan 1,098 vs 1,096 (0%), Virginia 917 vs 914 (0%).
- **Improved (33):** every other state. Biggest declines: Rhode Island 52 vs 71 (-27%), Wyoming 107 vs 144 (-26%), North Dakota 90 vs 106 (-15%), Vermont 59 vs 69 (-14%), Georgia 1,403 vs 1,610 (-13%, -207 deaths, biggest absolute drop), Idaho 238 vs 275 (-13%), Kentucky 707 vs 814 (-13%).
- **Per-capita (IIHS, 2024):** Mississippi 25.6 (worst), Arkansas 19.5, New Mexico 19.2; Rhode Island 4.7 (best), Massachusetts 5.1, New York 5.5; national 11.5. Gap: 25.6 / 4.7 = **5.4x**. Mississippi is 2.2x the national rate.
- **Per-mile (IIHS, 2024):** Mississippi 1.81 (worst), Arizona 1.74, South Carolina 1.67; Massachusetts 0.59 (best), Rhode Island 0.68; national 1.19. Gap: 1.81 / 0.59 = **3.1x**. Mississippi tops BOTH rankings, which is unusual — most states rank high on one measure and middling on the other.
- **Maine detail (FARS person-type table):** 177 deaths in 167 fatal crashes; drivers 106 (59.9%), motorcyclists 35 (19.8% vs 15.9% national), passengers 22, pedestrians 13. Maine's +42 deaths is the largest percentage increase of any state.
- **Minnesota detail:** 477 vs 402 is +75 deaths, the largest absolute increase of any state that got worse; New Jersey +65 (670 vs 605).
- **Other person-type extremes:** DC pedestrians 18 of 47 deaths (38.3%); Hawaii pedestrians 35 of 102 (34.3%); Florida motorcyclists 642 (20.5% of its 3,138 deaths).
- **2025 context:** NHTSA's early estimate puts 2025 at 36,640 deaths (-6.7%), rate 1.10 — the improvement continued after 2024, so the national story is genuinely good. That makes the state divergence sharper, not weaker.

## Original contribution
- Counted the full state table: 16 jurisdictions worse, 2 flat, 33 better — the national -4.3% hides a one-third split.
- Computed the 5.4x per-capita gap (25.6 vs 4.7) and 3.1x per-mile gap (1.81 vs 0.59) from the IIHS table.
- Identified Maine as the largest percentage increase (+31%) and Minnesota as the largest absolute increase among worsening states (+75 deaths); Georgia as the largest absolute decline (-207).
- Cross-tab: Mississippi is the only state that tops both the per-capita and per-mile rankings — it is dangerous by both denominators at once.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)
State percentages are noisy, especially in small states: Maine's +31% is 42 deaths, and a single multi-fatality crash moves the needle. Per-capita rates punish rural states whose residents drive far more, and per-mile rates punish states with long rural highways; neither controls for vehicle mix, speed limits, seat-belt use, or how far the nearest trauma center is. The national trend is real and good — three straight annual declines after the pandemic spike, and 2025 looks better still. A one-year state jump is not a verdict on a state's roads.

## Limitations
- FARS counts deaths within 30 days of a crash on public roads; it is a census, not a sample, but 2024 state tables are first-release final data and 2025 figures are estimates.
- One year of state change is not a trend; small-state percentages swing on a handful of crashes.
- Per-capita and per-mile rates measure different things and neither adjusts for who drives, how far, in what, or at what speed. IIHS's own caveat: rates depend on vehicle mix, travel speeds, licensure rates, traffic laws, emergency care, weather, and terrain.
- This article does not prove WHY Maine or Minnesota got worse — FARS state tables show counts, not causes. No causal claim is made about specific policies.

## Actionable insights
- If you live in Mississippi, Arkansas, or New Mexico: your per-capita risk is roughly double the national rate, and in Mississippi it is 5.4x Rhode Island's. That is not a rounding error — it is the single biggest geographic safety fact in America.
- Moving states changes your road risk more than switching car models does. Check your state's rate, not just your car's rating.
- Maine and Minnesota drivers: the 2024 jump is a warning, not a sentence. Watch whether 2025 confirms it before drawing conclusions.
