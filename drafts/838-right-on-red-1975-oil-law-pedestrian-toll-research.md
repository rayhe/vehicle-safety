# Research: #838 — Right Turn on Red: The 1975 Gas Law Still Killing Pedestrians

Slug: `right-on-red-1975-oil-law-pedestrian-toll`
Journalist: Rex Driverton | Kicker: Investigation | Date: 2026-09-07

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
Right turn on red was not a traffic-safety innovation. It was a fuel-conservation measure imposed by the Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975, which made states allow it "to the maximum extent practicable" or forfeit federal energy funds. Fifty years later the gas savings have evaporated (start-stop systems, hybrids, EVs that burn nothing at idle) while the pedestrian toll compounds — and in 2026, Atlanta banned it and Nashville is voting on a ban, repealing a Carter-era energy law one intersection at a time.

## Original computation (the novel contribution)
The benefit that justified the law is gone; the cost grew with the fleet. Two-part arithmetic, assumptions stated:

(a) The 1977 Virginia study is the only hard quantification of RTOR's fuel benefit: 3.6M gallons/year in one state. Scaled crudely to the US (Virginia ~2.5% of national VMT then), the national benefit was on the order of ~140M gallons/year in 1977 — real money during rationing. Today, EPA data shows roughly 1 in 3 new vehicles has auto start-stop and EVs/hybrids are ~20%+ of sales; an idling modern car burns ~0.2-0.5 gal/hr, an EV essentially zero at a light. The per-intersection fuel prize that justified a federal mandate has decayed toward zero while the mandate itself was never repealed.

(b) The lethality multiplier: IIHS finds a right-turning pickup is 89% more likely to kill a struck pedestrian than a car (SUV: 63% more). In the mid-1990s, when NHTSA last counted RTOR deaths (~84/year), light trucks were roughly 40% of new-vehicle sales. In 2026 they are roughly 80%. Weighting the IIHS multipliers by fleet mix, the expected lethality of the average right-turning vehicle striking a pedestrian is ~35-40% higher today than in 1995 — same 1975 law, deadlier fleet. Calculation: 1995 mix (0.6 car x 1.0 + 0.4 truck x ~1.75) = 1.30 vs 2026 mix (0.2 x 1.0 + 0.8 x ~1.75) = 1.60; 1.60/1.30 = 1.23. Using the SUV 63% figure for the truck share gives 1.50/1.25 = 1.20. So ~20-23% higher expected lethality per strike, before accounting for any change in exposure. (Conservative; uses midpoints.)

## Thesis
Congress bought this law with gas rationing in 1975. The gas savings expired; the EPCA provision did not. Every RTOR death since is interest on a paid-off loan — and cities from Atlanta to Nashville are finally foreclosing.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)
RTOR deaths were always a small slice of pedestrian fatalities — NHTSA's own 1995 count was 84/year against thousands of total ped deaths, and there has been no recent national count, so the 2026 toll is extrapolated, not measured. Banning has real costs: more idling (the original concern, still valid for the 250M+ ICE vehicles on the road), slower buses and delivery routes, and enforcement that Priya Sarathy Jones (Fines and Fees Justice Center) warns will fall hardest on lower-income drivers who must drive to work. Compliance is genuinely bad — 10-20% of drivers blow through existing no-turn-on-red signs, which can make pedestrians less safe by raising their expectations. And the fleet-mix math cuts both ways: AEB and pedestrian detection, now standard on most new cars, specifically target the low-speed intersection conflicts RTOR creates. The honest version: RTOR is a real but second-order hazard, and the cities banning it are buying pedestrian comfort as much as body-count reduction.

## Limitations
- No national RTOR death count exists after 1995; the article extrapolates from old NHTSA and state-level data (California 2011-2022, Toronto 2008-2012). Say so.
- The fleet-mix lethality estimate is illustrative, not epidemiological: it assumes the IIHS car/pickup/SUV kill-odds ratios apply uniformly and ignores AEB penetration, speed differences, and urban/rural mix.
- The fuel-benefit decay argument is directional: no current study measures RTOR's 2026 fuel savings; the claim rests on start-stop/EV penetration and per-vehicle idle consumption, not a metered total.
- CMF figures (0.625/0.709) come from intersection-level studies compiled by FHWA; applying them citywide assumes similar conditions.
- FARS does not code RTOR as a crash factor, so the site's own FARS dataset cannot directly verify the thesis — a data gap worth naming.

## Actionable insights
- Pedestrians: at any red light, the turning driver is looking LEFT for a gap in traffic, not at you. Make eye contact before crossing in front of a car that can legally roll through your crosswalk. (Fast Company cites the attention-skew study.)
- Drivers: the law requires a full stop first in every state; "rolling right" is the violation that kills. And check right, not just left — the crosswalk to your right has a walk signal at the same time you have a red.
- If your city council is debating a ban: the FHWA numbers are the ones that matter — ~29% fewer vehicle-pedestrian right-turn crashes where prohibitions go in, up to 50% at busy intersections. Pair the signs with leading pedestrian intervals (3-7 seconds of walk time before green), or non-compliance eats the gains.
- Used-car shoppers: none of this changes what to buy, but it changes where to walk. NYC — the one place in America that never allowed RTOR — is the natural experiment; its pedestrian death rate per capita runs well below comparable Sun Belt cities.
