Existential Dread

Ford Recalled 41,748 Trucks Over Headlights That Can Die in the Dark. The Warning Light Works Fine.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 2, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your evening commute, which is the commute that matters. Ford is recalling 41,748 Expeditions and Super Duty trucks because the LED chips inside their headlight assemblies may be contaminated, a defect that can knock out the low beams, high beams, parking lights, and daytime running lights all at once.[1] Not a burnt bulb or a blown fuse, but a dirty microscopic chip inside a sealed assembly you cannot open, in a headlight that was supposed to last the life of the truck.

41,748
Expeditions and Super Duty trucks with potentially contaminated headlight LED chips

The affected population is a strange pair. On one side: 2025 through 2027 Ford Expeditions, built between June 24, 2025 and July 2, 2026, more than a year of the family hauler rolling off the line. On the other: 2026 Super Duty F-250s, F-350s, F-450s, and F-550s produced on a single day, August 4, 2026.[1] Same chip, two assembly lines, thirteen months apart: not a line problem and not a design problem, but a supplier problem, one contaminated batch of components riding through the supply chain like a rumor and landing in two different vehicles built a year apart.

And here is the part Ford put in writing, which I admire for its honesty and fear for its implications. Per the company's own safety report: "A dashboard indicator light will illuminate for customers experiencing inoperative low beam and/or high beam headlamps."[2] Read that again slowly. The most dependable lamp on a $90,000 truck is the one on the dashboard telling you the actual headlights died. Your headlights fail, and the truck congratulates itself for noticing.

This is the quiet absurdity of the sealed-LED era. Under the old deal, bulbs burned out, you bought a new one at a gas station for nine dollars, and a teenager installed it in the parking lot while you waited. Now headlights are computers, and computers have supply chains, and supply chains have bad days. A contaminated chip means the entire assembly gets ripped out and thrown away, because there is nothing inside a sealed LED headlamp a dealer can actually repair. A macroscopic trash can is the fix for a microscopic defect, replaced free of charge, which is the one part of this story that works exactly as it should.

And the dark is not a neutral setting for any of this. Roughly half of all traffic fatalities happen at night even though far less driving occurs after dark, which makes headlight performance one of the few safety attributes you can verify with your own eyes instead of a spec sheet, so verify it tonight.

If you own a 2025 to 2027 Expedition or a 2026 Super Duty, walk around the truck tonight with the lights on and check every one of them: low beams, high beams, daytime running lights, parking lights. If any of them are out, that is not a bulb to replace later; it is a dealer visit now. Dealers will inspect the assemblies and replace them free, and owner notification letters go out October 26, but do not wait for the letter.[3] Run your 17-character VIN through nhtsa.gov/recalls tonight, which takes less time than reading this article. Ford reports no accidents or injuries tied to the defect so far, though darkness does not negotiate.

Now, the case for not panicking, stated at full strength, because it deserves it. Ford says it knows of zero crashes and zero injuries tied to this defect.[1] The contamination is batch-level, which means plenty of the 41,748 trucks on the recall list will pass inspection with clean chips and drive away unchanged; the population is potentially affected, not certainly affected. The dashboard warning works as designed, which is more than can be said for the headlights. And legally, this is a noncompliance finding under FMVSS 108, the federal lamp standard: it means the equipment may not meet the standard, not that 41,748 trucks are driving blind tonight. The recall is the system working before anyone gets hurt, and that is the outcome you want from a safety regulator and a car company.

What I do not know, and will not pretend otherwise: I could not locate the NHTSA Part 573 filing for Ford's 26C43, so the official federal campaign number and the exact model-by-model counts come secondhand through Ford's own reporting, not from the federal document. Nobody has published the actual contamination rate, meaning how many of the 41,748 carry the bad chip versus merely sharing a supplier with it. And there is no public data tying any specific crash to these chips. The zero-injury figure is Ford's own, and absence of reported injuries is not proof of absence of incidents.
