The Gap

Ohio Saw the Fog Coming. The Warning It Gave Drivers Was a Suggestion

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 28, 2026

Just after 6:15 a.m. on October 25, visibility on Interstate 75 near Findlay, Ohio fell below 50 feet. Drivers entering a fog bank over the Blanchard River bottomland braked hard. The tractor-trailers behind them could not stop. Three people are dead, at least 12 more are injured, and 28 vehicles are wreckage across three-quarters of a mile of northbound lanes.[1]

35.2%
Rear-end crash reduction on freeway corridors with variable speed limits, per FHWA research

The Ohio Department of Transportation saw this coming. It issued a dense fog advisory at 5 a.m. But an advisory is a suggestion, posted somewhere drivers may never look. A variable speed limit is a change in the law, displayed over the lane you are in. And per NBC News, no variable speed signs were active in that stretch of I-75.[1] The corridor knows its hazard: the Blanchard River basin is a documented trouble spot for early-morning whiteouts in late October. The agency fired the warning layer and skipped the control layer.

There is hard evidence the control layer works. The Federal Highway Administration evaluated variable speed limit installations in Wyoming, Georgia, and Virginia and found total crash reductions of 34.4% and 29.2% in Wyoming and Georgia, with rear-end crashes down 35.2% on the Georgia corridor.[2] The FHWA's crash modification clearinghouse lists VSL with a 35.2% rear-end crash reduction factor, and the economic analysis put benefit-cost ratios at 9 to 40 to 1.[3] Meanwhile fog itself is a uniquely pileup-shaped hazard: FHWA counts over 38,700 fog crashes a year, killing 600+ and injuring 16,300,[4] and FARS-derived tables show that fatal crashes involving 10 or more vehicles are 19.53% fog-related, against 1.44% of all fatal crashes. Fog does not just cause crashes. It causes pileups. Twenty-eight vehicles, six of them semis, is exactly fog's characteristic profile.[5]

Now the honest weak link. Virginia's own VSL study found that drivers do not comply with lowered speed limits until they are already inside the fog, and compliance gaps persist at the lowest posted speeds.[6] Nobody can prove VSL would have prevented Findlay. Trucks at full speed in a 50-foot whiteout at dawn is physics, not policy. And there is a simpler reading of "no signs were active in that stretch": the corridor may have no VSL hardware at all, in which case the 5 a.m. advisory was the best available move. The gap is infrastructure, not attention. Advisories warn. Signs command. One of the two was missing where the fog always forms.

What this article did not prove: the crash details rest on a single syndicated report citing NBC News and the Ohio State Highway Patrol; the NTSB is not investigating, and speeds, dashcam footage, and truck ELD data are not public. VSL effectiveness is population-level evidence applied to a single event, and it has never been shown to prevent a 28-vehicle pileup specifically. The FHWA fog figures and the FARS weather tables cited are not vintage-matched to 2026; treat them as order-of-magnitude. Whether ODOT operates variable speed limit hardware anywhere on its network is unknown from these sources.

What to do: in sudden fog, slow down before the wall, not inside it. Never stop in a travel lane. If visibility collapses, take the next exit and wait it out. Keep headlights on low beam and hazards on. And if your state DOT publishes its variable speed limit corridors, check whether the highway you commute on has them. Washington, Wyoming, Georgia, and Virginia operate VSL corridors today.[7] If your fog-prone commute does not have one, that is a question for your state representative, not your car.
