Five Years, Two Million Recalls, One Asleep Driver: Tesla Hits Another Cop Car

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 19, 2027

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, the impairment in last month's Fairfield crash was not chemical. It was technological. Just before midnight on October 13, a Tesla drove through a four-lane freeway closure on eastbound I-80, past flares, cones, and flashing emergency lights, and into a parked CHP Dodge Charger with two officers inside. Both officers were seriously injured. A 22-year-old driver from Sacramento told investigators he was asleep, with Full Self-Driving engaged.[1]

2,031,220
Teslas recalled in December 2023 to prevent exactly this. The fix added attention alerts. The attention, allegedly, was asleep.

Now count the federal paperwork, because I did. In August 2021, NHTSA opened a probe after a string of Teslas on Autopilot crashed into stopped emergency vehicles, many of them at night, at scenes marked by flares and cones. That ended with the December 2023 recall of 2,031,220 vehicles under 23V-838: an over-the-air update making attention warnings more prominent. In April 2024, NHTSA opened a recall query, because remedied vehicles were still crashing. In October 2024, an FSD visibility evaluation covering 2.4 million vehicles. In October 2025, an FSD traffic-violation probe covering 2.88 million. In March 2026, an engineering analysis covering 3,203,754 vehicles after nine crashes, one of them fatal. Five years of paperwork. Same failure.[2]

FSD is watched by a cabin camera, and Tesla's own manual reads like a confession. It says the monitoring will not activate when the camera is covered, when lighting is poor, or when the driver wears sunglasses or a hat that hides the eyes. Electrek has reported drivers defeating it with a dollar bill. In December 2025, Elon Musk told owners they could text and drive "depending on context of surrounding traffic," which is illegal in California and everywhere else. A U.S. congressman has cited 43 videos of sleeping drivers in pressing the Department of Transportation. In July, one driver was filmed apparently asleep at 100 km/h in British Columbia with two children in the car, wearing big sunglasses. A monitoring system that sunglasses defeat is not a monitoring system. It is a dashboard ornament.[1][2]

Fairness first. His account is unconfirmed: FSD engagement is so far only his word, and the vehicle logs will decide. Falling asleep at freeway speed would be lethal with or without software, and CHP is right that technology does not relieve anyone of responsibility. But driver fault and system fault were never mutually exclusive, and juries have started agreeing: a Florida federal jury found Tesla 33% responsible in a 2019 fatal Autopilot crash and awarded about $243 million, a verdict a federal judge upheld in February 2026. Then there is the name, which helps the complacency along. Tesla agreed to drop "Full Self-Driving" in Europe after Germany called it misleading. Washington got to keep it. When you sell people those three words, a 22-year-old driving home at midnight is exactly the person who believes them.[2][3]

Do the obvious things, because the obvious things are the only ones that work. Treat FSD as a Level 2 driver aid: eyes up, hands ready, and never drive it drowsy. That camera admits, in writing, that sunglasses defeat it. If your eyelids are heavy, pull over; the car will not do it for you. Shopping for hands-free? GM's Super Cruise and Ford's BlueCruise only engage on mapped highways and watch the driver with infrared cameras, and even then NHTSA escalated a BlueCruise probe after two fatal night crashes into stationary vehicles. No system yet survives a driver who checks out. Five years of federal paperwork says so. Be the driver who does not add to the census.
