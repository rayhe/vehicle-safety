Three of America's Biggest Pickups Flunked the First Auto-Brake Test. The Electric Vans Passed.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds after a truck's sensors spot something in its path. For the 2025 Ford F-350, apparently nothing: IIHS ran the industry's first front crash prevention tests for commercial vehicles this month, and the F-350 could only avoid a passenger-car target at 31 mph. In a 43-mph test it failed to slow down at all. Three of the four Class 3 pickups tested scored Poor for vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention.[1]

work trucks and cargo vans rated marginal or poor for vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention

The scorecard is a rough read. IIHS tested eleven commercial vehicles, seven cargo vans and four heavy pickups, on October 8, and across the whole fleet only one vehicle earned a Good rating for vehicle-to-vehicle braking: the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500. Everywhere else the sensors fell apart. The Silverado 3500HD failed to avoid the passenger car in all six of its tests. Motorcycles were the worst category of all. Most of the eleven never warned their drivers about the bike in time, and some never slowed for it. The Transit's pre-collision system couldn't produce a timely warning regardless of speed or position. Think about that for a second.[2]

Pedestrian detection looked slightly better, and the twist should embarrass Detroit. The only two Good ratings for pedestrian braking went to battery-electric delivery vans: the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 and the Rivian RCV-Delivery 500. Meanwhile the Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500, a platform family old enough to have a mortgage, scored Poor in both categories. Reason given: they have no automatic emergency braking at all in 2026. A manufacturer is still selling a new van with no AEB and IIHS just published the receipt.[3]

There is a regulatory hole underneath all of this, and it is load-bearing. NHTSA's AEB requirements apply to passenger vehicles, while Class 3 pickups, the 10,001-to-14,000-pound band where the F-350 and Silverado 3500HD live, sit above the mandate's cutoff. IIHS vice president David Kidd told the Detroit Free Press there are "gaps in the U.S. vehicle safety system" between what passenger cars must have and what work vehicles must have, and added: "When there are large crashes, those vehicles are usually doling out the harm." IIHS is now grading what the government does not require. The industry's defense deserves a fair hearing: a false-positive panic stop in a loaded 14,000-pound truck is genuinely dangerous, and fleet managers will say their professional drivers don't need a nanny. Fair. But IIHS was not testing shadows on the pavement. It was testing whether these trucks can see a car, a motorcycle, or a person at all, and for nine of eleven vehicles the honest answer was barely.[4]

Which brings us to the number that should sit over your desk. In 2024, crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans killed 6,215 people, 16 percent of all U.S. roadway deaths.[5] Some trucks in this test also lost sight of child-sized pedestrian dummies as they closed in, a front-blind-zone observation IIHS folded into the results. If you drive one of these, or your kids walk to school past a fleet lot, the practical advice is blunt. Fleet managers: the Sprinter is the only Good vehicle-to-vehicle score on the board; the BrightDrop and Rivian vans lead on pedestrians; do not spec an Express or Savana for a route that runs through neighborhoods. Everyone else: follow a heavy pickup or full-size van like its electronics are ornamental, because IIHS suggests the gap between "has sensors" and "can stop" is wider than the grille.[6]
