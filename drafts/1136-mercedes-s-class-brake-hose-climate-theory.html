Mercedes Blamed the Weather for Its Leaky Brake Hoses. The Reports From Outside the U.S. Disagreed.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

For twenty months, Mercedes-Benz had a theory about its leaking brake hoses. The theory was weather. Hot, humid weather, to be precise: the kind that supposedly explained why front brake hoses on the flagship S-Class were weeping fluid before their time. Mercedes filed the original recall in February 2025, scoped it to specific production windows and steamy climates, and moved on. Then reports started arriving from places where the theory did not live, and the theory died. Mercedes just recalled 30,078 S-Class sedans under NHTSA campaign 26V638, expanding a recall that was supposed to be a Florida problem into something much larger.[1]

30,078
S-Class sedans recalled under NHTSA campaign 26V638, up from about 20,339 in the original 25V116. The expansion covers 2022-2025 models built between February 2, 2021 and November 28, 2024, including Maybach and AMG variants.

Start with what failed, because the failure is almost insultingly ordinary. The front brake hoses, supplied by Continental Automotive Technologies of Frankfurt, did not meet durability specifications.[2] Heat from the brakes and mechanical stress from suspension movement fatigue the rubber over time, and a fatigued hose can rupture without warning, bleeding fluid out of one of the car's two hydraulic brake circuits. One circuit gone means longer stopping distances, which is the polite regulatory phrasing for a two-and-a-half-ton luxury sedan that needs more road than you have. Your dashboard will show a red brake-fluid warning once the fluid is already leaving. There is no warning before the hose itself gives up.[1]

Now the autopsy of the climate theory. Mercedes became aware of the issue in late 2023, filed 25V116 in February 2025 covering 20,339 cars from model years 2021-2023, and blamed hot, humid operating environments.[3] Between March and August 2025, field reports arrived from outside the United States, and a weather theory cannot survive geography. Mercedes pulled hoses for inspection, reviewed lab and durability test results in July 2026, and by September 2026 the company acknowledged what the foreign reports had been saying for a year: it could not rule out a safety risk for the larger population.[1] This month came 26V638, and the S 580 4MATIC alone accounts for 16,881 of the 30,078 vehicles, which tells you exactly which car this was always about.

Here is the detail that will sit with you. Mercedes started building cars with an improved brake hose on November 29, 2024, a running production change made nearly a year before the recall expanded.[1] The factory was fixed while the field was not. If your S-Class rolled off the line after that date, you have the good hoses and were never in the population. If it rolled off before, your letter arrives starting November 27, and until then the only thing standing between you and the knowledge is a VIN lookup. Continental, for its part, corrected the defect on its end back in 2022, which means the bad hoses had a multi-year head start on everyone trying to catch them.[4]

What to do is simple, which is rare enough to savor. If you own a 2022-2025 S-Class, including any Maybach S 580, Maybach S 680, or AMG S 63 E Performance, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today rather than waiting for the November letter.[5] Dealers replace both front brake hoses free of charge. Do not drive on a red brake-fluid warning. Shopping for a used W223 S-Class? Confirm this recall is closed on that specific VIN before money changes hands, and note that cars built after November 29, 2024 already carry the improved part, which is one more reason to check the build date on the door jamb sticker.

Fairness requires the defense, and the defense is stronger than it looks. Dual-circuit brake systems exist precisely for this failure: lose one circuit and the other still stops the car, which is why 30,078 recalled vehicles produced three U.S. field reports in 2026, dated April 2, May 29, and August 10, and zero crashes, zero injuries, zero property damage.[1] Mercedes expanded the recall on foreign field reports and laboratory data before anyone was hurt, which is the system working rather than failing. And the eleven-month gap between the production fix and the expanded recall is how recall scoping works in practice: you do not re-notify a population you have not finished defining, and defining it took lab tests, not press releases.

Limitations: Every figure in the filing is Mercedes' own, from the 30,078 count to the three field reports, so both could understate the real population. FARS cannot isolate brake-hose failures, so there is no fatality-rate cross-tab to run against this defect; the honest number for observed harm is zero. The failure is age- and stress-dependent, which means a 2021 car with six summers on it is more exposed than a 2024 garage queen. And the red dashboard warning only fires after fluid is lost, so the absence of a warning is not evidence the hoses are healthy.

A brake hose is a rubber tube that flexes every time the wheel moves and bakes every time you stop. Mercedes spent twenty months learning that its rubber did not care about the forecast. Check the VIN. The weather was never the problem, and the hoses were always the story.
