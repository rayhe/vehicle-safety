DUI Blood Tests Take 370 Days to Process. The Drugs Leave Your System in 72 Hours.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
August 20, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, there are a lot of them. Specifically, in Washington state, there are about 17,000 a year, growing 13% annually, and the state patrol's forensic laboratory takes 370 days to process each one.[1] That is not a typo. A full calendar year, plus five days for good measure.

370
Days for Washington State's toxicology lab to return a DUI blood test result

Colorado's labs run about 140 days behind.[2] North Carolina's state crime lab has returned results "up to a year" after collection.[3] The National Institute of Justice found in 2019 that state and local forensic laboratories were understaffed by more than 900 positions nationwide, and backlogs were growing.[4] AAA's December 2025 issue brief put it plainly: chronic backlogs mean "delayed results, dismissed cases, and missed opportunities to hold impaired drivers accountable."[5]

The numbers, as usual, tell the ugly version. A University of Wisconsin study of 8,923 crash-involved DUI arrests found that blood alcohol concentration and THC levels both decline measurably with every hour between the crash and the blood draw.[6] Fatal crashes averaged 2.35 hours to specimen collection. By then, a BAC that was 0.12 at impact might read 0.09 on paper. A THC level that proved active impairment at the scene might test as residual metabolite in the vial. The biology does not wait for the bureaucracy.

But the real damage happens after the blood is drawn. Washington's lab manager Brian Capron told KIRO 7 that 60 to 65% of the 17,000 annual submissions are DUI cases. The lab opened a second location in Federal Way with six authorized scientists. It needs four more. Training each takes 12 to 18 months.[1] So Washington's answer to a one-year backlog is a staffing pipeline that takes another year to produce a single qualified analyst.

Meanwhile, defense attorneys file speedy trial motions. North Carolina's court of appeals found a speedy trial violation in State v. Sheppard after a 14-month delay in an impaired driving case.[3] Colorado's task force flagged that toxicology delays were disrupting court timelines, victim compensation claims, and dram shop liability cases all at once.[2] In Colorado, 42.4% of DUI suspects now refuse the blood draw entirely. Only 45% of those caught even bother requesting an administrative hearing.[2] The system has become so slow that offenders have learned to simply wait it out.

There is one more problem buried in the pipeline. Researchers from the Center for Forensic Science Research and Education found that when a blood sample tests at BAC 0.10 or above, many labs stop testing. Case closed, drunk driver identified, move on. Except 17.3% of those cases were also positive for other drugs that were never screened.[5] Stop-limit testing means a driver who was drunk and high gets charged as merely drunk. The drug impairment vanishes from the record. Multiply that by 17,000 cases a year in a single state and the statistical portrait of drug-impaired driving in America is missing a significant chunk of its own face.

Washington passed Senate Bill 5880 to let cities and counties contract private accredited labs for toxicology work. It is the legislative equivalent of calling a plumber because the house is already flooded. The backlog is not a surprise. Impaired driving cases in Washington grew from 490 serious or fatal crashes in 2019 to 748 in 2023.[1] Lab capacity did not grow with them. Staffing did not grow with them. The evidence piled up and the system shrugged.

What you should know: If you are involved in a crash with a suspected impaired driver, the toxicology results that prove impairment may not exist for 4 to 12 months, depending on your state. Victim compensation claims, insurance disputes, and criminal charges all hinge on lab results that arrive on a timeline measured in seasons. Check your state's forensic lab turnaround times. If you are pursuing a dram shop liability claim or civil action, the statute of limitations may be more forgiving than the lab queue, but not by much. The evidence exists. The system to process it does not.
