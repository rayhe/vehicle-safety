## News hook

NHTSA is about to announce its rewrite of federal fuel-economy (CAFE) standards for 2022–2031, undoing the Biden-era rules. Buried in the draft is a reclassification: crossovers and small SUVs — currently regulated as "light trucks" with laxer targets — would be reclassified as **passenger cars**, which face stricter targets. GM told NHTSA in a filing that roughly 70% of new U.S. light-duty vehicles are currently classed as trucks vs. 30% cars, and the new method would roughly **reverse that balance**. The 2031 draft targets: **37.4 mpg for passenger cars, 28.6 mpg for trucks**.

Ford is fighting the reclassification of the Explorer, its best-known "truck": its public comment to NHTSA argues many Explorer-based law-enforcement vehicles are "utilized to patrol beaches, marshes, intercoastal areas along with rural communities."

## The Crash Report angle (original contribution)

Nobody has cross-referenced the regulatory car/truck taxonomy with FARS fatality rates. The vehicles NHTSA wants to **demote from truck to car** — the RAV4, CR-V, Rogue, Equinox, Tucson — are, by FARS occupant death rate, **dramatically safer per mile than the cars they'd be reclassified alongside**:

| Model (cls) | FARS deaths 2014–2023 | Rate per 100M VMT |
|---|---|---|
| Toyota RAV4 (SUV) | 914 | 0.19 |
| Toyota Camry (Sedan) | 6,328 | 2.03 |
| Honda CR-V (SUV) | 2,072 | 0.53 |
| Honda Civic (Sedan) | 6,553 | 2.25 |
| Nissan Rogue (SUV) | 968 | 0.35 |
| Toyota Corolla (Sedan) | 4,945 | 1.85 |
| Hyundai Tucson (SUV) | 669 | 0.34 |
| Honda Accord (Sedan) | 7,102 | 3.07 |
| Ford Explorer (SUV) | 3,797 | **1.54** |

RAV4's rate (0.19) is roughly **one-tenth** of the Camry's (2.03). The Explorer — the vehicle Ford is defending as a truck — is the crossover exception: at 1.54 it's in sedan territory, which either supports Ford's "it's a truck" argument or undercuts it, depending on how dark your humor runs.

The paradox for the article: the regulatory system draws a line between "car" and "truck" for fuel purposes, but the body-count taxonomy tells the opposite story. The safest vehicles on the road per mile are currently legally "trucks." The reclassification that makes compliance **harder** (cars face the tougher 37.4-mpg target) applies to vehicles that are already beating actual cars on the only metric that counts to the dead.

## Supporting facts

- NHTSA draft would revise down the 2022 standards, then hike 0.25–0.5% annually through 2031 (vs. Biden's 8%/10% hikes). (Reuters, Feb 4 2026)
- NHTSA also proposes eliminating credit trading among automakers in 2028 and ending some credits for fuel-saving features. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation (GM, Toyota, VW, Hyundai, Ford, etc.) backed the stringency rollback but asked NHTSA to reconsider the reclassification. (Reuters)
- Harvard EELP analysis (Jan 2026): NHTSA's own rationale — "require a significant reclassification of the passenger car and light truck fleets to reflect better the intent of the CAFE program established by Congress." Also excludes EVs from the fuel-economy baseline, a departure from Obama/first-Trump/Biden administrations.
- NHTSA estimates the rule cuts average up-front vehicle cost by $930, adds ~100 billion gallons of fuel consumption through 2050, up to $185B in extra fuel costs. (Reuters)
- Brookings critique: the administration's safety case claims freezing standards avoids 12,700 fatalities; of that, vehicle weight accounts for just **160**. The rest comes from the "rebound effect" (cheaper-to-run cars get driven more) and fleet turnover — "new and largely untested modeling," per Brookings.
- SEMA (Dec 2025): confirms crossovers and small SUVs reclassified as passenger vehicles under the proposal; comment period ran to mid-January 2026.

## Limitations (must appear in article)

- FARS `rate` = estimated deaths per 100M VMT; VMT is estimated from NHTS travel survey data, not odometer readings (±15% uncertainty for low-volume models). Relative rankings are more robust than absolutes.
- Rates conflate vehicle physics with driver behavior: crossovers skew family-driven, lower-speed, suburban trips; sedans skew younger, urban, higher-risk exposure. The 10x gap is not proof of 10x better engineering.
- FARS captures only fatal crashes (~40K/yr) — a fraction of ~6M annual crashes. Injury-only outcomes are invisible here.
- The CAFE reclassification is about fuel economy, not safety. Any safety consequence (lighter cars vs. heavier trucks in two-vehicle crashes) is second-order and speculative.
- IIHS research shows mass helps the heavier vehicle's occupants and hurts the lighter vehicle's — the classic incompatibility problem. A rule that pushes the fleet toward lighter passenger cars has real two-vehicle-crash implications, but quantifying it requires fleet-mix modeling, not asserted here.

## Strongest counterargument (must appear at full strength)

The classification isn't supposed to track safety — it's a fuel-economy rule, and the "truck loophole" is exactly the kind of gaming Congress never intended. A RAV4 driven on pavement 100% of the time getting truck-classified mpg targets is regulatory fiction, and Ford's beach-patrol defense of the Explorer is a transparent attempt to keep a family hauler in the laxer bin. Reclassifying crossovers as cars is arguably *correcting* a decades-old cheat, not punishing safety. The FARS gap reflects who drives crossovers, not what the regulation should be.
