The Gap

America's Deadliest Trucks Aren't the Big Ones. They're the Small Ones Nobody Remembers.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 17, 2026

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. Everybody knows big trucks are dangerous, yet the data disagrees, and it is not subtle about it. It turns out the deadliest pickups in America are the small ones, the forgotten compacts, and the gap is wide enough to drive a Silverado through.

Four nameplates tell the story: the Chevrolet S-10 kills at 4.83 deaths per 100 million miles, the Ford Ranger at 2.91, the Dodge Dakota at 2.62, and the GMC Sonoma at 2.13. Together they account for 6,131 deaths in the federal database, and every one of them out-kills the full-size trucks by a margin that should embarrass the industry.[1]

4.6x
The S-10's fatality rate versus the Ford F-150's

For scale, the Ford F-150, the best-selling vehicle in America, sits at 1.04, and the Silverado at 1.25. The S-10's rate is 4.6 times the F-150's and nearly nine times the fleet median, which means the cheapest truck on the used lot is, per mile, one of the most dangerous vehicles you can buy.[1]

Start with the obvious theory: cheap old trucks attract young drivers, rural roads, late nights, bad decisions. Toxicology says otherwise, because driver impairment in fatal crashes runs 17 to 21 percent across the compact pickups and 19 to 21 percent across the full-size trucks, which is to say it is essentially identical.[1] Same drivers, by the only behavioral measure in the dataset, and wildly different body counts, which leaves the trucks holding the bag.

What differs is physics, and IIHS has been saying it for decades: in a two-vehicle crash the lighter vehicle loses, so a compact pickup meeting a full-size Silverado is not a contest.[2] Most of the fleet also predates modern design: the S-10 and Sonoma were gone by 2004 and the Dakota by 2011, which puts it squarely in the pre-stability-control era.[1]

A skeptic will note, correctly, that these are per-mile estimates built on VMT approximations rather than odometer readings. Compact pickups are often second vehicles with low annual mileage, and if the exposure estimates run hot, the rates run hot with them. Age, belt use, road type, and speed are not controlled here either, so some of the gap is surely demographic, but surely not 4.6 times worth.

Here is the practical version: that $4,000 Ranger on the used lot is not a bargain but a 2.8-times-the-F-150 risk profile with a tailgate. If the truck is for a teenager, buy the full-size, get electronic stability control, and run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands.[3] If you already own the small one, drive it like the margin is thin, because it is.
