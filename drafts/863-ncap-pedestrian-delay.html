Your Car's Five Stars Don't Grade How It Treats Pedestrians. That Test Got Pushed to 2027.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

Before you trust the window sticker, you might want to know what the stars don't measure. In December 2024, NHTSA finalized the first major overhaul of its 5-Star Safety Ratings since 1978: pedestrian automatic emergency braking, lane-keeping assist, and blind-spot tech joined the program, plus a crashworthiness pedestrian protection program grading how a vehicle's front end treats the people it hits.[1][2] It was the first time the federal rating would look outside the car. Then the industry asked for a year, and Washington handed it over.

Pedestrians killed in traffic crashes in 2024, 18% of all traffic deaths

Nine months later, NHTSA pushed the package from model year 2026 to 2027, after the Alliance for Automotive Innovation said it needed more time.[3] Read the lobby's complaint carefully: the Alliance didn't say the technology wasn't ready. It said NHTSA hadn't published the updated pedestrian crash test procedures in time, and that few 2026 vehicles would earn five stars under the new timeline.[3] Under NCAP, automakers run the tests themselves to NHTSA's procedures and report the results. You cannot self-certify against a procedure that doesn't exist. So the pedestrian-protection half of the rule is the half that bought the delay.

NHTSA's own statement didn't dispute the characterization: a delay would, in the agency's words to Reuters, "provide industry additional time to implement these technologies."[3] Meanwhile the body count that motivated the rule kept its own schedule. Pedestrian deaths climbed 38 percent between 2015 and 2022, from 5,494 to 7,593, and pedestrians' share of all traffic deaths rose from 15 percent to 18 percent.[7] The 2024 total was the second straight annual decline, down from 7,367 in 2023.[7] Call that progress if you like. It is still 29 percent above 2015, and a pedestrian is still killed every 74 minutes.[7]

This May, the 2026 Tesla Model Y became the first vehicle to pass the new ADAS tests: pedestrian AEB, lane keeping, both blind-spot checks, plus the four legacy categories.[4] Two footnotes. One: the new evaluations are pass/fail checkmarks that do not affect the five-star rating at all.[5] A car can wear five stars and flunk the pedestrian checks, or pass them and the stars won't budge. Two: being "first" is partly an artifact of the delay; it's simply the first vehicle NHTSA formally tested under a framework everyone else got an extra year to ignore.[5] And consider what NHTSA is crashing meanwhile: of the 38 vehicles selected for 2026 five-star testing, 29 are SUVs and exactly two are sedans.[6] The federal test fleet is 76 percent tall, heavy, blunt-nosed vehicles, and the test grading what those front ends do to pedestrians is the one that slipped.

Now the defense, at full strength, because it isn't empty: the Alliance's complaint was procedural and fair. NHTSA had not published the pedestrian test procedures, you cannot test to a spec that doesn't exist, and rushing one would have produced junk ratings. Pedestrian deaths are falling. 2024 was the second straight annual decline, and the first half of 2025 posted the largest drop the Governors Highway Safety Association has ever recorded, down 10.9 percent.[8] The technology isn't waiting on the rule: pedestrian AEB is already standard on the vast majority of new cars, and IIHS already grades pedestrian front crash prevention today. NCAP is a voluntary consumer-information program, not a mandate. A delayed checkmark made nobody less safe. All true.

Also true: information delayed is protection denied to the shopper. Those five stars on the sticker are the most trusted safety signal in American car buying, and they still cannot see a pedestrian. Three caveats, stated plainly: the delay was reported via Reuters and Autoblog, and NHTSA framed it as still implementing the requirements, so check the agency's NCAP pages for final model-year 2027 details.[3] The 7,080 figure describes the year the tougher ratings missed, not deaths caused by the delay. That 76 percent SUV share reflects NHTSA picking high-volume sellers, not agency preference. And this site's FARS extract has no pedestrian split, so the trend numbers are NHTSA's published fact sheet, cited above.

What you can do today starts with the sticker itself: five stars grade occupant crash protection, frontal, side, rollover, not pedestrian protection. Those ADAS checkmarks are a separate pass/fail list that never touches the star count. The pedestrian rating you want already exists at the IIHS: look up your model's pedestrian front crash prevention score, because NHTSA's version won't exist until model year 2027. And drive like the statistics: 76 percent of pedestrian deaths happen in the dark.[7] If your car has pedestrian AEB, make sure it's actually on, and keep the camera and sensor area clean. The test may be delayed. The night is not.
