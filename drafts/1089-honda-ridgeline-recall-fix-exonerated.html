NHTSA Spent Two Years Trying to Prove Honda's Recall Fix Was Broken. The Fix Won.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 7, 2026

The feds do not open defect investigations on vibes. In June 2024, they did exactly that. NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened a file on roughly 129,000 Honda Ridgelines whose backup cameras had already been fixed under a 2022 recall, and the opening memo contained a sentence you almost never see in a federal file: the agency had received zero allegations that the fix was failing, not one, and it investigated anyway, because the replacement harness was built from the same supplier's materials as a harness Honda was recalling on newer trucks at that very moment.[1]

0
Post-remedy failure complaints on file when NHTSA opened its investigation

Start at the beginning: in November 2022, Honda recalled 2017-2019 Ridgelines under campaign 22V-867 because opening and closing the tailgate could break the rearview camera wiring harness. The protective corrugated tubing was too short to survive bending wear, and the zip ties holding it in place were not tight enough to hold anything. Every tailgate cycle flexed the harness a little more until the wires fatigued and the camera went dark. Honda had logged 3,437 warranty claims, and nobody was hurt. Honda's fix: a longer tube and tighter zip ties.[1][3]

Then, in May 2024, Honda recalled 187,290 newer Ridgelines, model years 2020 through 2024, for essentially the same failure. Different supplier, same story: the tailgate harness fatigued and snapped. And here is the part that got NHTSA's attention. Those remedy parts, the ones already installed in the older trucks, shared a supplier and critical materials with the harness being recalled on the newer trucks. In other words, the old fix was made of the same stuff as the new defect.[2]

Read that again, because it is the whole case. So NHTSA opened Recall Query RQ24-011, a "recall remedy review," on pure pattern recognition. In February 2025, after some owners did report post-fix failures, the agency escalated to an Engineering Analysis, EA25-003. That is the last stop before a recall demand. Honda was staring at the prospect of re-recalling 129,000 trucks.[4]

It never happened: on September 22, NHTSA closed the file with no further action. Its Vehicle Research and Test Center had surveyed owners, inspected trucks, and run durability testing on the harness. Result: no camera failures traceable to harness damage. Most of the complaints turned out to be other failure modes entirely. Their conclusion: the repaired part should not fail within a reasonable vehicle lifespan.[4][5]

Now compare that with the other recall remedy under the federal microscope right now. GM's L87 V8 fix is the subject of Engineering Analysis EA26005, covering nearly a million trucks and SUVs, after owners reported engines failing after the recall repair. Same regulatory tool, opposite trajectory: Honda's fix survived two years of suspicion, and GM's is still fighting for its life.[6]

What this means if you own one: drive a 2017-2019 Ridgeline? Punch your VIN into nhtsa.gov/recalls, and if 22V-867 shows complete, stop worrying about the harness. Federal engineers spent two years and a durability lab trying to break it, and it held. If your camera is dead anyway, the odds say it is the camera module or a different failure mode, not the wires. Own a 2020-2024? Check for 24V-321 instead: different harness, different supplier, real recall.[7]

A few honest caveats before anyone frames this. NHTSA never said what "a reasonable vehicle life span" means, and it did not publish its sample sizes. "Most complaints were other failure modes" is not the same as "zero harness failures." If you paid out of pocket for a post-remedy camera repair, this closure does not refund you. An engineering analysis that closes without action is a finding of insufficient evidence, not a certificate of perfection. But as federal verdicts go, this is about as clean as they get: investigated on suspicion, tested on hardware, closed on data.
