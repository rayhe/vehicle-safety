By The Numbers

Nine States Saved 457 Lives. California Gave Back 338 of Them.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 17, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, because the first pass looked like a spreadsheet error. In August 2026, an estimated 3,440 people died on American roads, down 9 percent from the same month last year, and the monthly mileage death rate fell to 1.17 per 100 million miles.[1] That is the third straight August of improvement. The national trend is genuinely good, and one state is ruining the curve.

California added 338 deaths year-to-date, an 18 percent increase, the largest absolute jump of any state in the country.[1] Pennsylvania added 73. The District of Columbia posted the scariest percentage on the board, up 93 percent, which sounds apocalyptic until you learn it means 14 deaths. Percentages are cheap, but bodies are expensive, so here is the ledger in bodies.

74%
Of the nine best states' combined lives saved, erased by California alone

Now the other side of the ledger. Nine states cut deaths by 12 percent or more through August: Hawaii (34 percent, 29 fewer deaths), New Hampshire (20 percent, 18), Illinois (17 percent, 128), Colorado (15 percent, 64), Kansas (15 percent, 36), Oklahoma (15 percent, 62), Idaho (13 percent, 21), Tennessee (12 percent, 79), and Utah (12 percent, 20).[1] Add up everything they saved and you get 457 lives, then divide California's 338 by that number: seventy-four percent, nearly three-quarters of the nine best states' combined progress, gone in a single state.

Put it another way: Illinois was the best in the nation, saving 128 lives, a 17 percent cut. California added back 2.6 times that in the other direction. If you plotted every state's year-to-date change as a bar chart, California's bar would stick out like a redwood in a cornfield, and the chart would need a second axis just to keep the rest of the country legible.

Zoom out and the improvement is real, which makes the outlier more interesting, not less. August's mileage death rate was 1.41 in 2023, then 1.34, then 1.28, now 1.17. That is a 17 percent decline in three years, achieved while Americans drove slightly fewer miles this August than last.[1] NHTSA's early estimates put the first quarter of 2026 at 7,770 deaths and a 0.99 rate, and NSC put the first half at 18,460 deaths, down 2 percent, at a 1.12 rate.[2][3] The direction is not in doubt, though the distribution is.

The fair objections deserve a hearing, because there are several. These are preliminary estimates, and preliminary estimates mature upward as coroners finish their paperwork, so the final numbers will be worse than these. NSC's figures are also not NHTSA's figures: NSC follows the NCHS definition, counting traffic and non-traffic deaths within a year of the incident, while NHTSA counts traffic deaths within 30 days, which means you cannot stack the two agencies' numbers into a single arithmetic.[1] California holds roughly 12 percent of the country's population and an enormous share of its miles, so some of those 338 are exposure rather than danger. And small bases lie for a living: Rhode Island's 42 percent increase is 10 deaths, Alaska's 35 percent is 12, and Wyoming's 13 percent is 10.

What the data does not tell us is why. Nothing in the monthly estimates separates enforcement from engineering, demographics from weather, or policy from luck. Illinois cut 128 deaths and California added 338, and the spreadsheet is silent on the mechanism. Anyone who claims to know the reason from these numbers alone is selling something.

Here is what you can do with it anyway. Nationally, the recipe behind the improvement is unglamorous: slower speeds, seatbelts, sober drivers. Speeding deaths fell an estimated 11 percent in 2025, to 10,035, which suggests enforcement and physics are doing their jobs.[4] If you drive in California, you are in the one large state swimming against that current, so the marginal value of every one of those behaviors is highest exactly where you are. If you make policy anywhere else, Illinois just demonstrated that a 17 percent cut is achievable. Somebody should ask what Illinois did, and then do that.
