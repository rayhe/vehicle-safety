Existential Dread

America's Safest Drivers Are Dying in Record Numbers, and the Problem Isn't Their Driving

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

NHTSA just published its full accounting of older Americans on the road, and the numbers refuse to behave.[1] In 2024, 8,019 people 65 and older were killed in traffic crashes, one in five of all deaths on American roads, and the count is up 29 percent in a decade. That much is grim but familiar, and what follows is not the part anyone expected, because by every behavioral measure the agency tracks, older drivers are the best drivers in the country.

They crash less, speed less, drink less, and buckle up more than any other age group. And they are dying anyway.

15.60
Fatal-crash involvements per 100,000 licensed drivers for drivers 65 and older in 2024, the lowest rate of any age group NHTSA measured.

Start with the scoreboard, because it demolishes the stereotype before the argument even starts. Older drivers were involved in fatal crashes at a rate of 15.60 per 100,000 licensed drivers, lower than every other age group NHTSA measured.[1] Eight percent of them were speeding when they crashed, against 19 percent overall and roughly 30 percent for the youngest drivers. Ten percent had a blood alcohol level of .08 or higher, against 23 percent for drivers 21 to 64. Seventy percent of the older occupants killed were wearing seat belts, against 46 percent of the younger ones. Their crashes happen in daylight, on weekdays, in more than one vehicle, at hours when the roads are full of everyone else's mistakes. If bad driving were the cause, this group would be the last one dying, yet they are the first.

What kills them, then, is two things, and neither one shows up on a driving test, starting with fragility. A 65-year-old's body absorbs a crash the way a 40-year-old's does not, and the fact sheet proves it by accident: older drivers' per-population fatality rate is 13.11 per 100,000, higher than the under-65 rate of 11.13, even though their per-driver crash rate is the lowest on the chart. Same population, safest behind the wheel, most likely to die per capita. Only one mechanism produces both numbers at once, and it is not behavior; it is bone density.

Second, the left turn: older drivers killed in 2024 were 50 percent more likely than younger drivers to die from a left-side impact, 15 percent against 10 percent, and a third of their fatal crashes happened at intersections, against a quarter for drivers 15 to 64.[1] Front impacts still dominate, but the overrepresentation lives on the side, where a driver has to judge the speed of oncoming traffic and commit. Gap judgment degrades before reflexes do, and the unprotected left is where that degradation turns fatal. Fifty-two percent of older drivers killed died on rural roads, the reverse of younger drivers, which fits: rural intersections are faster, emptier, and less forgiving of a misjudged gap.

Then there is the cliff: at 85, the fatality rate hits 17.31 per 100,000 population, the highest of any older subgroup, and it jumped 9.3 percent in a single year while every other older subgroup fell.[1] Men over 85 are involved in fatal crashes at 29.69 per 100,000 licensed drivers, against 10.91 for women the same age. And the sex gap never closes: older men crash at nearly three times the rate of older women per licensed driver, 23.48 against 8.44, a male risk premium that survives into the ninth decade. Meanwhile the danger is not only behind the wheel. Older pedestrian deaths rose 49 percent since 2015, to 1,535, and older male pedalcyclist deaths more than doubled, up 160 percent. The person walking to the store is dying faster than the person driving to it.

Now the counterargument, at full strength, because it is a good one: per-driver rates flatter older drivers, since they drive fewer miles and self-restrict to daytime, weekday, familiar roads. Per mile, IIHS data shows fatal-crash involvement climbing from ages 70 to 74 and peaking at 85 and older.[3] An 85-year-old driving 3,000 miles a year to the grocery store is not safer per mile than a 40-year-old commuting 15,000. Exposure is doing real work in these numbers, and the article's claim is about behavior and per-driver risk, not per-mile risk. Fair. But exposure does not explain the restraint gap, the alcohol gap, or the speeding gap, and it does not explain why the 85-plus rate jumped 9.3 percent in one year. Per-mile risk is worse than per-driver risk, both are true, and neither lets the trend off the hook.

What this does not prove matters too, starting with the fact that these are 2024 Annual Report File numbers, not the finalized file, so counts may shift slightly. Restraint percentages exclude cases where belt use was unknown. State shares like North Dakota's 23.8 percent reflect small denominators and older state populations. The 8,019 count is the highest in the fact sheet's 2015-2024 window, not a claim about all of highway history. And the 2025 follow-through is no comfort: NHTSA's sub-category estimates show every age group fell in 2025 except the 65-plus group, which was flat at zero percent change, while its share of all deaths rose to 21.9 percent.[4] The problem is not resolving. It is compounding, one birthday at a time.

What to do with this depends on which side of the conversation you are on. If your parent is 75 or older and still driving, the risk is not speeding or drinking; it is the unprotected left turn. Route planning beats lecturing: right-turn-only routes, avoiding unprotected lefts across fast traffic, and daylight driving are worth more than another talk about speed. When shopping for their car, side-impact protection and automatic braking with intersection support matter more than horsepower, because the left-side impact is the crash that overrepresents them. Have the driving conversation before 85, not after, because the 9.3 percent jump says the cliff arrives fast. And if your parent walks or cycles, that matters as much as the driving: older pedestrian deaths up 49 percent in a decade is a walking problem, not a driving problem, and reflective gear plus intersection timing belong in the same conversation as the keys.
