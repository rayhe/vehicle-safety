Three of Four New SUVs Hit the Car Pulling Out of a Side Street. All Four Aced the Test That Counts.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

AAA put four 2026 SUVs on a closed course in November and ran their intersection automatic emergency braking through the scenarios it is supposed to handle.[1] Every one of them passed the standardized left-turn test, the one regulators actually grade. Then three of the four drove straight into a car pulling out of a side street, in every single run. Thirteen collisions in sixteen runs, and all three cars that avoided a crash were the same car.

Collisions in AAA’s turn-into-path runs. All three avoids came from one vehicle, the Tesla Model Y.

Start with what the test actually was, because the setup is the story. AAA partnered with the Automobile Club of Southern California’s Automotive Research Center and tested four 2026 models with intersection AEB as standard equipment: a Chevrolet Equinox, a Ford Explorer, a Hyundai Tucson, and a Tesla Model Y.[1] Seventy-four closed-course runs in daylight, against a robotic soft-car target, across four scenarios. In the first, the test vehicle drove straight at 30 mph while the soft car turned right into its path from a perpendicular road at 5 mph, which is roughly how fast a car pulls out of a strip-mall exit into traffic doing the speed limit. That is the turn-into-path scenario, and it is where the test fell apart.[1][3]

Only the Model Y stopped. It avoided contact in all five of its runs, stopping an average of 1.60 meters short of the target, a little over five feet.[5] Equinox drivers got a warning in four of five runs and braking in one, and the car hit the target all five times anyway. Explorer and Tucson gave their drivers nothing at all, no warning and no braking, and hit the target in all three of their valid runs before AAA stopped testing those configurations early to avoid damaging the equipment.[3] Five planned runs per vehicle per configuration, three runs minimum before AAA calls it, and two of the four cars could not produce a single warning in three tries. That is not a marginal result. That is a system that did not see the car.

Now run the same four cars through the scenario the industry grades, and watch the story invert. In the standardized Euro NCAP left-turn-across-path test, the test vehicle turns left at 9.3 mph across an oncoming target doing 18.6 mph, turn signal on, steering robot at the wheel, and all four vehicles warned, braked, and avoided the collision in every run.[1] Same cars, same lab, same week: zero collisions in the graded scenario, thirteen in the ungraded one. A safety rating that only measures the scenario everyone already passes is a participation trophy with a crash-test dummy attached.

AAA then changed one thing at a time, and the second change is the one that should worry you most. With the turn signal off, three cars still avoided the oncoming target, but the Tucson gave no warning and no braking and hit it in every run.[1] Signal on, the Tucson avoids the turn every time. Signal off, it does nothing at all. One in four tested systems apparently keys its intersection logic off the driver’s own blinker, which means its competence is conditional on a human remembering to signal, and the whole point of automatic braking is the moments when the human does not do the thing. With a human driver steering instead of the robot, all four cars avoided the turn, though the Tucson’s margin was eighteen inches and its warning came an average of 0.74 seconds before impact.[5]

Give the industry its due, because the progress since AAA’s last look at this is real, and it is also narrow. In 2022, AAA tested four vehicles with standard AEB that did not include manufacturer-identified intersection functionality, and in two common intersection scenarios the systems provided no alerts and no automatic braking in any run.[1] Four years later, all four cars warn and brake in the standardized left turn. That is genuine improvement. It is also improvement confined to the scenario with a grade attached, while the scenario nobody grades is still three-of-four failing. AAA’s own researchers put it plainly: system robustness should not depend on protocol-specific conditions such as turn signal activation or idealized steering inputs.[3] Greg Brannon, AAA’s director of automotive engineering, said the quiet part out loud too: AEB is making progress in the limited intersection scenarios it is tested to handle, and unfortunately, performance outside those scenarios is spotty.[1]

None of this is an accident of engineering. It is an accident of regulation, or rather of the absence of it. American vehicle-to-vehicle AEB test protocols have historically focused on rear-end crashes. Euro NCAP added a limited set of intersection tests in 2020, the left turn across oncoming traffic, and that is the test everyone passes. NHTSA developed a draft Intersection Safety Assist test procedure in 2019 for research purposes, and seven years later no final test standard has been implemented.[1] Twelve days after AAA released this study, NHTSA proposed delaying the AEB mandate, FMVSS 127, from 2029 to 2031, citing test data showing current systems struggle in high-speed and low-light scenarios.[7] Worth noting: FMVSS 127 covers rear-end and pedestrian AEB, not intersection scenarios at all. The mandate being delayed is about the scenarios that are already graded, while the scenario that failed three of four cars is graded by nobody, and the draft procedure that would have graded it has sat unfinished since 2019.

Fairness requires the other side of this story, and it is not small. Four vehicles, daylight only, closed course, soft targets, one lab. This is not a brand ranking, and it is not a fleet-wide verdict. Tesla’s win is a single data point, and its system carries its own documented false-positive tradeoff, phantom braking, that AAA did not test here. Turn-into-path is hard by design, a car pulling out at 5 mph into traffic doing 30, and plenty of human drivers would fail it too; the claim is about the gap between a window sticker that says intersection AEB and performance in a scenario nobody grades, not about perfection. Equinox deserves credit for warning in four of five runs, because a warning can still help an attentive driver. And standardized tests exist for a reason, repeatability, which is why AAA’s argument is to broaden the protocols rather than scrap them. All of that is true. It is also true that the stakes here are not theoretical: intersections account for roughly a quarter of American traffic fatalities and about half of all traffic injuries, and in 2022 that meant 12,036 intersection deaths out of 42,514 total.[6]

What this study does not prove matters as much as what it does. AAA tested in daylight, so it says nothing about night intersections, which is the other gap NHTSA cited when it proposed the FMVSS 127 delay. It says nothing about false positives, nothing about rain, and nothing about the many other models on sale with intersection AEB that were not in the test. What it does prove is narrower and harder to dismiss: the four newest SUVs from four major brands were put through the intersection test the industry grades and the intersection test it does not, and the difference between passing and failing was which test AAA ran. If you drive one of these cars, the practical advice is unglamorous and free. Cover the brake at intersections, especially when a car could pull out of a side street. Signal your left turns, because one tested system apparently needs the blinker to know a turn is happening. And if you are shopping, ask what scenarios the intersection braking was tested in, because the sticker will not tell you, and the graded answer is not the whole answer.[1]
