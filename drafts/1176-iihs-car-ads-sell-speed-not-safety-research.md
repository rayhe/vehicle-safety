## Kill Test

**Angle in one sentence:** Automakers spent 2018-2022 systematically deleting safety from their advertising while cranking speed messaging up; safety mentions fell 72% in four years, and the industry spends more airtime selling you the exact behavior that kills 29% of everyone who dies on US roads.

**Challenge:** "Stale (May 2026), just a media study, no causal link." Verdict: PROCEED. The counterargument IS the story: IIHS itself admits no proven causal link, which makes the cultural framing stronger, not weaker — the industry sells speed with no evidence it's safe and no evidence the ads don't kill. Plus: genuinely new data the site never touched, with a regulatory comparison (UK/France already ban this; US broadcasters can't even define "risky behavior") and a paradox (sedans are dying as a segment but 47% of their 2020 ads sold speed; traction messaging = off-road fantasy viewers will never live but rainy-day driving they will).

## Key Data (from IIHS release, verified on iihs.org 2026-10-15)

- Sample: 1,500+ TV ads (2018/2020/2022) + 1,000+ internet/social ads (2020/2022). 23 themes. 10 coders, UVA media studies, 3-hr training, 600 ads each. Weighted by Nielsen Ad Intel spend (heavy-rotation ads count more).
- 43% of ads = performance themes (speed, maneuverability, traction, stopping, power). 8% = safety. Ratio: **5.4x**.
- Speed/speeding sub-theme: ~16% overall (CarBuzz: 16.3%); trailing family functionality (28.1%), comfort (24.3%), escape (22.3%), styling (21.5%). Incentives/sale price top at 40.5% (ungrouped).
- Trend 2018->2022: speed share 14%->19% (+36%); traction 20%->38% (+90%); **safety 11%->3% (-73%)**.
- SUV performance-themed ads: 28%->45% (2018->2022).
- Sedan speed/speeding themes in 2020: **47%** of sedan ads vs 11% SUV, 5% pickup.
- Traction ads: <1 in 10 also coded for safety. Traction = dust clouds on dirt tracks, beach runs, boulder crawling. Viewers will never dune-bash but will drive in rain.
- Body count: 11,288 speed-related deaths in 2024 = 29% of all US road deaths.
- History: 1990 Super Bowl Nissan 300ZX Turbo ad condemned by IIHS ("blatant disregard for public safety"), pulled. 1998 IIHS TV analysis: performance in ~half of all auto ads. Chevy C6 Corvette ad also pulled after outcry.
- Regulation: UK prohibits ads encouraging dangerous driving, restricts power/acceleration/handling messaging unless safety-context (swerving to avoid crash). France: car ads must promote walking/safety and less driving. US: broadcasters self-regulate; ViacomCBS/Paramount banned "risky behavior portrayed positively" with NO definition of risky behavior; ABC required "safe and lawful driving" with no mention of speed; NBCUniversal required seatbelts but ignored speed.
- Quotes: Harkey — "Automakers and broadcasters need to start treating unsafe speed the same way they would drunk driving or failure to use a seat belt." Woods — "Advertising like this has helped normalize speeding, masking how dangerous it is. Just think about how different attitudes are toward speeding versus impaired driving."

## Original Contribution (mine, not IIHS's)

1. **The 73% erasure vs the 36% surge:** compute the divergence rate — safety mentions fell ~3.7 percentage points while speed mentions rose ~5 points; the ratio of performance-to-safety messaging went from ~4:1 to ~14:1 (43/3 approx, if endpoints). Actually careful: 43% is the full-period average. Endpoint calc: unknown total performance share in 2022. Don't overclaim; report what IIHS gives.
2. **Tension with 2025 data:** NHTSA's July 2026 Crash-Stats early estimates show speeding-related fatalities FELL 11% in 2025 even as the ad culture IIHS measured kept selling speed — the cultural lag question: if ads normalize speeding, why did speeding deaths drop? (Honest: I don't know; present as open.)
3. **The sedan paradox:** sedans as a market segment are dying, yet 47% of 2020 sedan ads sold speed — the segment with the least future got the most speed messaging; and IIHS notes sedan-ad speed depictions influence ALL drivers, not just sedan buyers.
4. **Traction as the laundering mechanism:** the biggest performance sub-theme (27.5% per CarBuzz) is framed as capability, never safety — the exact feature that saves you in rain gets sold as a reason to drive into a river.

## Limitations

- IIHS study covers 2018-2022 only; no data on 2023-2026 ads. Can't claim the trend continued.
- No causal link between ad exposure and individual speeding behavior (IIHS and critics both admit this).
- Coding is interpretive (UVA coders, 23 themes) — "performance" is a constructed bucket; incentives (40.5%) dwarf everything when ungrouped.
- FARS 2014-2023 embedded data is occupant-focused; speeding-death figures come from IIHS/NHTSA, not my own FARS cut.

## Strongest Counterargument (full strength)

CarBuzz/Carscoops: (a) no established causal link — the study "could happen or be happening"; (b) traction/maneuverability/stopping ARE safety features — a driver who swerves around a drunk or brakes for a child benefits from exactly the capabilities the study codes as "performance"; (c) road design, not ads, is the proven driver-behavior lever (people drive the speed roads feel designed for; IIHS itself backs Safe System road redesign); (d) buyers self-select — performance-car buyers speed for their own reasons; (e) the biggest ad theme is sale prices (40.5%), not speed. All true. The article must concede: ads are downstream of demand and upstream of culture, and the road-design lever is bigger. The claim is normalization, not causation.

## Actionable Insights

- When you watch a car ad, notice what it sells: if it shows dust clouds and canyon carving, that's marketing, not a capability briefing — and the same AWD system is what keeps you alive in rain (use it for that).
- Speeding kills 29% of road deaths (11,288 in 2024); treat "professional driver, closed course" fine print as what it is — an admission the depicted behavior is illegal where you drive.
- Policy: the UK already restricts this; the US lets broadcasters define (or not define) "risky behavior." If this bothers you, that's a regulatory gap with a working foreign model, not a mystery.
- Check your own speedometer habits: the cultural baseline has moved; cruise control is the cheapest countermeasure.
