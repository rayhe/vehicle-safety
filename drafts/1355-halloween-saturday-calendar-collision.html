Sobriety Report

Saturday + Halloween = 45. Do the Math.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 1, 2026

According to the toxicology reports – and there are a lot of them – last night was the worst night of the year to be on the road, and the calendar told us in advance. Halloween fell on a Saturday, the first time since 2020. Saturday accounts for more than 22 percent of annual alcohol-impaired fatal crash deaths, the highest share of any day of the week.[2] Halloween night already averages 29 drunk-driving deaths. Compound those two risks and you get an expected 45 deaths in a single Saturday Halloween night, before a single costume went on.

45
Estimated drunk-driving deaths on a Saturday Halloween night, vs 29 on an average Halloween night (NHTSA 2019–2024, author calculation)

Raw materials first, because this column runs on arithmetic, not vibes. Between 2019 and 2024, 176 people died on Halloween night in crashes with a driver at or above 0.08 BAC, plus 25 more at lower alcohol levels.[3] Fifty-four percent of the 21-to-34-year-olds killed on Halloween night died in drunk-driving crashes. Thirty-one of the victims were pedestrians. AAA found that half of 2023’s Halloween-night fatalities were alcohol-related.[2] And 2023 was a Tuesday. A Tuesday.

Now the state board, which is where the sports-stats framing pays off. Florida attorney Andrew Pickett’s analysis of NHTSA data put Kentucky at 9.1 fatal crashes per million residents on October 31 – 134.6 percent above the national average, worst in the country four years running, while Vermont recorded zero.[4] A separate FARS analysis by FLBR Motorsport ranked Wyoming, Maine, Rhode Island, Alabama, and Kentucky the five deadliest Halloween states, and found Rhode Island’s deadliest single hour was 1:00 to 1:59 a.m.[5] Not trick-or-treat o’clock. Party o’clock. Kentucky’s own highway safety office logged 4,013 impaired-driver crashes last year – 1,887 injuries, 136 deaths – and warned that in the week around Halloween alone, 84 alcohol-involved collisions killed two and injured 30.[6]

Show the math. Baseline: 176 deaths across six Halloween nights (2019–2024) is 29.3 per night at 0.08+. Saturday effect: 22 percent of annual alcohol-impaired deaths land on Saturdays versus 14.3 percent under a uniform week, a 1.54x multiplier. 29.3 × 1.54 = 45.1. Cross-check against AAA: if a Tuesday Halloween already runs ~50 percent alcohol-involved, multiplying by the Saturday factor lands in the same neighborhood.

Now the part where the columnist eats his own cooking. FARS will not have 2026 data for well over a year, so 45 is a projection with wide error bars, not a body count. The 22 percent Saturday share comes from a single secondary report of NHTSA data. The 2019–2024 window contains exactly one Saturday Halloween – 2020, a COVID year when driving patterns went sideways. And the strongest case against the whole premise: Halloween’s real killer may be the pedestrian surge, with 42 years of federal data showing 43 percent higher pedestrian death risk that night and tenfold risk for kids aged 4 to 8,[7] peaking at 5–8 p.m., hours before the bars empty. The calendar coincidence might just be a statistical curiosity. We will know in about 18 months, when 2026 FARS closes.

Actionable version, since outrage without a to-do list is noise. If you are 21 to 34, read that 54 percent figure twice; your age bracket is the modal victim of Halloween drunk driving, not its audience. The 1–2 a.m. window is the deadliest hour – the party ends at midnight, the body count starts at one. Kentucky drivers: your state leads the country on this specific night, four years running; plan like it. And for every Halloween yet to come, the cheapest insurance policy is a rideshare receipt, because a DUI conviction runs your premiums 40 to 80 percent higher for years and the surge pricing never looked that expensive again.
