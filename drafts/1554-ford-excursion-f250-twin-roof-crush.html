Same Truck, 4x the Deaths: The Excursion's Roof Was Never Tested

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

From the B-pillars forward, a Ford Excursion is an F-250 Super Duty: same chassis, same front suspension, same engines, built on the same Kentucky Truck Plant line.[3] In FARS data from 2014 through 2023, the pickup kills at 0.43 deaths per 100 million miles, while its SUV twin kills at 1.65, which means same bones and nearly four times the bodies.[1]

3.8x
Excursion death rate vs. the F-250 Super Duty it shares a chassis with (1.65 vs. 0.43 per 100M miles)

Roof strength is where a wagon body earns its keep, and it is exactly where Ford stopped testing. Rafael Trejo died in a 2000 Excursion rollover when the roof crushed and pinned his neck, and at trial Ford conceded something remarkable: it had never run a single physical roof-crush test on the Excursion, relying instead on a 2002 computer simulation borrowed from Super Duty pickup modeling.[2]

Numbers from that trial deserve a slow read, because Ford's own internal guideline demanded a roof strength-to-weight ratio of 1.725 for vehicles under 8,500 pounds, while the Excursion measured 1.25, falling to 0.79 if the windows broke in the rollover and stopped bracing the roof, which means the roof could not support even the weight of the vehicle sitting on it. Ford had no internal guideline whatsoever for vehicles over 8,500 pounds, and the Excursion's gross vehicle weight rating was 8,600, so the biggest SUV Ford ever built was too heavy for Ford's own safety rulebook.[2]

Federal law had the same hole. Before FMVSS 216a was finalized in 2009, the roof-crush standard simply did not cover vehicles over 6,000 pounds GVWR, and production of the Excursion ended in 2005, four years before the upgraded rule extended coverage to 10,000 pounds at a 1.5-times threshold, which means that for its entire life the largest SUV ever sold in North America was exempt from the roof standard by weight class.[4]

Occupancy multiplies the damage, because nine seats mean nine people inside when things go wrong, and FARS bears that out: each fatal Excursion crash kills 0.58 people on average, against 0.36 for the F-250. Before reaching for the easy explanation, check the toxicology, where Excursion drivers in fatal crashes test impaired 17.5 percent of the time, below the 19.5 percent SUV average, which means sober drivers are dying in a vehicle whose roof was never tested.[1]

Fairness demands the counterargument at full strength: F-250s are mostly work trucks driven by professionals in daylight, while Excursions haul families on weekends with kids aboard, so some of the gap is exposure and occupancy rather than engineering, and the Excursion's tiny surviving fleet of 87,500 makes its rate estimate noisier than the F-250's. All true, but the courtroom record does not depend on the statistics, since Ford's own engineers measured the roof at 1.25 against a 1.725 guideline, expert testimony put a fix at $70 in production costs and 70 pounds of steel, a jury found the roof defective, and the Nevada Supreme Court affirmed.[2]

Every Excursion on the road today is at least 21 years old, and 181 people have died in them over the last decade, about one every 20 days, in a vehicle that has not been built since 2005, with no stability control ever offered and no modern roof standard ever applied to it. If you own one, belt every seat, all nine, because belts are what keep occupants out of the crush zone when a roof gives way, and if you are shopping for a cheap big used hauler, a same-era Expedition or Tahoe at least lived under a roof rule, so check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls either way.[1]
