Ford Filed 146 Recalls in 2025. The Backup Camera Got Recalled Twice.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

I ran the numbers twice, and they didn't get better. In calendar year 2025, Ford Motor Company filed 146 separate vehicle recall campaigns with NHTSA, covering 12.9 million potential units.[1] That is one recall every two and a half days, the most campaigns any automaker has ever filed in a single year, and it more than doubled Ford's own 2024 total of 62. The previous record holder was General Motors in 2014, the ignition-switch year, with 77 campaigns, and Ford nearly doubled that too.

Vehicle recall campaigns Ford filed in 2025, per NHTSA's official flat file. Next-busiest automaker: Chrysler, at 41.

Start with the backup camera, because Ford did. Three of the 146 campaigns, covering nearly 4 million vehicles, were about the rearview camera failing to show you what's behind the truck. Campaign 25V572000 (1.46 million units): the camera can display a distorted, inverted, or blank image.[3] Campaign 25V695000 (1.45 million): same symptom, different model years. Campaign 25V315000 (1.08 million): a software error that makes the camera image delay, freeze, or vanish entirely. And 25V572000 contains my favorite line in the entire file: "This recall expands previous NHTSA recall number 25V270." Read that again. Ford recalled the same cameras twice in one calendar year. Ten of the 146 campaigns carry backup-camera component codes. The most recalled part on the most recalled automaker's most recalled year is the thing that shows you where you're going backwards.

Zoom out and the machine's shape gets weird. Of the 146 campaigns, 105 were defect recalls and 41 were FMVSS noncompliance filings, the paperwork-grade kind. Ford initiated 121 of the 146 voluntarily; only 23 came after NHTSA's defect office got involved. This is not a company being dragged to the altar; it is a company that files. Software is eating the defect mix: the 1.08-million-unit camera-software recall and a 356,000-unit instrument-cluster recall both fix over the air, yet only 8 of the 146 campaigns mention an OTA remedy at all. The recall system was built for wrenches. Ford's defect list was increasingly built for downloads. And the year-over-year curve is a hockey stick with no blade: 53 campaigns in 2021, 67 in 2022, 58 in 2023, 62 in 2024, 146 in 2025. For perspective, the next-busiest automaker in 2025 was Chrysler at 41 campaigns; Toyota filed 15.

Steel-manning the other side, because the numbers demand it. Campaigns are not vehicles: one F-150 can sit in the camera, software, fuel-pump, and brake-hose campaigns simultaneously, so 146 filings overstates the number of distinct broken things. "Potential units" is the maximum exposed population, not the defect rate; most recalled trucks are fine. Ford's count partly measures the industry's most aggressive self-reporting culture, and a quieter automaker with identical defects would file fewer campaigns without having fewer problems. And on raw exposure, GM's 2014 still dwarfs this: 77 campaigns, 26.5 million units, more than double Ford's 12.9 million. What Ford set is a record for filing frequency, not for scale of harm.

If you own a Ford, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls first, because recalls now stack on the same vehicle and multiple open campaigns on one truck is the norm, not the exception. Turn on automatic software updates; an overnight download already fixes the camera-software and instrument-cluster recalls, and it is the fastest-growing remedy in the file. If you're buying a used Ford, run the VIN before you sign and ask specifically whether the software recalls were applied, since OTA fixes leave no dealer service record to inspect. A filing lands every two and a half days, so check more often than that.
