The Gap

In the Year Everything Got Safer, SUV Deaths Went Up

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

Good news first, because there is plenty of it. American roads got safer for the third straight year in 2024: 39,254 people died, down 1,771 from 2023, and the fatality rate fell to 1.19 per 100 million miles.[1] Passenger-car occupant deaths fell 9.7%, pickup deaths fell 4.9%, and van deaths fell 5.7%. Speeding deaths fell 5.2%, drunk-driving deaths fell 3.9%, pedestrian deaths fell 3.9%, and nearly every line in NHTSA's annual report points the same direction, except one.

+112
SUV occupant deaths in 2024 vs. 2023 (6,901 to 7,013), the only occupant class in the entire NHTSA report with more deaths than the year before

SUV occupant deaths rose from 6,901 to 7,013 while everything else fell, and the injuries went the same way: 723,240 SUV occupants hurt in 2024, up 28,952 from the year before, while car occupants injured fell 4.6% and pickup occupants injured fell 9.2%, both statistically significant drops.[1] SUVs now account for 30.9% of all passenger-vehicle occupant deaths, up from 28.7% a year earlier. That is 19 SUV occupants dead every day, in the year the national body count shrank by 1,771.

Before anyone starts shopping for a sedan on the strength of this paragraph: per mile, SUVs are still the safer bet, and it is not close. In this site's own 2014–2023 FARS data, SUVs kill at 5.47 deaths per 100 million miles against 13.92 for sedans.[2] The story is not that SUVs are dangerous. The story is that the safest-per-mile class is the only one whose body count is still growing, and the likeliest explanation is also the most boring one: America keeps buying SUVs, roughly half of every new light vehicle sold, so SUV miles keep rising while car miles shrink, and a rising denominator drags the death count up with it even if every individual SUV gets safer. NHTSA's report does not publish miles traveled by vehicle type, so that explanation cannot be confirmed or ruled out from this data alone, which is exactly the kind of gap that should make a reader suspicious of any clean moral drawn from one table.

Still, two details in the same report refuse to sit quietly inside the exposure story. First, the impairment numbers: SUV drivers in fatal crashes who were drunk fell just 0.6% in 2024, from 2,641 to 2,624, while passenger-car drunk drivers fell 5.0%. Whatever is getting safer about American driving is retreating slowest exactly where deaths are rising.[1] Second, the injury side. Fatalities are a census, a hard count, but injuries are survey estimates, and NHTSA flagged the car and pickup injury drops as statistically significant while it did not flag the SUV injury increase, so the +4.2% on injuries carries more uncertainty than the +112 on deaths. Read the death count as fact and the injury count as a nudge in the same direction.

What to do with it: if you are shopping for a family hauler, buy the specific model, not the class average: an IIHS Top Safety Pick+ with a good moderate-overlap rear-seat score beats a big badge every time. If you already drive an SUV, the two levers that actually move your risk are the same as everywhere else, and the 2024 report states them bluntly: 48% of passenger-vehicle occupants killed last year were unrestrained, and the drunk-driving problem is shrinking slowest in your vehicle class.[1] Belt on, sober up, and check any VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before signing, because a 2024 SUV is now a two-year-old used car and the recall cycle is already running.
