Your Nissan Kicks Is Safe. Unless It Was Built Before July.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 31, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. On October 22, IIHS released its updated moderate-overlap crash test results for six small crossovers, and the winner was the cheapest car in the room: the $22,730 Nissan Kicks earned a Good rating for rear-seat protection, while the $43,700 Audi Q3 earned a Poor.[1][2] But the headline buried the real story. Nissan only earned that rating by quietly changing the rear seat belts on Kicks models built after June 2026. Buy one built in May, and you own the Poor version. Same nameplate, same window sticker, different car.[3]

Here is what Nissan actually did. The redesigned Kicks debuted for the 2025 model year and flunked the test IIHS toughened in 2022 by adding a rear-seat dummy, with scoring tightened again in 2024 for rear seat belt force. Nissan revised the rear seat belts and added load limiters, and only units built after June 2026 carry the new hardware. IIHS retested those, scored them Good, and handed the Kicks a 2026 Top Safety Pick. It missed the higher Top Safety Pick+ on a Marginal vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention score. All of it happened without a word to buyers: no recall, no retrofit, no letter. June's buyers got a Poor back seat and a shrug.[3][4]

Audi ran the same play and face-planted. The Q3's rear belts were modified after May 2026, yet the dummy still submarined: the lap belt slipped off the pelvis onto the abdomen, the shoulder belt rode toward the neck, moderate chest-injury risk.[8] Audi scored Good in every other category and still walked away with nothing, because Good in this test is mandatory for any award. Two automakers, two mid-year belt fixes, two opposite results. And because these are running production changes rather than recalls, early owners of both cars keep the worse hardware permanently. Your protection level is set by the month your car was welded together.[4][5]

Now the part that makes the data people uncomfortable. Our FARS 2014-2023 numbers give the Poor-rated Trailblazer a fatality rate of 2.83 per 100 million vehicle miles, 14 times the RAV4's 0.19 and four times the Kicks' 0.69.[6] Sounds damning, except the Trailblazer number is a ghost: it is dominated by the 2002-2009 body-on-frame truck (430 deaths in 2003 alone), while the IIHS Poor belongs to the unrelated 2021+ crossover (9 to 11 deaths a year). Same nameplate, two different vehicles, one misleading statistic. And the sharper edge: the real-world morgue says the merely Acceptable RAV4 kills at 0.19 per 100M miles, 3.6 times fewer than the Good-rated Kicks at 0.69. The lab grades one 40-mph scenario with dummies while the field grades everything else, and neither is the whole story.

What this article did not prove: which VINs carry the revised Kicks hardware beyond the June 2026 cutoff, whether Audi will attempt a second fix, or how many early-build owners are affected. FARS rates cover prior generations, and IIHS results are lab evaluations with no exposure denominator, drawn from a single six-vehicle test round. The strongest case against my thesis is real: a Good rear-seat rating does not make the Kicks the safest car here. Buy on the field data first, the lab score second.

The advice here is concrete, starting with anyone shopping a 2026 or 2027 Kicks. Open the driver's door and read the build-date sticker on the jamb: 07/26 or later gets the revised belts. A 2026 built in May is the Poor car wearing the same badge. Shopping used? Ratings now follow build dates, not model years, so verify the sticker before the price. RAV4 shoppers: the hybrid's Acceptable beats the plug-in's Marginal, and the PHEV's extra battery weight is the likely reason the lap belt slid onto the dummy's abdomen. And if you bought a Kicks or Q3 before the cutoff, call your dealer and ask about a retrofit. Expect silence. There is no recall, because nothing is technically broken. That is precisely the problem.[1]
