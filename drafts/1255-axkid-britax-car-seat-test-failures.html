Both Car Seats Passed Every Law. Then the Crash Test Threw the Dummies Out.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 23, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. The sled decelerates, the seat pitches forward, and every restraint component either does its job or it doesn't. There is no partial credit. This month, a European testing consortium strapped two leading-brand child seats to a sled, and both failed so badly the dummies came loose.[1]

1 click
The Britax buckle held when upright, and failed when reclined. One recline setting is the difference between restrained and ejected.

The Axkid Minikid Core is an extended rearward-facing seat, the kind Scandinavian safety nerds love. With a 6-year-old dummy aboard, the support leg, the steel strut that anchors the seat to the car floor, retracted mid-impact. In both upright and reclined positions. A support leg is the load path for a rear-facing seat in a frontal crash. When it collapses, the whole assembly travels forward uncontrolled. In the reclined test, the harness tore away from the seat shell. Which? looked at that and said: stop using it immediately.[1]

The Britax Römer Baby-Safe Pro is worse in a sneakier way. Rearward-facing, reclined on its ISOFIX Vario Base 5Z, with an 18-month-old dummy: the harness buckle popped open and the dummy came free. Upright on the same base, it held. Installed with the car's adult seatbelt and no base, it held. Same seat, same harness, same child. One recline click is the difference between restrained and ejected, and nothing about a recline switch looks like a structural decision. Britax says the buckle is an industry-standard part from a common supplier, used by multiple manufacturers. If the buckle is the fault rather than the seat geometry, this failure may be sitting in other brands' carriers right now, untested in this configuration.[2]

Both seats were fully legal, which is the part that infuriates me. Axkid says the Minikid Core passed all required approvals, and that its own standards exceed the law. It passed the law and failed a realistic crash. The consortium's tests, derived from Euro NCAP scenarios with higher realistic loads, are tougher than the R129 regulations. Seats get designed to the regulation, not to the crash. In the US the floor is thinner still: FMVSS 213 certifies restraints on a 30 mph frontal test, with new side-impact and frontal standards only phasing in through the end of this year. Legal is the floor, not the ceiling, and the floor is thin.[1][6]

Both brands issued voluntary recalls. Axkid will replace every Minikid Core free of charge, while also telling parents they may keep using the seat while waiting for the replacement, because it "still performs well in legal requirements." The side that ran the test and watched the harness leave the shell gets the benefit of the doubt: stop now. Britax, after first saying its own tests showed no abnormalities, recalled Baby-Safe Pro units manufactured between 26 December 2025 and 30 September 2026, and is building a dedicated recall site.[1] For the Britax, the testing says upright-on-base and belt-installed are safe configurations; reclined on the Vario Base 5Z is not. Flip the shell, read the production-date label on the bottom, and check whether your unit falls in the recall window. A passing regulatory mark is a receipt, not a guarantee. This month the receipt got checked against reality, and two of the best names in the business came up short.
