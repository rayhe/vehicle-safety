Existential Dread

Construction Fleets Watch 92% of Their Trucks. Their Drivers Speed 5x Worse Than Truckers Anyway.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
June 30, 2027

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. That truck in the lane next to you is almost certainly being watched — cameras, GPS, accelerometers logging every twitch of the wheel, every stab at the throttle. And it changes nothing. A new industry report tracking 500 million miles of commercial driving found that construction fleets, which monitor roughly 92% of their vehicles, produce 254 severe speeding events per 100,000 miles. Long-haul transportation fleets: 48. Same cameras. Same sensors. Five times the restraint.

5.3x
How much more often construction fleets trigger severe speeding alerts than transportation fleets, per 100,000 miles — with 92% of their vehicles monitored

Somewhere along the way we confused visibility with control. Ninety percent of commercial fleets now run telematics, and the adoption curve has flatlined — only 5% signed up in the past year, because there is nobody left to sign up. Sensors worked. Data flowed. Then 53% of fleet managers looked at the mountain of behavioral data and confessed they had no idea what to do with it. So much for the all-seeing eye: it surveyed 135 million driver behavior events, and blinked. Speeding alone accounts for 73% of everything the sensors catch, which means the industry spent a decade building a machine that mostly reports one finding: everyone is speeding.

The speeding clusters exactly where the dread lives. Forty-one percent of severe speeding events — defined as 21 mph over the limit or faster than 85 — land between 6 a.m. and noon. That's 41% of the events crammed into a quarter of the day, during your commute, when the highways fill with work trucks racing dispatch windows. Faced with its own paralysis, the industry hired software to do the reading: 60% of fleets and 94% of insurers now run AI over the data they couldn't act on. We built the cameras, drowned in the footage, and handed the remote to the robot. Omniscience achieved. Obedience pending.

Buried in the same report is the sentence that should worry everyone who isn't a fleet manager. Eighty-four percent of insurers now rank distracted driving as a top threat to commercial auto profitability, up ten points in a year — putting a phone in a driver's hand on the same threat level as litigation and the dreaded nuclear verdict. The people who price risk for a living have decided that distraction is discussed in the same breath as nine-figure jury awards. Meanwhile the trucks keep doing 21-over at dawn, watched by everyone, corrected by no one. We're all going to die, but statistically some of us sooner — and the sensors will have a beautiful, high-resolution record of exactly how fast.

So here is the morning-commute survival guide, courtesy of the industry's own confessional data. First, the 6 a.m. to noon window is when heavy commercial vehicles are statistically at their fastest — leave extra following distance around work trucks and dump trucks, and don't camp in their blind spots. Your car loses the physics argument against 80,000 pounds at 21 over, every time. Second, if a commercial truck ever hits you, remember that its telematics record almost certainly exists: tell your insurer or attorney to request preservation of that data immediately, before it gets overwritten. It logs speed, location, and behavior events second by second. Third, the same monitoring tech the fleets can't act on can pay you — usage-based insurance programs reward drivers who actually slow down, so if you drive clean, stop leaving that discount on the table.
