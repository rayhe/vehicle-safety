The School Bus Is the Safest Vehicle in America. The Most Dangerous Place Is Standing Next to It.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 4, 2026

In July, NHTSA published its ten-year review of fatal school-transportation crashes, and every newsroom printed the reassuring part. Between 2015 and 2024, 962 crashes killed 1,069 people: three-tenths of one percent of the 358,460 fatal crashes nationwide in that span.[1] School buses: still the safest ride in the country. Now read the part nobody printed, because only 119 of the dead, eleven percent, were on the bus.

Seventy percent of the decade's dead were occupants of other vehicles, and nineteen percent were nonoccupants: pedestrians, pedalcyclists, and everyone else caught outside a vehicle. So sit with that a moment. A school bus is a 25,000-pound yellow wall, and when a sedan argues with it, the sedan loses the argument and sometimes the driver. It protects its riders with almost insulting efficiency, then punishes everyone in its blast radius: nobody died from the bus failing; they died because everything around the bus kept moving.

Run the per-rider math and it gets silly. Roughly 21.4 million children ride a bus every school day.[2] Seventy passenger deaths across ten years is seven a year, about one death per three million riders annually. A school-age child was roughly twice as likely to die walking around the bus as riding inside it: 77 young pedestrians died in the decade versus 39 riders. Riding is the safest part of the trip. Walking is the gamble, and the house always wins eventually.

NHTSA's tables draw the victim in uncomfortably specific strokes. Fifty-five percent of the school-age pedestrians killed were between five and ten years old. Two-thirds died away from intersections, which is the polite statistical way of saying mid-block, darting, small and hard to see. Deadliest hour: 7:00 to 7:59 in the morning, dark, cold months, kids hurrying. Per the Governors Highway Safety Association, 1,279 children were killed in the ten-foot danger zone around buses over five decades, which means the bus is a fortress and the curb is a killing field.[3]

And every school day, America runs a live experiment in how many times you can break the law around that fortress before physics collects. For its 2026 count, the National Association of State Directors of Pupil Transportation Services had 101,334 drivers tally illegal stop-arm passes on a single spring day, and they logged 56,024.[4] Projected nationally, that is 213,800 a day and 38.4 million a school year, roughly one illegal pass per bus every two to three days. Indiana's April survey found one pass for every three to four buses, with afternoons the worst of it, and Congress noticed: the Brake for Kids Act would order NHTSA to run a national campaign telling drivers to stop doing a thing they do 38 million times a year.[5]

Give the other side its full due, because the numbers invite it. That 38.4 million is a projection, not a census: a voluntary one-day snapshot from about a quarter of the nation's drivers, extrapolated across 180 school days, with every sampling bias multiplied along the way. Most passes are low-speed rolling violations on multi-lane roads, not full-throttle runs through a loading zone. And its spotless record is partly operational rather than vehicular: low-speed residential routes, professional drivers, flashing lights, paint visible from orbit. Put the same crash cage on highway duty and the halo dims, though none of that changes the body count; it only explains the shape of it.

What this does not prove matters too, starting with the fact that FARS records fatal crashes only, so the 12,500 injuries in school-transportation crashes in 2022 sit outside this frame.[2] One hundred seven deaths a year is a small number, and a single multi-fatality crash bends the annual curve. This decade includes the COVID years, when ridership and traffic were both strange. And the largest category of all, the near-miss, appears in no dataset anywhere, though any bus driver has a personal archive.

So here is the actionable part, and it has nothing to do with the bus. If you drive: stop for the flashing reds on any undivided road, both directions, and wait. First offenses run to $1,000 in several states plus points and suspension; Washington writes a mandatory $500 ticket.[6] If you are a parent: the data says the danger is the walk, not the ride. Teach the ten-foot rule, wait for the driver's signal, never cross behind the bus. Ages five to ten, mid-block, seven in the morning is the exact profile in NHTSA's tables. America's safest vehicle is doing its job. It is the rest of us who keep failing the test.
