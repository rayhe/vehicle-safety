China Recalled 4.3 Million Cars Over Doors You Can't Open. The Fix Is a Sticker.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 7, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. On August 21, Tesla and eight other automakers recalled about 4.3 million vehicles in China because the emergency door handles may be impossible to locate after a crash, making it the largest automotive recall in Chinese history.[1] The remedy for a defect that can trap you inside a burning car is a warning sticker and a software patch. Read that sentence twice, because the handle itself does not change.

4.3 million
vehicles recalled in China over hidden emergency door handles; zero recalled in the US for the same defect

Here is what the regulators say is wrong. On modern EVs the door opens with an electronic button or touch panel, and when power dies in a crash, the backup is a mechanical release that is often hidden, unlabeled, and unfamiliar to anyone who has not read the manual.[3] China's State Administration for Market Regulation put it plainly: the mechanical emergency handles "may be difficult to identify following a severe collision and electrical system failure, potentially hindering occupants' escape or rescuers' access."[1] Tesla's share alone is 2.98 million imported and China-made Model 3, Model Y, Model S, and Model X vehicles, with the recall starting September 25. Xiaomi is recalling about 390,000 vehicles, Leapmotor about 371,000, Xpeng about 264,000, and each of those three calls it the largest recall in company history.[1]

Now savor the fix: warning labels marking the handles, over-the-air software updates, and a Tesla patch that automatically lowers the windows after a collision so occupants can climb out.[1] Leapmotor said the quiet part out loud: the upgrade "involved only a warning label and upgrading the power-window control software," and the company was "managing and filing it in accordance with the recall process out of a sense of responsibility to our users."[1] Four point three million cars, and the engineering change is a sticker telling you where the real handle hides. If your safety plan requires a label, you did not have a safety plan.

Beijing did not act on theory. Chinese state media reported in October that the driver of a Xiaomi SU7 sedan died after a crash and fire because passersby could not open the doors to pull him out.[1] A December 2025 Bloomberg investigation identified at least 15 people who died in crashes in which Tesla doors could not be opened.[3] China has also said it will ban concealed door handles from 2027, making it the first country to phase out a design popularized by Tesla and widely copied by domestic EV makers.[1]

Meanwhile, in the United States, the same handles sit on the same cars and nobody has filed anything. Twenty-eight days before China's recall, NHTSA denied a petition seeking a defect investigation into the door release on about 180,000 model-year 2022 Tesla Model 3s, ruling the issue "would be more appropriately addressed through an ongoing rulemaking process."[2] The reason was almost elegant in its bureaucratic circularity: FMVSS 206, the federal standard for door locks and latches, contains zero requirements about the labeling or location of a mechanical door release, so there is nothing on the books to enforce.[2] NHTSA granted a separate petition to consider new egress rules, while stressing that granting it "is not a decision by NHTSA to issue a new safety standard," and as of August 21, the agency said automakers "have not notified the agency of plans to file a similar recall for the U.S. market."[1][3] Same defect, opposite answers.

Fairness demands the counterargument, stated at full strength: China's 4.3 million is inflated by the sheer size of its EV fleet and by a regulator willing to classify a label campaign as a recall, and Leapmotor filed voluntarily, which suggests at least part of this is regulatory theater. On the American side, NHTSA found exactly one consumer complaint matching the defect petition, filed by the petitioner himself about his own car, which is thin evidence for a federal defect finding.[3] A rulemaking that eventually mandates genuinely obvious mechanical releases could protect more people than a sticker campaign, or it could take years and produce nothing at all.

So here is what you do, because nobody is coming to fix this for you in America. Find your car's mechanical emergency release today, before you need it: it is in the owner's manual under the emergency section, usually a small lever near the window controls or the door pull, and it looks nothing like a handle. Show every regular passenger, including the kids in the back, because a release only you know about is a release nobody has. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, and understand that you will find no door-handle recall, because none exists in the US.[4] The absence of a recall is not evidence of safety; it is evidence of paperwork.
