The Gap

Chrysler Replaced the Grand Caravan With the Pacifica. Deaths Per Mile Fell 86%.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 2, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. In 2012, IIHS started slamming cars into a barrier with just the front-left corner, a test that replicates hitting a tree or a pole at 40 mph, and the 2016 Dodge Grand Caravan folded up like a lawn chair. It earned a Poor rating, offered no front crash prevention at any price, and rode a platform that had barely changed since 2008.[2] Then Chrysler did the thing automakers claim they do and almost never actually do: it threw the whole van away and started over. The 2017 Pacifica got a clean-sheet structure with 72% high-strength steel, automatic emergency braking, and became the first minivan ever to earn IIHS Top Safety Pick+.[2][3]

86%
Decline in deaths per 100M VMT from the Dodge Grand Caravan (1.33) to its replacement, the Chrysler Pacifica (0.19)

A decade of federal fatality data now measures what that redesign bought. Across 2014 through 2023, the Grand Caravan killed 1,782 people at a rate of 1.33 deaths per 100 million miles, roughly double the van-class average of 0.65.[1] The Pacifica, over the same window, killed 160 at a rate of 0.19. That is a sevenfold gap between two vans built by the same company, sold to the same families, on the same roads. It beats the Honda Odyssey (0.93) and the Toyota Sienna (0.49) by embarrassing margins. NHTSA's own star ratings told a milder version of this story, five stars versus four, which is precisely why the star system undersells what engineering can do.[3]

Here is the part that should change how you read crash data. Split the gap into its two halves and they tell different stories. The Pacifica gets into 5.75 times fewer fatal crashes per mile than the Grand Caravan, but once a crash turns fatal, it kills only 1.18 times fewer people per crash.[1] In other words, about four-fifths of the 86% comes from never having the crash at all, which is the automatic emergency braking and forward collision warning doing exactly what the brochures promised, and only about one-fifth comes from surviving one better, which is the high-strength steel cage earning its keep in those first 150 milliseconds. FCA ran 8,500 simulated crashes and more than 80 full-vehicle impacts before the Pacifica launched; the Grand Caravan's structure predates the test that would have exposed it by four years.[3] Both halves of the number check out. The van that was never engineered for the modern test failed the modern world, and the van engineered past it is winning.

Now the honest pushback, because the gap is so large it deserves suspicion. The Pacifica fleet is young, every one of them built in 2017 or later, and new cars are bought by affluent households that crash less for reasons that have nothing to do with steel grades; the Grand Caravan fleet is old, cheap, and heavily bought used by exactly the demographics that crash more, in vehicles whose brake lines and bushings are aging in real time. The dataset cannot separate engineering from demographics, so some unknown fraction of the 86% is just money wearing a fender badge. And the 2026 IIHS update delivers the other rebuke: under today's tougher rear-seat test, even the Pacifica scores Marginal on rear-occupant protection and earns no award at all.[4] Safest minivan ever is a time-stamped claim. The Grand Caravan, though, was never the safest anything, in any year, under any test, and its own model-year series shows deaths flat from 2014 through 2019 with no within-nameplate improvement. Twelve years of "refresh" bought nothing, and one clean sheet bought 86%.

So here is what to do with this, and it is the most lopsided used-car advice this desk has published. A 2018 to 2020 Grand Caravan and a 2017 to 2019 Pacifica routinely list within a few thousand dollars of each other on the used market, and one of them carries roughly one-seventh the fatality rate of the other. Buy the redesign, not the refresh. Generalize it: when a nameplate gets a clean-sheet platform, the safety dividend can be enormous, and when it gets a decade of facelifts instead, check the small-overlap rating before you check the price. Check any used VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before you sign, because the Grand Caravan's long production run means a long recall tail, and then go find the van that was engineered for the crash instead of the one that predates the test.[1]
