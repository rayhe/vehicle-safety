October’s Recalls Came With Warnings. The Fixes Are Still Theoretical.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 12, 2026

Somewhere between Stellantis and Volkswagen and Mercedes-Benz, somebody quietly redefined what a recall is. It used to mean: we found the problem, here is the repair, come get it. As of this month, it means: 318,100 vehicles have defects serious enough to trigger federal recall, and the manufacturers’ plan is a letter that says they’ll write again later.[2]

318,100
Vehicles in October 2026 campaigns launched with interim letters and no final remedy (Ram 92D, VW ID.4 93EX, Mercedes brake hoses)

Start with the biggest one, because the universe loves scale. Chrysler is recalling 265,512 Ram ProMasters and Ram 3500 Electrics from 2022 through 2026. Water gets into the electric power steering wiring harness, and power steering assist dies. On a ProMaster, that means a fully loaded delivery van suddenly requiring gorilla strength to turn at parking-lot speed, or worse, a wrestling match at highway speed. Chrysler’s filing is admirably honest: interim letters go out November 17, and additional letters will follow when a final remedy is available.[2] Translation: your steering might quit, and we cannot yet say how to stop it.

The ID.4 campaign, covering 22,524 vehicles, is somehow more unsettling: the high-voltage battery can fail and torch the vehicle. VW has logged 23 fire claims in the US since January 2024. And the root cause is still unknown, per VW’s own filing. The interim remedy is behavioral, not mechanical: charge to 80% maximum and hope, because the actual remedy is under development. Your EV’s warranty manual now includes vibes.[7] Meanwhile Mercedes-Benz expanded recall 25V116 to 30,064 more S-Class and Maybach sedans whose front brake hoses can rupture and stretch your stopping distance: same hoses, second recall. The hose industry is having a worse October than anyone.[3]

And these are just the letter-only club. Ford pulled 41,748 Expeditions and Super Dutys because a supplier shipped contaminated LED chips that can kill every exterior light on the truck (six warranty claims, six field reports, zero injuries so far; the Field Review Committee signed off September 15).[5] Toyota recalled about 31,000 plug-in RAV4s because a software fault can delete motive power at speed, and the owner letters aren’t going out until December.[6] All told, October’s campaign announcements cover roughly 390,000 vehicles. That is a decent-sized city driving on promises.

Now the part where I have to be annoying: the interim letter is also the system working as designed. Federal rules require automakers to notify owners fast, even when the engineering isn’t done (49 CFR 577, roughly 60 days). The alternative is silence until a remedy is engineered, crash-tested, and stacked in parts warehouses, which leaves everyone driving blind for months. Still, a letter with no fix beats no letter: warnings were always the point, repairs were the bonus. But notice what that logic concedes: the recall system is now, formally, a warning system. It tells you the steering might die, but it cannot stop it. The letter arrives, you file it, and you keep driving the defective truck to the job site because the rent is due. NHTSA complied. Chrysler complied. Everybody complied, and 265,512 trucks still have wet wiring harnesses.

So do the one thing the system lets you do: search your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now, not when the letter shows up. Interim means no repair exists yet, and the mailbox is not a safety plan. ID.4 owners should cap charging at 80%, and Ram ProMaster owners in campaign 92D: memorize how your van feels when the assist dies, because it will not ask permission. And S-Class owners: if 25V116 already touched your car once, check again. The second recall is proof the first one didn’t catch everything.[1]
