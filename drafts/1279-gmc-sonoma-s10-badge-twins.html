GMC's Sonoma Was the S-10 in a Nicer Shirt. It Killed 378 People Anyway.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2026

GMC sold the Sonoma as the grown-up small pickup: same bones as the Chevrolet S-10, nicer trim, quieter badge, higher sticker. FARS 2014–2023 counts 378 deaths in Sonomas, at a fatality rate 7.6 times that of the Colorado that replaced it.[1] Buyers paid extra for the badge. Crash data charged exactly the same.

378
Sonoma deaths in FARS 2014–2023, in a fleet of just 131,250 trucks

Start with the twin math, because it reframes the whole truck. Chevrolet's S-10 killed 1,427 people in the same ten-year window, and adding the Sonoma's 378 brings the platform total to 1,805.[1] One platform, two badges, nearly two thousand deaths. GMC's version was never a different truck in any way that matters in a crash: same ladder frame, same cab structure, same era of safety engineering, which in this case means no stability control and no side curtain airbags on most trims. A premium badge buys leather and chrome. It does not buy a second load path.

Now compare the Sonoma to what replaced it, because that ratio is the number a used-truck shopper should carry in their pocket. Chevrolet's Colorado posts a fatality rate of 0.28 against the Sonoma's 2.13, making the old truck 7.6 times deadlier per mile.[1] Against the full-size establishment, the gap narrows but never closes: the Sonoma's rate runs about double the Ford F-150's 1.04 and well above the Silverado's 1.25, despite weighing far less than either.[1] Small and old beats big and new in exactly one contest, and it is not the contest you want to win.

Impairment does not rescue the story, and that is the part worth sitting with. Sonoma drivers in fatal crashes tested positive for alcohol or drugs 17.4% of the time, below the 20.0% fleet-weighted average across all models and below the S-10's own 20.5%.[1] Sober people, by the usual measure, died in this truck at an elevated rate anyway. Blaming the driver is the easy move in every one of these stories, and here the toxicology reports refuse to cooperate: strip out the impaired drivers and the Sonoma still looks like a bad place to have a crash.

Age finishes the explanation. Sonoma deaths in the dataset end with model year 2003, and production stopped after 2004, so every one of those 378 deaths happened in a truck at least eleven years old, most of them fifteen to twenty-five.[1] Fifteen-year-old steel does not get stronger with birthdays. Rubber, bushings, and brake lines age alongside the owner, and a frame designed in the 1980s meets modern traffic at a weight disadvantage measured in tons. Old trucks do not become classics in crash data. They become statistics.

Fairness requires two admissions. First, FARS rates are estimates built from fleet and mileage approximations, and a small fleet like the Sonoma's 131,250 carries wider uncertainty than a mass-market model.[1] Second, impairment is only one behavioral axis: FARS does not break out seat belt use, speed, or rural-versus-urban split at the per-model level here, so some of the Sonoma's excess could reflect how and where old trucks get driven, not only how they are built. Neither caveat erases a 7.6x gap. Both caveats mean you should treat the exact multiple as approximate and the direction as certain.

Shopping for a cheap used pickup? Skip the Sonoma and S-10 generation entirely, and put the same money toward a used Colorado, which carries roughly one-seventh the per-mile fatality rate. Already own a Sonoma? Treat it as a fair-weather, low-speed truck rather than a highway commuter, and check its VIN for any open recall before the next long trip.[3] More broadly, judge a used truck by its platform and its safety equipment, not by its badge: GMC charged extra for the shirt, and the crash data never noticed what it was wearing.
