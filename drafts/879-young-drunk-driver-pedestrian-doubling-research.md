## Kill test
Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. The buried line in the GHSA report: among fatal pedestrian crashes involving a driver aged 16-24, the share where the driver had BAC ≥ .08 **more than doubled** from 7.2% (2019) to 15.6% (2024) — the ONLY driver age group trending worse. Meanwhile pedestrians' own alcohol involvement **fell across nearly every age group**, and total pedestrian deaths fell for the third straight year. The drunk in a pedestrian death used to be the person on foot; the toxicology data says the drunk is increasingly the young person behind the wheel. Novel for the site? Verified: no existing story makes the young-driver impairment doubling its thesis (checked: teen-oldest-car-fatality-premium, 100-deadliest-days-teen-divergence, soberest-vehicle-426-dead are all different angles; the GHSA 2024 figures were only ever used as secondary context in #834's lethality story). Angle: inversion of the "drunk pedestrian" stereotype, with the decomposition (pedestrians sobering up + all driver ages flat except 16-24 rising) as the original contribution.

## Key numbers (all GHSA July 2026, from FARS 2024 analysis unless noted)
- 16-24 driver BAC ≥ .08 in fatal pedestrian crashes: **7.2% (2019) → 15.6% (2024)**. More than doubled.
- Driver alcohol involvement: stable across ALL other age groups; 16-24 is the sole exception.
- Pedestrian alcohol involvement: **decreased across all age groups**.
- Pedestrian impairment still exceeds driver impairment in every age group except 75+.
- Total pedestrian deaths: 7,237 (2024) → projected 6,732 (2025), -7%, third straight annual decline; still +5% vs 2019.
- Hit-and-run share: 23.8% of pedestrian deaths (1,669) — a systematic blind spot for driver BAC measurement (fled drivers are rarely tested).
- Context: distraction up (driver 15.6%→21.7%, pedestrian 15.3%→29.2%); 76.5% of deaths after dark; 62.2% where no sidewalk noted.

## Original contribution
The crossover decomposition: the classic "drunk pedestrian" stereotype is empirically fading — pedestrian impairment fell in every age group while the death count fell. The one cell of the toxicology table moving the wrong way is drivers 16-24. Frame: the pedestrian-death crisis is being repackaged as a young-driver sobriety crisis.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)
The rise may partly reflect **better testing of young drivers, not worse drinking**. BAC measurement in FARS is incomplete and uneven across states, ages, and years. Zero-tolerance norms for under-21 drivers mean police are more likely to test a 19-year-old than a 45-year-old, and if testing of the youngest cohort intensified between 2019 and 2024, the recorded impairment rate rises without behavior changing. GHSA's own report cautions that reporting methods vary by state. Separately, hit-and-run drivers (23.8% of these deaths) are almost never tested — driver BAC is systematically undermeasured, so the true impaired-driver share is higher than 15.6% for all ages, not just the young. The honest read: the direction for 16-24 is likely real, but the magnitude is hostage to testing practice.

## Limitations
- BAC data incomplete: unknown for many drivers and pedestrians; hit-and-run share (23.8%) is an untestable blind spot.
- Association, not causation: impaired young drivers also skew toward night, speed, and rural arterials — alcohol rides along with other risk.
- 2024 is the latest FARS year (released April 2026); 2025 is state-reported preliminary (~2% higher than FARS due to 30-day rule, private property, conveyance definitions).
- Absolute counts for the 16-24 cell are small; percentages can move on modest numerator changes.
- The article cannot attribute the rise to specific causes (pandemic behavior shifts, enforcement gaps, social norms) — those are hypotheses, labeled as such.

## Actionable insights (required)
- For parents of teen/young drivers: the data says alcohol + this age bracket is the one impaired-driving category getting worse nationally. Ignition interlock for young drivers post-violation, explicit rules about driving after any drinking (zero-tolerance is already the law under 21 — the problem is compliance), and rideshare credit rather than second chances.
- For pedestrians: the impaired-walker stereotype is outdated; the danger now skews toward being hit by someone else who's impaired — same defenses still apply (reflective gear, sidewalks, well-lit arterials), but the risk is less in the mirror than in the windshield.
- For policymakers: the GHSA report's own countermeasure list (enforcement on high-risk driving behavior, infrastructure) — and the testing gap: incomplete BAC testing means we likely undercount impaired driving in these crashes.
