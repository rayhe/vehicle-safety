Pedestrian Deaths Fell 3.9% in 2024. Injuries Rose 5%. The Road Isn't Safer.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 13, 2026

NHTSA published the finalized 2024 pedestrian numbers in June, and they open with a paradox. 7,080 pedestrians were killed, down 3.9 percent from 2023.[1] An estimated 71,635 were injured, up 5.0 percent.[1] Every 74 minutes, a pedestrian died. Every 7 minutes, one got hit and lived. Which of those is the safety story?

10.1
injuries for every pedestrian death in 2024 — up 9.2% from 9.3 in 2023, in a single year

Nobody prints the ratio, so I did the arithmetic NHTSA leaves out. In 2023, each pedestrian death came with 9.3 injuries; in 2024, 10.1. Divide 71,635 by 7,080 and 68,241 by 7,367, and the injuries-per-death ratio jumped 9.2 percent in twelve months.[1] That is not the shape of a safer road. It is the shape of more strikes landing and fewer of them being fatal.

And it is not alone, because this site just ran the identical divergence for large trucks: deaths down 2.5 percent, injuries up 5 percent, in NHTSA's finalized 2024 truck data.[4] Two independent datasets, same contradiction, published weeks apart. When deaths fall and injuries rise together, the press-release reading is that roads got safer. The literal reading is that hits went up while lethality per hit went down. Better crash survival, faster trauma response, softer vehicle fronts, or sheer luck. More people in the emergency room either way.

Look at the profile of the ones who didn't make it, because it reads like a police sketch. Eighty-four percent were in urban areas, and seventy-three percent died somewhere that wasn't an intersection. Seventy-six percent died in the dark.[1] Ninety percent were hit by a single vehicle. Sixty-nine percent were men, dying at 2.91 per 100,000 people, 2.3 times the female rate.[1] Nearly one in four, 24 percent, were struck by a driver who kept going.[1] If you wanted to design the most lethal ten seconds of walking in America, it would be a man in dark clothes crossing an unlit arterial, mid-block, at night.

Alcohol complicates the eulogy, since it was involved, for the driver or the pedestrian, in 43 percent of fatal pedestrian crashes in 2024.[1] But the share of pedestrians killed with a BAC of .08 or higher fell from 34 percent in 2015 to 27 percent in 2024, a real decline in the drunk-walking slice while everything else grew.[1] Credit where it's due. The sober are getting hit more; the drunk are getting hit less.

Zoom out and the decade tells the grimmer story. In 2015: 5,494 pedestrian deaths; in 2024: 7,080. That's 29 percent more dead pedestrians in ten years, while injuries grew barely 2 percent over the same stretch.[1] Over the long arc, each hit got more lethal. In 2024 alone, each death came with more injuries. Both things are true, and neither is the headline anyone asked for.

The honest counterargument, stated at full strength: injury figures are not counts. They are national estimates built by sampling police reports through the Crash Report Sampling System, with real statistical wobble baked in.[1] Part of the 5 percent injury rise could be estimation noise, and some of the death decline could be better trauma care rather than fewer or gentler strikes. NHTSA does not explain the divergence in its own report. The ratio is a signal, not a verdict.

What you can actually do is boring, which is how you know it works. Cross where it is lit, because three-quarters of pedestrian deaths happen in the dark and nearly three-quarters happen outside intersections, so the deadliest combination is the one you already picture: unlit arterial, mid-block, after dark.[1] Wear something that catches headlights. Pick the crosswalk even when it costs you a minute. Assume the driver never saw you, because at night, outside a crosswalk, they statistically did not until the last second.[3]

One pedestrian dies every 74 minutes in this country, and that number fell in 2024. The number who got hit and survived rose. Keep both numbers in your head at the same time. The universe keeps its books in a language only it reads, and sometimes it lets you read along.
