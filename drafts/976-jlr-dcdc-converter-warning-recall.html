JLR's Fix for 23,677 Cars That Can Die in the Dark Is a Warning Light

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 23, 2026
☕ 4 min read

On September 17, Jaguar Land Rover filed a new recall campaign covering 23,677 of its mild-hybrid SUVs, every one of them estimated to carry the same defect: a DC-DC converter that can fail and take the car's drive power and exterior lighting down with it. JLR's announced remedy is free, simple, and remarkable for what it leaves out. No new converter, and no new part of any kind. Dealers will update the software so the car can warn you when the converter dies.

This failure has a choreography. Within ten seconds of the converter failing, a red “Stop Safely Electrical Fault Detected” message appears on the dash. Then the cascade begins: lane-keep assistance disabled, suspension fault, stability control fault. Keep driving and the gearbox shifts itself into Neutral with a gearbox fault message. Meanwhile the engine keeps running for a while, rationing its last breaths. First to die: the center screen and the air conditioning. Then the engine shuts down. And some time later, the battery loses enough charge to sustain the exterior lighting. Your lights are the last thing to go, which means the dark is the final symptom.

JLR's own recall paperwork documents 393 claims and field reports for this failure. None involved an accident, an injury, or a fire. That zero is doing a lot of work in this story. Back in September 2024, JLR's Product Safety and Compliance Committee reviewed rising global warranty claims for converter failures and concluded that, “given the gradual progression of symptoms experienced by the customer,” the issue “did not pose an unreasonable risk to safety.” In corporate translation: it's a customer-satisfaction problem. It tells you it's dying, and it dies slowly. Slow death, in this reading, is not a safety issue.

NHTSA disagreed, and on April 7, 2026, the agency clarified its view of the matter as “one of a safety nature rather than customer satisfaction.” Three days later, JLR's safety committee “decided to proceed in accordance with the Agency's viewpoint,” and the recall launched. Nineteen months separated “not a safety issue” from the federal government deciding it had been one all along. Between those two sentences sit 5,952 U.S. claims and field reports of converter replacement, logged from July 2019 through April 2026.

5,952
U.S. claims and field reports of DCDC converter replacement, July 2019 to April 2026 — zero accidents, zero injuries, zero fires

September's filing is the second wave under campaign 26V248, and this time there is a remedy. April's wave covered 170,169 vehicles, the largest recall in Jaguar Land Rover's history. Now 23,677 more join under manufacturer campaign E008, with expanded model-year coverage for the Evoque, Discovery Sport, and E-Pace lines. Two nameplates carry almost all of the weight: Range Rover Sport (10,973) and Defender (8,100), together 80.6 percent of the September population. JLR estimates 100 percent of them carry the defect.

As for the defect itself: an internal fault in the boost control microchip inside the converter, built by LG Innotek in Poland or by Yazaki, according to the Part 573 filing. Or, in the September report's framing, an electrical overload during high power demand that a software error lets spiral out of control. What the update does is twofold. It guards against the overload, and it introduces a limp-home mode that warns the driver “in case the DC-DC converter goes offline for any reason.” Read that phrase again: in other words, the fix anticipates the failure. It does not stop the hardware from dying; it makes sure you get a heads-up and a limp home when it does.

80.6%
Share of the September recall population in just two models: Range Rover Sport (10,973) and Defender (8,100)

Fairness requires the defense to speak at full volume, because JLR's original call was not obviously insane. Five thousand nine hundred fifty-two converter failures. Zero accidents, zero injuries, zero fires. That failure sequence gives you a red warning within ten seconds and a slow, loud cascade after that. If a defect announces itself and dies slowly, and nobody has been hurt across thousands of events, “customer satisfaction” is not a crazy classification. It is simply the wrong one when the failure mode is losing your headlights on a dark highway, where the sample size that matters is not the 5,952 who made it home but the one night drive that goes wrong. NHTSA saw it; it just took them nineteen months.

If you own a 2019–2024 JLR mild hybrid — Range Rover Sport, Defender, Range Rover, Discovery, Velar, Evoque, F-Pace, E-Pace — check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today. Interim owner letters mail November 13, but don't wait for the mail: call your dealer and ask about the DC-DC converter software update. And if “Stop Safely Electrical Fault Detected” ever appears on your dash, do exactly what it says. The company's own failure sequence says you get minutes, not hours. Do not be driving when the lights go out.

What this doesn't prove: the September filing's exact relationship to the April population is inferred from the changed manufacturer campaign code (E008 vs. D126 H575), the expanded model-year ranges, and press framing — I did not have the September Part 573 text. “Zero accidents” is JLR's reporting, not an independent count, and near-miss strandings may be under-reported. Remedy details beyond “software update plus limp-home mode” are not public. The 393 figure is the recent-window claim count cited in September coverage; 5,952 is the long-window U.S. replacement count from the April filing.
