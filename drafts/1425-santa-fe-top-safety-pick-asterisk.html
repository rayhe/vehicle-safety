The Gap

The Santa Fe Won the Lab's Top Prize. Its Own Decade on the Road Says Wait.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds, because that is where Hyundai just won its trophy and where its customers need to read the fine print. IIHS gave the redesigned Santa Fe its highest award, Top Safety Pick+, for 2025 and 2026 models, but only for vehicles built after November 2024, when Hyundai modified the rear seat belts.[1] Buy a 2025 Santa Fe built in October 2024 and a 2025 Santa Fe built in December 2024, park them side by side, and you are looking at the same model year, the same trim, the same window sticker, and two different back seats. One of them earned the lab's top prize. One of them did not.

Santa Fe fatalities per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, FARS 2014–2023, respectable, unremarkable, and mostly describing a car Hyundai no longer builds

Rear seat belts are the whole story here, and they have been the whole story across the industry for two years now. IIHS rewrote its moderate overlap front test in 2022 to put a dummy in the back seat, a dummy the size of a fifth-percentile adult woman, roughly a 12-year-old child, belted in behind the driver while the car hits a barrier at 40 mph.[5] Front seats got decades of engineering. Back seats got leftovers. Nissan learned this the expensive way when its Kicks scored Poor, then revised the rear belts and load limiters and jumped to Good. Hyundai's November 2024 change is the same fix, a belt revision, not a platform redesign, which is exactly why it stings: cheap, fast, proven on a $22,000 Nissan, and invisible to the buyer who never checks the door jamb sticker.

Meanwhile NHTSA, the other lab in town, does not agree with IIHS at all. KBB's January 2026 family-car writeup notes that NHTSA faulted the Santa Fe's front crash protection and gave it four out of five stars overall, while IIHS handed it the top prize.[4] Two federal-adjacent test programs, same car, different verdicts. That split is worth sitting with, because it tells you the award measures a specific test protocol, not the car in general. A trophy is a test result with better branding.

Now the road, which keeps its own score. FARS records 555 Santa Fe deaths across 2014 to 2023, at 0.39 fatalities per 100 million miles, on a fleet of about 1.14 million vehicles.[6] Stack that against the segment and the number looks fine. Highlander's 0.42. Grand Cherokee's 0.51. Explorer's 1.54, which is four times the Santa Fe's rate and the number that should end every argument about which family SUV is actually dangerous. But fine is not a prize. Across all 121 SUVs in the FARS table, the Santa Fe ranks 64th by rate. Dead middle. Its own platform sibling, the Kia Sorento, sits at 0.29. And the deaths cluster where you would expect for a pre-redesign car: model year 2017 peaked at 51 deaths, then the count falls off a cliff for 2019 and later, down to single digits by 2021.

Before anyone prints a leaderboard, here is the trap inside the leaderboard. Hyundai's Palisade scores 0.06 and the Kia Telluride 0.04, which looks like a six-fold safety gap over the Santa Fe. It is mostly a birthday gap. Both nameplates are young, with small fleets and few exposure years inside the 2014 to 2023 window, so their rates are flattered by youth while the Santa Fe's rate carries a decade of older, less protected cars. FARS rates punish old nameplates and flatter new ones. Read the rate without the birth certificate and you will conclude the newest car is the safest car, which is sometimes true and always unproven by that number alone.

Fairness requires the strongest case against this story, stated straight. FARS 2014 to 2023 barely contains the redesigned Santa Fe, and it contains zero cars with the November 2024 belt revision, so the decade of deaths cannot indict the 2026 model. A reader could fairly say the 555 deaths are irrelevant to a buyer walking onto a lot today. That reader is right, and that is the point. This article does not claim the new Santa Fe is unsafe. It claims the prize is new, the asterisk is real, and the nameplate's real-world record is ordinary, so the buyer should verify the build date instead of trusting the badge or the trophy.

What this piece did not prove, stated plainly. No real-world fatality data exists yet for the TSP+ car, because the car is barely two years old and the belt revision is barely two years old. Model-year death counts mix exposure with safety: the 2019 and later decline is consistent with a safer redesign and with fewer miles driven by newer cars, and FARS cannot separate the two. Estimated rates use VMT estimates rather than odometer readings, with roughly 15% uncertainty for low-volume models, though the Santa Fe's large fleet makes its 0.39 comparatively stable. NHTSA's four-star overall rating is reported via KBB; check NHTSA's own ratings page for the exact model year before quoting it.

What you can actually do with this. Shopping for a 2025 or 2026 Santa Fe, open the driver's door and read the build date on the jamb sticker. Built after November 2024, it carries the Top Safety Pick+ rear-seat rating. Built before, it does not. Same model year, same price, different back seat, and no dealer is required to volunteer the difference. Driving a 2013 to 2018 Santa Fe, know that model year 2017 peaked at 51 deaths in FARS, which is the clearest argument the redesign ever had. And if you own any car whose award came with a build-date footnote, remember the lesson the Kicks taught first: the fix was always a belt revision away, and the only person who can check your build date is you.
