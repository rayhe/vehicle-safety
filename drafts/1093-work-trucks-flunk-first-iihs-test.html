IIHS Finally Crash-Tested the Work Truck. It Flunked.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
July 8, 2027

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a panic stop in a 2027 Chevrolet Express: nothing. No warning chime, no automatic braking, because the van lacks the hardware; its platform dates to 1996. IIHS just confirmed what the spec sheet already said: the Express and its GMC Savana twin earned Poor ratings in both vehicle-to-vehicle and pedestrian crash avoidance, the worst result in the institute’s first safety test of work vehicles.[1]

6,215
People killed in 2024 in crashes involving heavy/medium-duty trucks or light vans: 16% of all US roadway deaths

Start with the test itself. In June 2026, IIHS began running cargo vans and Class 3 pickups (10,001 to 14,000 pounds) through the same crash-avoidance program it uses on family crossovers. Eleven vehicles went through the Ruckersville, Virginia, facility; seven earned Poor in vehicle-to-vehicle scenarios: the Express, Savana, Transit, and ProMaster vans, plus the heavy-duty Silverado, F-350, and Sierra.[1] Only the Mercedes Sprinter 2500 managed a Good.

Two findings should embarrass the industry, starting with motorcycle detection, the hardest scenario in the program: several systems never warned and never slowed at all, while others braked but warned too late and lost points.[1] Then the disappearing child: the F-350 and Ram 3500 earned Acceptable pedestrian ratings yet could not reliably spot a child-size dummy in daylight, though the adult dummy at night did not. At close range the dummy vanishes below the driver’s sight lines and the mirror-mounted sensors, swallowed by the hood.[1]

17×
Annual work-vehicle crash deaths (6,215) relative to the lives NHTSA expects its AEB mandate to save each year (360)

Now the regulatory cliff, where the numbers get rude. FMVSS 127 requires automatic emergency braking with pedestrian detection on every new light vehicle at or under 10,000 pounds GVWR from September 1, 2029, a rule NHTSA says will save at least 360 lives and prevent 24,000 injuries a year.[3] Divide 6,215 by 360: roughly 17. That headline benefit is about one-seventeenth of the annual death toll in the category the rule does not cover. IIHS research chief David Kidd said the AEB requirement “is not coming for commercial vehicles.”[1]

Consider the other side at full strength, because the fleets have a case. Work vehicles log far more miles per year than family cars, so part of that 6,215 is exposure, not per-mile menace. GM still sells roughly 40,000 Express and Savana vans a year, because they are cheap, simple, and easy to fix.[4] Fitting AEB to a cab-chassis truck is genuinely harder than fitting it to a Camry: the factory ships a blank chassis and an upfitter bolts on whatever body the customer ordered. Those are real constraints, and also the same ones cited for thirty years while the platform aged in place.

A note on what this did not prove. IIHS’s 6,215 comes from 2024 FARS data against NHTSA’s early estimate of 39,345 total deaths; the math lands at 15.8 percent, rounding to the cited 16.[5] This was an eleven-vehicle first round, not a fleet census, and Class 4 through 6 trucks have not been tested at all. “Thirty-year-old van” means the platform launched for 1996 and facelifted in 2003.[4] In GM’s favor: the Express 2500’s GVWR sits under 10,000 pounds, so FMVSS 127 covers it and compliant AEB must be aboard by September 2029; above the weight line, the Class 3 heavy-duty pickups never will be.

What to do about it: these vehicles finally have independent ratings, so check IIHS scores before ordering the next van the way you would for the company sedan.[2] Spec forward-collision warning at minimum and full AEB wherever offered, and treat a 2026 Express like the 1996 vehicle it structurally is. If you share the road with these trucks, and you do, give them room. Your motorcycle may be invisible to the driver, a child can vanish at the bumper, and the brakes will not apply themselves.
