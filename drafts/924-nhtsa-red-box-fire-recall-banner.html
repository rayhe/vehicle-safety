NHTSA Just Added a Red Box to Its Recall Site. It's a Sign 3.2 Million Cars Might Be on Fire.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 17, 2026

Starting today, the federal government's recall website has a red box for cars that might burn your garage down. Search any VIN at NHTSA.gov/Recalls and, if that vehicle carries an open do-not-drive or fire-risk recall, a red banner appears at the very top of the page telling you not to drive it or park it inside until it's fixed.[1] That is not a website refresh; it is a federal agency admitting its last warning was too quiet for the size of the problem.

Forget anecdotes; the problem is measured in millions. In the first seven months of 2026, automakers issued 17 park-outside recalls covering roughly 1.7 million vehicles. That's more than all of 2024, which had 10, and roughly seventeen times the pace of 2015 through 2019, when the annual average was about one. Seventeen campaigns in seven months annualizes to roughly 29 per year.[2] Eight of this year's campaigns landed in June and July alone.

17
Park-outside recall campaigns in the first seven months of 2026, versus 10 in all of 2024

The outstanding pile is worse than the annual flow. Carfax counts 3.2 million vehicles nationwide sitting under unresolved park-outside recalls as of June, a number NHTSA confirms, and two months later nothing suggests it has shrunk.[3] The June surge came almost entirely from one Stellantis recall: 1,076,999 Jeep Wranglers and Gladiators from model years 2021 through 2025, recalled for an electrical connection issue in the power-steering pump wiring that can overheat combustible materials and start a fire. Kia followed in July with 462,000-plus Tellurides under park-outside notice.[2]

This red box is NHTSA's third attempt to make the same warning stick, which is the part that should bother you. NHTSA created the park-outside designation in 2015, added a warning box to its online recall listings in 2022, and now shoves the alert to the top of every VIN lookup in 2026.[5] Every escalation arrived because the previous warning failed to keep up with a fire rate that keeps doubling. The alarm is not getting louder by accident. It is getting louder because the fire is getting bigger.

3.2M
Vehicles nationwide under unresolved park-outside recalls, per Carfax data confirmed by NHTSA

No single recall can fix the underlying problem, because the underlying problem is every modern car. Former NHTSA investigator Frank Borris explained it to the Washington Post: “The engine is off, but the circuits are still hot, and they stay hot all the time.”[2] Dozens of circuits in a new vehicle never power down: batteries, relays, seat motors. That is why park-outside recalls span gasoline, diesel, hybrid, and electric models alike. More electronics in the car means more things that can fail after you walk away.

Sarah Lammersen learned this the hard way. Her 2022 Jeep Wrangler plug-in hybrid was destroyed by fire days after she found out it had been recalled. Her daughter was behind the wheel, and nobody was hurt, though a lawsuit is now working its way through the courts.[2] Lammersen did what the system asked, and the system failed her anyway.

Credit where it's due: the red box is a genuine improvement. Recall completion rates rise when urgency is communicated clearly, and a regulator that upgrades its tools instead of writing stern letters is doing its job. But the improvement carries its own indictment, because nobody bolts a red alarm banner onto a homepage when things are going well.

So go look: enter your VIN at NHTSA.gov/Recalls or in the SaferCar app and see whether the red box appears. If it does: park outside and away from structures and other vehicles, don't charge until the repair is done, and know the fix is free at any authorized dealer.[1] Shopping for a used car means running the VIN before you sign, especially on 2021 through 2025 Wranglers and Gladiators. Parts shortages mean some owners wait months in driveway limbo. Read it as a parking instruction, not just a repair order.

What this piece can't prove. Carfax's 3.2 million counts vehicles with outstanding recalls, which blends the unfixed with the temporarily unfixable: for some campaigns, the remedy still doesn't exist. That 17-campaign tally is the Washington Post's analysis of NHTSA data, not a published agency figure, and annualizing seven months of 2026 assumes the pace holds through December. Forbes puts the outstanding total at 3.4 million, so treat the headline number as plus or minus 200,000 either way. And FARS plays no role here. This is a recall-policy story, not a fatality-rate analysis, so none of the site's usual crash-rate methodology applies.
