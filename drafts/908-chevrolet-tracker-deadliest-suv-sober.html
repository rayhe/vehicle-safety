The Deadliest SUV in America Is One Nobody Remembers

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 15, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds when a Chevrolet Tracker gets hit in the side. Nothing good, that's what, and according to our FARS 2014-2023 table, the Tracker dies at 7.83 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles, the highest rate of any SUV in the dataset and the second-highest of all 337 models we track[1]. GM stopped building the Tracker in 2004, yet it has killed 856 people since 2014. Our pipeline estimates 87,500 Trackers remain registered and rolling, which means each one is, per mile, 41 times deadlier than a Toyota RAV4.

41×
Tracker fatality rate vs. Toyota RAV4 (7.83 vs. 0.19 deaths per 100M miles)

The usual story behind a scary rate is impairment. Sports cars kill because young men drive them drunk. Check the toxicology column and the Tracker refuses to cooperate: only 12.7 percent of its fatal-crash drivers tested positive for alcohol or drugs, against a 20.0 percent dataset average[1]. These are sober drivers dying in a machine that cannot protect them. When the impairment signal is this low and the rate is this high, the vehicle is the verdict.

So let's do the engineering autopsy, because every number on the Tracker's report card reads like a coroner's note. IIHS rated the 2004 model's moderate-overlap front crash "Acceptable" and its side impact "Poor," with Poor head protection and Poor head/neck injury measures for the driver[2]. Roof strength was never tested, and neither was small overlap. In 2003 IIHS found the Tracker and its Suzuki Grand Vitara twin essentially the same vehicle and assigned both the same Poor side rating; only a Santa Fe with optional side airbags managed "Acceptable"[3]. It had no side airbags and no electronic stability control. Federal ESC rules phased in from 2009 to 2012, five years after the last Tracker left the factory[4].

Then there is the rollover physics, and this is where I get judgmental about tall narrow boxes. In dynamic fishhook and J-turn tests run for the federal government, the Chevrolet Tracker and the Ford Ranger performed worst of every vehicle tested, and those two had the highest fatal and injury rollover rates of the entire test group[5]. The researchers noted something damning: the Tracker actually had a BETTER static stability factor than the Toyota 4Runner, yet its fatal single-vehicle rollover rate (127) beat the 4Runner's (119)[5]. Static numbers lied; the dynamics didn't. A tall, narrow, softly-sprung box on skinny tires does not need to be drunk to roll. It just needs a wet curve and a driver correction.

One more number that should embarrass the data system: 89 percent of the Tracker's FARS deaths carry a coded vehicle model year AFTER 2004, when production ended[1]. The model-year field is fiction for this nameplate. The 856 deaths are real crashes with real bodies; the coding is a mess, which is itself a finding. Per-model FARS rates for discontinued vehicles rest on paperwork that cannot tell a 2002 Tracker from a ghost.

What to do with this

If you drive a pre-2005 small SUV, a Tracker, a Vitara, a Sidekick, any of the tall cheap boxes of that era, understand that you are driving a vehicle with a Poor side rating, no stability control, and rollover dynamics that failed federal dynamic tests. Drive it like the antique it is, keep the speed down on rural two-lanes, and do not hand the keys to a teenager because it was cheap, and cheap is the trap. The cheapest used-car deal in America has the worst per-mile body count in the SUV segment.

If you are shopping used small SUVs, the modern numbers are not close. The RAV4 sits at 0.19 deaths per 100M miles while the Tracker sits at 7.83. The Forester (0.26) and Rogue (0.35) sit in the same safe cluster. Check any used vehicle's IIHS ratings at iihs.org/ratings before you buy, and treat any pre-ESC nameplate as guilty until proven innocent[6].

The strongest counterargument

The denominator is an estimate, not an odometer reading, and for a discontinued vehicle with 87,500 survivors it carries real uncertainty. If some of those 856 deaths are mis-VIN-decoded vehicles, which the impossible model years suggest, the true rate could be lower. And the people still driving a $3,000 Tracker in 2023 are not a random sample: rural roads, off-pavement use, deferred maintenance, higher speeds, longer EMS response times. All of that inflates the rate with no design flaw required. A fair reading is that the Tracker's TRUE rate is probably worse than a modern SUV by a lot and maybe not literally 41 times. The gap is too wide for bookkeeping to close.

What this does not prove

FARS captures fatal crashes only, not the injuries or the near-misses, and the rate math divides by estimated miles, not measured ones. We did not prove which specific design feature killed which driver; we proved the nameplate is lethal per mile and showed three independent datasets pointing at the same mechanism. That is a strong case, not a closed one.
