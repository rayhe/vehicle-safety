The Gap

Kia Recalled One EV9 for a Battery Fire Risk. Sixteen Owners Had Already Complained.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 2, 2026

Kia recalled exactly one 2024 EV9 in the United States for a battery defect that can short-circuit and catch fire. Sixteen other owners of the same model year had already told federal regulators their high-voltage batteries were failing. One car made the recall list, sixteen owners made the complaint file, and nobody connected the two lists.[1][4]

16 to 1
2024 EV9 owners who reported failing high-voltage batteries to NHTSA, versus 2024 EV9s in recall 26V431

Recall 26V431 covers eight vehicles total: seven 2022-2024 EV6s built between December 30, 2021 and September 14, 2023, plus a single 2024 EV9 built on October 28, 2023. Certain cells in their high-voltage battery assemblies may have been produced with a misaligned electrode, a supplier quality deviation from SK On that can lead to an internal short and a fire. Kia dealers will replace the entire battery assembly free of charge, a three-hour job, under campaign SC375, with owner letters mailed from August 7, 2026. Kia's own filing records no fires, injuries, crashes, or fatalities tied to the defect.[1][2][3]

Now read the other file. An analysis of NHTSA's public complaint database, pulled October 28, 2026, found sixteen different 2024 EV9 owners reporting high-voltage battery failures, in complaints filed between November 6, 2024 and February 26, 2026, out of 210 total complaints on file for the model year. Nine of the sixteen reported a dash warning. Nine said they were told not to drive or were towed. First complaint to recall filing: 603 days.[4]

Here is why the two lists diverge, and it matters more than any single count. Kia built its recall population from production and supplier records, tracing specific cells from a specific deviation to specific VINs. That is how a recall list gets to exactly eight cars across two models. Complaints arrive through a different door entirely, one owner at a time, describing symptoms in plain language. Nothing in the recall process automatically sweeps the second list into the first. At most one of the sixteen complaining owners could be the owner of the recalled EV9, and whether even that overlap exists is not established in any public record. Precision about the supplier's list, silence about the complaint file.

Fairness requires the strongest version of the other side, because it is a good argument. Owner complaints are unverified, and some of those sixteen batteries may have failed for reasons that have nothing to do with misaligned electrodes, while Kia's field analysis found no related battery-fire incidents at all. A one-car recall built from traceability records is manufacturing control working with unusual precision, not a company hiding a fleet-wide defect. Kia found this problem through its Korean-market investigation and recalled cars in the United States before any American fire. All of that is true, and it still leaves the gap standing: sixteen people did the thing safety regulators ask people to do, they filed complaints, and the recall that followed was built without them.

Anyone driving a 2024 EV9 or a 2022-2024 EV6 should run the VIN through nhtsa.gov/recalls today, not when a letter arrives, because absence from a supplier-built list proves nothing about your car. Owners who have seen a battery warning, lost power suddenly, or been towed for a battery issue should get the failure documented with the dealer and filed with NHTSA as well, since the complaint file is evidently part of the record even when it is not part of the recall. Used buyers should treat a replaced high-voltage assembly under SC375 as documented good news, and ask for paperwork on any EV9 with a battery complaint history that never became a recall. File the complaint, check the VIN, and trust neither list alone.[6]
