Jeep Recalled Its 2026 Cars 12 Times. Its Biggest Recall of the Year Isn't One of Them

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 14, 2026

Let's talk about what happens when you count a brand's recalls the honest way, by model year, one NHTSA query at a time. Jeep's ten 2026 nameplates carry exactly 12 recall campaigns filed in 2026. That count is verified: 42 NHTSA API queries, cross-checked against the Stellantis-wide census this site ran two days ago. Twelve is a real number. It is also the wrong number to quote, because Jeep filed seven more recalls in 2026 that touch no 2026 model at all, and the seven you miss contain the biggest Jeep recall of the year: a park-outside fire recall covering more than a million Wranglers and Gladiators.[1][13]

1,467,098
Jeeps recalled in 2026 under campaigns the 12-count misses, plus the Wagoneer S share of one more

Start with the twelve that count. One campaign, the rearview-camera blackout 26V531, covers 844,027 vehicles across four Stellantis brands, and Jeep's share alone is 355,527, from 170,033 Wranglers down to 4,119 Compasses. Another, the trailer-tow module failure 26V059, covers 456,287 vehicles, though Jeep's slice of that one is just 378, a rounding error next to Ram's 312,365 trucks. A TPMS software error, 26V559, covers 201,975 vehicles and every single one is a Jeep. Add a 641-vehicle steel-wheel recall where every wheel in the suspect batch is defective, and the four campaigns with verified Jeep splits already total 558,521 Jeeps. Eight more sit on top of that floor: an ESC loss on the resurrected Cherokee, a child-seat tether weld on the Wrangler, a Grand Wagoneer that loses its brakes, a Grand Cherokee that loses its wipers.[4][5][7][12]

Per model, the Grand Wagoneer and Grand Wagoneer L each carry five campaigns, the Cherokee and Grand Cherokee four each, the Grand Cherokee L three, the Wrangler three, the Gladiator two, the Wagoneer S two, the Compass one. Only the Recon, Jeep's new electric SUV, carries zero. The Cherokee deserves a longer look: a resurrected nameplate, discontinued after 2023 and back for 2026 as a hybrid-only SUV, and its first model year back already includes an ESC-loss recall. Four campaigns on a car that did not exist last year. Year two starts looking like the smart buy.[1][13]

Now the seven the count misses. Biggest first. Campaign 26V363, filed June 4, covers 1,076,999 model-year 2021 to 2025 Wranglers and Gladiators whose electric hydraulic power steering pump wiring can overheat and start a fire, even with the vehicle parked and the ignition off. NHTSA knows of 51 fires and one injury. FCA estimates only 0.1 percent of the population actually carries the faulty connection, about 1,077 Jeeps, roughly 21 times the fires NHTSA counted. The agency closed its two-year investigation on September 24 precisely because this recall covers the vehicles it was examining. Park outside until the dealer checks the wiring. That instruction is still in NHTSA's database.[2][11]

Next comes a trilogy. Campaign 26V562, filed September 1, covers 328,381 Grand Cherokees whose rear coil springs may have been installed incorrectly and can detach while driving. It is the third recall for the same spring: 23V413 in June 2023, then 26V051 in January 2026, after springs kept detaching, then this one, after NHTSA sent more complaints in July and FCA opened another investigation. It replaces the first two and changes the remedy to new lower isolators on both rear springs. Vehicles repaired under either earlier recall still need the new fix. Three campaigns, one spring, zero crashes or injuries reported, and 328,381 owners told to come back a third time.[8]

Then the quartet. Campaign 26V290, filed May 7, covers 61,711 Cherokees from 2019 to 2023 whose two-speed power transfer unit can fail internally, cutting drive power at any speed or letting the vehicle roll while in Park. It is the fourth Cherokee PTU recall since 2020, and every Cherokee model year from 2014 to 2023 is in at least one of them. As of late October the fix still was not available: new software for the drivetrain control module, the PTU, and the electronic park brake, then a new PTU if needed. One accident and one injury are tied to this filing.[3]

Rounding out the excluded set: 26V262, a 20,271-vehicle instrument-cluster recall on 2024 and 2025 Wagoneer S and Charger Daytona electrics, where blanked displays fail multiple federal standards at once; 26V327, a 16-vehicle rearview-camera recall spread across Pacifica, Ram, and Wrangler; and 26V413, a seven-vehicle shifter-display recall under a stop sale. Seven vehicles and 1.08 million vehicles, counted as equals. Campaign counts are not severity scores.[6][9][10]

Put the two sides together and the honest headline is not "Jeep: 12." Counted campaigns cover at least 558,521 Jeeps, on the four campaigns with verified splits. Excluded campaigns cover at least 1,467,098 Jeeps, plus the Wagoneer S share of 26V262. For every Jeep the census counts, about 2.6 more were recalled in 2026 under campaigns the method excludes. That gap is a property of the counting method, not a cover-up: the census counts only filings that touch a 2026 model year, and the fire recall touches none. Zero of the 12 is flagged over-the-air in NHTSA's API, the same zero-for-everything result every census in this series has returned, even though 26V531's radio fix is delivered over the air.[1]

Fairness requires the other side, stated at full strength, because 1.47 million sounds alarming and the truth is quieter. Campaign counts are not severity scores. One radio-software bug dominates the counted total, and most owners fix it with an over-the-air update. One fire recall dominates the excluded total, and its defect estimate is 0.1 percent, about 1,077 Jeeps out of 1,076,999. Jeep sells ten nameplates, so it files more campaigns than a brand selling three, and the per-model numbers are the honest comparison, not the brand total. Compare the ratio with care: the excluded total is fully verified while the counted total sums only four campaigns, so the true ratio is lower than 2.63 to 1. And the coil-spring trilogy counts one defect three times across three years, which inflates any campaign-count reading of Jeep's record. All of that is true. None of it changes the part that matters: a reader who takes "Jeep: 12" as the brand's 2026 safety record is wrong by 1.47 million vehicles, and the biggest one can burn a parked car in a driveway.

What this census does not prove: the counted Jeep-vehicle total is a floor, not a total, because eight of the twelve campaigns have no verified Jeep split in this article. The excluded total is also a floor, because the Wagoneer S share of 26V262's 20,271 vehicles is not broken out in the sources consulted. Unit counts are the manufacturers' filed estimates for all model years in each campaign, not 2026-only counts, so no 2026-only Jeep vehicle total can be computed from the filings. NHTSA's API can still hide a campaign under an untested spelling, and the 2026 model year is still in progress, so late filings will move every number here. FARS data is not part of this census: these are recall counts, not crash outcomes.

What to do with all of this. Own a 2021 to 2025 Wrangler or Gladiator? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for 26V363 now, and park outside until the dealer has checked the steering pump wiring. Own a 2021 to 2023 Grand Cherokee with coil-spring suspension? The coil spring recall is on its third campaign, and an earlier repair does not clear you. Own a 2019 to 2023 Cherokee with the two-speed PTU? 26V290 is the fourth PTU recall, the fix is not available yet, and a second letter comes when it exists. Own a 2026 Jeep? Check for 26V531, the rearview camera, and 26V559, the TPMS software, both free software updates. Shopping a 2026 Jeep? The Grand Wagoneer carries five campaigns, the Cherokee and Grand Cherokee four each, and the Recon carries zero. A resurrected nameplate with an ESC-loss recall in its first year back is a reason to buy the second year, or to negotiate like you know.
