Ford’s Backup Camera Has Been Recalled Six Times in Six Years. The $165 Million Fine Didn’t Fix It.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 15, 2026

On October 23, 2026, federal regulators opened a formal investigation into a recall Ford already performed. Five Maverick owners told NHTSA their backup cameras still freeze after the dealer applied the fix, and the agency wants to know whether the remedy works at all.[1] A recall of a recall is embarrassing. A recall of a recall about a camera that has now been recalled six times is a pattern.

Start with the count, because the count is the story. Since September 2020, Ford has filed six separate NHTSA campaigns against its own rearview camera, and the campaign populations add up to more than 3.3 million vehicles.[2] Six campaigns, one component, six years. Nobody at Ford set out to build a camera that fails. They built one anyway, then kept finding new ways for it to fail.

Vehicles across Ford’s six rearview-camera recall campaigns since 2020, counting each expansion once. Some trucks appear in more than one campaign.

Chapter one opened in September 2020, when Ford recalled 620,246 vehicles because the rearview camera could fail outright, including the F-Series, the best-selling vehicle in America.[3] NHTSA opened an investigation into the recall’s timing and scope about a year later, and in 2022 and 2024 Ford quietly expanded the same recall by roughly 24,000 more vehicles. That should have been the end of it. It was the beginning.

September 2024 brought campaign 24V684: 144,516 Mavericks whose camera image could freeze while reversing, thanks to a memory leak in the Connected Touch Radio. Ford knew of two accidents and no injuries.[4] April 2025 brought 25V270, another 160,729 trucks and SUVs whose camera connector could work loose and leave the driver staring at a blank or distorted screen. May 2025 brought the big one, 25V315: 1,075,299 vehicles, because a software error in SYNC 4 versions 1.7 through 1.9 could make the camera image delay, freeze, or never appear at all, and the F-150 alone accounted for 527,371 of them.[2] Ford only reproduced the bug after NHTSA contacted the company in January 2025 about owner complaints.

September 2025 brought 25V572, an expansion of the April recall to 1,456,417 vehicles, covering analog cameras that could go blank, distorted, or inverted, including 365,041 Mustangs and 515,004 Edges.[5] Ford’s own filing counted 44,123 global warranty claims, 195 American owner complaints, and 18 minor crashes, with a defect rate of 2.7 percent. Then July 2026 brought 26V489, another 47,587 F-150s and F-250 Super Dutys, because leaving the camera “Views” menu open on the 8-inch SYNC screen and shifting into reverse leaves the menu sitting on top of the camera image until the driver clears it by hand or drives away faster than 5 mph.[6] A menu that blocks the camera is a camera that does not work, and federal standard FMVSS 111 says so.

Somewhere in the middle of all that, the government lost patience. On November 14, 2024, NHTSA announced a consent order and a $165 million civil penalty, the second-largest in the agency’s 54-year history, behind only Takata’s $200 million airbag order.[3] Ford had failed to recall vehicles with defective rearview cameras in a timely manner, had given NHTSA inaccurate and incomplete recall information, and had failed to file required quarterly recall reports on time. Do the division and the fine works out to roughly $266 for every vehicle in the original 2020 recall. That is the price of moving slowly, per truck.

Consent orders are supposed to change behavior. Ford paid $65 million up front, with $55 million deferred and $45 million earmarked for performance obligations, accepted an independent monitor for three years, agreed to quarterly meetings with NHTSA, and promised to review every recall it had filed in the previous three years to check whether the scope was right.[3] And then, after signing that order, Ford filed three more camera recalls, 25V315, 25V572, and 26V489, and in March 2026 quietly set up a customer satisfaction program offering a free one-time camera repair for up to 15 years on 12,554,966 vehicles.[7] That program is not a safety recall. It is, however, a 12.5-million-vehicle admission that the camera problem is bigger than any single recall.

None of this would matter much if the camera were a luxury. It is not. NHTSA’s own rule-writing estimated that backover crashes killed about 210 people and injured about 15,000 every year before cameras were required, and children under five accounted for 31 percent of the dead.[8] Adults over 70 were another 26 percent. Congress ordered the camera mandate in 2007 after a father backed over his own toddler, and the rule took full effect in May 2018. A camera that freezes, goes blank, or hides behind a menu is not a minor infotainment glitch. It is the safety device failing at the exact moment it exists to work.

The counterargument, at full strength: Ford sells more trucks than anyone, so of course it files more recalls, and a 2.7 percent defect rate on the biggest campaign means 97 out of 100 recalled vehicles never had a bad camera at all. Recall counts are not failure counts, and a company that finds its own defects and files the paperwork is doing the system correctly, which is more than can be said for manufacturers that wait to be caught. Five owner complaints against a 273,301-vehicle query population is a rounding error, and RQ26004 is a question, not a finding. All fair. But the counterargument has a hole in it, and the hole is the fine: NHTSA did not fine Ford $165 million for finding defects, it fined Ford for sitting on one, and for handing the agency bad information while it sat. Finding defects is the system working. Hiding the scope is not.

What this does not prove: the 3.37 million figure counts recall actions, not unique vehicles, because the same F-150 can appear in two or three campaigns, and the expansions overlap by design. Nothing here shows that 3.37 million cameras actually failed; on the largest campaign, Ford’s own estimate says 2.7 percent did. RQ26004 has not established that the Maverick fix failed, only that five owners say it did, and the query’s own summary lists no crashes. Nor does any of this show Ford violated its consent order: the later recalls are separate defects, filed on time, and the order required Ford to review old recalls, not to stop having new problems. What the record does show is narrower and worse in its own way. For six years, across six campaigns and four different failure modes, hardware and software alike, Ford has not shipped a rearview camera it can leave alone.

What This Means for You

If you drive a 2015 through 2019 Ford or Lincoln, check your VIN for 25V572, the analog camera recall: 1.45 million vehicles, and the fix is a replacement camera, free. If you drive a 2021 through 2025 Ford or Lincoln with SYNC 4, check for 25V315: the fix is a software update that can arrive over the air, no dealer visit required, and the recall covers more than a million vehicles, so do not assume yours escaped it. Maverick owners from 2022 through 2024, pay attention to RQ26004. If your camera still freezes after the 24V684 recall repair, that is exactly the complaint that opened the federal query, so file a Vehicle Owner Questionnaire at nhtsa.gov and say so in plain language. Five complaints opened this investigation. Yours could be the one that closes it. And every Ford owner, regardless of model year, should run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before the next oil change, because the camera is now the single most-recalled component in Ford’s modern history, and the next campaign may already be filed.
