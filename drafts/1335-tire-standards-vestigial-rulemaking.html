Your Tires Are Certified by a Test Built for Tires Nobody Makes Anymore

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
March 9, 2028

The most violent thing federal law does to your tires happens in a laboratory. A technician presses a steel plunger into the tread of a brand-new tire and records how much force it takes to punch through. That plunger test is one of three federal certification tests NHTSA announced on October 26 it will finally rewrite, kicking off the first major overhaul of tire safety standards in more than twenty years.[1][2] A steel rod, a fresh tire, a number. This is how Washington has certified the only part of your car that touches the road since before radial tires finished off the bias-ply.

20+
years since the last major overhaul of federal tire safety standards

Here is the shape of the thing. NHTSA published a Federal Register notice on October 27 opening a negotiated rulemaking, and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy will appoint an 18-member advisory committee for a single two-year term.[2] Comments and committee nominations close November 27, nominations going to tiresfac@dot.gov. The committee's charge is to examine "the continued necessity and effectiveness" of the tire strength test (the plunger), the bead unseating resistance test, and the endurance test, plus figure out what to do about non-pneumatic tires and modernize tire markings.[2] Translation from bureaucrat to English: the agency suspects its own tests are fossils, and it is asking the industry to confirm.

The industry obliged, which is the funny part. Robert Braswell, executive director of the American Trucking Associations' Technology & Maintenance Council, told Transport Topics the bead-unseat and plunger tests were "developed many decades ago when bias tires were popularly used," adding: "Some of these tests are really kind of a legacy. They're vestigial, and they're not really necessary with respect to how a radial tire is constructed."[2] When the regulated industry tells the regulator its tests are pointless, believe them. Then ask what replaces them, because that is where the bodies are buried.

Because here is what the plunger test never measured: the things that actually kill people. NHTSA estimates roughly 11,000 tire-related crashes a year. Tires running 25 percent or more underinflated are about three times as likely to be involved in a tire-problem crash, and only around 19 percent of drivers keep their tires properly inflated.[5] Heat. Age. A tread separating at highway speed on rubber that looked fine in the driveway. No plunger has ever been pressed against a six-year-old tire running 20 psi on a July afternoon in Texas, because certification happens exactly once, on a tire that has never touched a public road.[6] The test certifies strength at birth. The deaths happen in middle age, flat and unobserved.

And now the part that should make you put the coffee down. Tire-related crash deaths fell roughly 30 percent between 2017 and 2024, from 738 to 511, all under the frozen, vestigial tests.[5] Sit with that. Either the tests never constrained anything real, in which case modernizing them will not save a single life, or something else did the work: tire-pressure monitoring, mandatory on light vehicles since model year 2008; better manufacturing; the TREAD Act's early-warning reporting, passed in November 2000 after Firestone tread separations on Ford Explorers killed 271 people and injured more than 800.[7] That was twenty-six years ago, and it remains the last time this country seriously rewrote tire rules. This committee might aim at what actually fails. It might polish the plunger. Nobody knows, including the eighteen people who have not been appointed yet.

Steel yourself for the counterargument, because it deserves to be heard at full volume. A negotiated rulemaking is a consensus body, and consensus bodies in Washington get stacked with the people being regulated: tire manufacturers, trucking lobbies, the same industry that just called the tests vestigial. "Modernization" can mean tougher tests aimed at real failure modes, or it can mean streamlined tests that cost less to pass, and the process takes years either way.[2] The deepest version of the objection is the one this column cannot dodge: no certification test, however modern, can ever measure air pressure or age. If underinflation and old rubber are the killers, and the agency is rewriting the test for how strong a new tire is, then it is polishing the wrong instrument while the real lever sits in your driveway, unreadable by any laboratory. A better plunger does not fix a flat tire. Nothing in a Federal Register notice ever will.

What to do with this, since nobody appointed you to the committee. First, the comment period is real: negotiated rulemakings genuinely read public comments, and November 27 is the deadline.[3] Second, read the DOT date code molded into your sidewall, four digits, week and year, like 3221 for the 32nd week of 2021. Rubber degrades on a calendar no test can stop; six years is the number the tire people whisper. Third, the penny test still beats the plunger test for your actual risk: Lincoln's head upside down in the tread, and if you can see all of it, you are driving on a rumor. Fourth, the tire-pressure light is the single highest-leverage safety device in your car, because four out of five drivers are rolling underinflated and the light is the only federal test that runs every day.[5] The plunger test certified your tires at birth. Everything after that is on you.
