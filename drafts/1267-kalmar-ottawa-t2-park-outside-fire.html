Parked, Switched Off, and Still a Fire Risk: 16,127 Yard Trucks Recalled

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
December 31, 2027

Kalmar recalled 16,127 Ottawa T2 terminal tractors in October 2026 for a defect that breaks the basic deal of vehicle safety. A cabin circuit board can short and start a fire while the truck is parked with the ignition off, so the most dangerous thing one of these trucks can do is nothing at all. No driver required. No speed required. No road required. Just a parked truck, a degraded insulator, and time.[1]

Start with what this machine is, because most readers have never seen one on a highway. A terminal tractor, also called a yard spotter, spends its life inside ports, warehouses, and distribution terminals, shuttling trailers from dock to dock. It rarely touches a public road. Its workplace is the facility itself. A fire in one of these trucks is therefore not a highway crash. It is a building fire that happens to start inside a truck, surrounded by trailers, fuel, and warehoused goods, possibly at 2 a.m. when the only witness is a security camera.[2]

Inside the cab sits a power distribution board. Kalmar says its insulation can break down, letting current take a shortcut it was never meant to take. Short circuits make heat. Heat makes fire. Nothing about that chain needs the key turned. Federal regulators stated the consequence in one flat sentence: a short increases fire risk even when the vehicle is parked and the ignition is in the off position. Read that twice. Off is supposed to be the safe state. Here off is just a state.[1]

Until the repair, regulators advise owners to park outside and away from structures. Pause on the comedy of that order. A machine whose entire job is working inside a facility has been told to sleep outside, like a dog that chewed the furniture. Fleet managers must now reorganize yard operations around trucks that cannot go home. Compliance is correct. Compliance is also absurd. Both things are true, which is usually how you know a story is real.[3]

Kalmar's own framing deserves a hard look. Its release calls thermal events very rare, involving less than 0.001 percent of the total Ottawa T2 fleet. That denominator is doing heavy lifting. It counts every T2 ever built, not the 16,127 trucks actually recalled, and Kalmar never states either number plainly enough to check the arithmetic. Against that percentage stands a regulator telling owners to move the truck away from the building. When NHTSA issues a park-outside order, the denominator debate is over. Percentages reassure shareholders. Park-outside orders protect warehouses.[2]

Fairness demands the other side, stated without a sneer. No injuries or deaths are attached to this campaign in the published material. Kalmar filed voluntarily in early October, the fix is cheap and complete, and owner letters were scheduled by December 4, 2026. Yard tractors are commercial equipment run by professionals, not passenger cars, so a smoldering cab is likelier to be spotted and handled. One earlier Ottawa recall, 52 trucks in 2015 over reversed ABS wiring, shows this nameplate has been through federal paperwork before without becoming a byword for danger. A proportionate reader could call this a well-handled, low-frequency electrical correction.[4]

What this piece did not prove: no count of actual fires is published, so the per-recall fire rate cannot be computed from Kalmar's fleet-wide percentage. No source says whether any fire happened inside a building. And the 2014-2026 span means twelve model years share the board design, but the filings do not say whether every year carried identical hardware. Check a used Ottawa T2 for the fuse retrofit documentation before trusting the hour meter. If a yard truck smells of hot plastic, move it away from buildings and trailers first and file the maintenance ticket second. And remember the lesson this recall teaches about every machine you own: off is a switch position, not a guarantee.[1]
