Waymo Declared Its School Bus Problem Fixed. NHTSA Says It Wasn't.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

Waymo declared its school bus problem fixed on November 17, 2025.[1] NHTSA spent the next year counting the robotaxis that kept passing stopped school buses anyway, and on November 6 it opened a formal recall query to ask whether the fix did anything at all.[1]

Start with what actually breaks. A Waymo robotaxi approaches a stopped school bus, red lights flashing, stop arm extended, children boarding. It slows. Sometimes it stops. Then, according to Waymo's own recall report, it moves again before the warning ends, rolling past a bus that is still legally a wall.[2] That is not a sensor failure. Cameras see the bus fine. It is a decision failure: the software reads the scene, classifies it correctly enough to brake, and then decides the scene is over while the stop arm is still out.

354
Days from Waymo declaring its school-bus software remedy complete (November 17, 2025) to NHTSA opening recall query RQ26006 (November 6, 2026).

October 2025 brought the footage: a Waymo maneuvering around a stopped bus in Atlanta while children unloaded.[2] NHTSA opened a preliminary evaluation. Waymo pushed a software update fleet-wide between November 5 and 17 and said every affected car had it. Austin ISD counted 19 illegal passes by November 20.[3] Waymo filed the voluntary recall, 25E084, on December 10, covering 3,067 vehicles. Paperwork, after the fact: the recall ratified a software push already declared complete. January 12, 2026, gave the NTSB its own case file: a Waymo in Austin stopped for a bus, then proceeded with the stop arms extended, and six vehicles passed the bus.[3] No crash. No injuries. Just a second federal agency opening its own inquiry.

Now run the numbers NHTSA published November 6. Recall query RQ26006 covers 2,895 vehicles, 172 fewer than the 3,067 in the original recall, and the filings do not say why.[1] Nearly a full year separates the declared fix from the query, and by NHTSA's own count a majority of the incidents it identified happened after the remedy date. Eight incident reports appear in the resume summary, crash and injury counts marked confidential, Waymo's own incident total marked confidential, and no total encounter count anywhere in the public file. Without a denominator, nobody can compute a failure rate: eight reports could be eight failures out of ten school-bus encounters or eight out of ten thousand. What the filing proves is narrower and worse: the fix did not eliminate the behavior it was built to eliminate.

Why a stopped school bus matters this much: NHTSA's FARS data counts 1,069 people killed in school-transportation-related crashes from 2015 to 2024, an average of 107 a year.[4] Only 119 of those were bus occupants. Seventy percent were occupants of other vehicles, and 162 were pedestrians, 117 of them struck by the bus itself. Of the 204 school-age children killed, 77 were pedestrians, and 55 percent of those were between 5 and 10 years old, the age group most likely to dart out from behind a bus into a lane a robotaxi has just decided is clear. Human drivers are no model here either: a 2025 national survey logged 67,258 illegal school-bus passes in a single day.[5]

The counterargument, at full strength: Waymo found the problem, pushed a fleet-wide fix within weeks, filed a voluntary recall, and says it has received no citations since March 2026.[3] By that account the system worked, defect found, fix deployed, behavior corrected, and the November query is a regulator doing due diligence on a closed chapter rather than catching a live failure. That reading has real weight on the citation point, and no child was hurt in any incident in the federal file. But it does not answer NHTSA's central sentence. A fix that works eventually is not the same as a fix that worked when declared, and the regulator, not the company, gets to decide when the chapter is closed.

What this does not prove: a recall query is not a finding of defect and not a new recall, and it can close without further action. No crash or injury is reported in the NHTSA resume. Waymo's no-citations-since-March claim, if accurate, means the behavior may have stopped months before the query opened. NHTSA published no total incident count, so "a majority" is the only proportion on the record, and whether the January 12 incident counts as six incidents or one event in that tally is not stated. Do not read the query as proof the behavior is ongoing, or as proof it has stopped.

What This Means for You

If you live in a Waymo city, Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin, or Atlanta, and a driverless car passes your child's bus, report it to the school district and file a complaint at nhtsa.gov, referencing recall query RQ26006. Austin ISD's log of 19 passes and 8 citations is the reason this query exists; districts that do not log violations leave them out of the federal record entirely. Riders should know nothing in the filings suggests a current, ongoing failure, but the episode is a reminder that a software recall is only as good as its verification, and verification here took 354 days and a second federal probe.
