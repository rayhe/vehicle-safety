## News peg
IIHS updated its "Older people" fatality-statistics page in June 2026 with full 1975–2024 trend tables. The headline numbers create an apparent paradox that nobody has written up for a general audience: **5,679 people age 70+ died in motor vehicle crashes in 2024 — a 50% increase since 1975 — while the per-capita death rate fell 47% over the same span** (25.9 → 13.6 per 100,000). The 70+ population nearly tripled (14.6M → 41.7M). More old people, driving more miles, dying in greater absolute numbers while each individual's risk falls.

## Primary sources (4)
1. **IIHS, "Older people" fatality statistics** (posted June 2026; FARS-based, 1975–2024 tables).
   https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/fatality-statistics/detail/older-people/2014
   - 5,679 deaths age 70+ in 2024; 50% increase since 1975; rate down 47% (25.9→13.6/100K).
   - Per-mile fatal crash involvement (2022, NHTS mileage): **85+ = 7.9 per 100M miles vs 16–19 = 7.5**. The oldest drivers edge out teenagers.
   - "The increased fatal crash risk among older drivers is largely due to their increased susceptibility to injuries, particularly to the chest, and medical complications, rather than an increased tendency to get into crashes (Cicchino, 2015)."
   - Who dies: in crashes involving a 70+ driver, **57% of deaths were the older drivers themselves**; for under-30 drivers, only 37% were the young driver and 46% were occupants of other vehicles/peds/cyclists. Old drivers mostly kill themselves; young drivers mostly kill other people.
   - Crash types: multiple-vehicle intersection crashes = **43% of fatal involvements for 80+** vs ~24% for ages 20–64.
   - 70% of 70+ deaths were passenger-vehicle occupants; 18% pedestrians.
2. **NHTSA, "Older Drivers" (Countermeasures That Work)**.
   https://www.nhtsa.gov/book/countermeasures-that-work/older-drivers
   - 65+ population up 28% (2014–2023); 65+ were 19% of all traffic fatalities in 2023.
   - License-holding among 65+: 78% (2001) → 89% (2021); 59% of 85+ held licenses in 2020 vs 50% in 2000.
   - Fatalities involving older drivers: 8,209 (2021) vs 5,613 (2009 low) — but population grew faster than fatalities.
3. **IIHS, "'Retirement vehicles' raise the risk of crash fatalities for older drivers"** (Dec 7, 2021; Cicchino et al.).
   https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/retirement-vehicles-raise-the-risk-of-crash-fatalities-for-older-drivers
   - Drivers 70+ significantly more likely to be in vehicles 16+ years old; less likely to have ESC or head-protecting side airbags.
   - No ESC → 37% higher odds of driver fatality (70+). No standard side airbags → **double** the odds.
   - Drivers 75+ are ~4x as likely to die as middle-aged drivers in a side-impact crash, ~3x in frontal.
   - Quote (Cicchino): "Persuading older drivers to take another look at the vehicles they're driving could reduce crash fatalities substantially."
4. **Cicchino, J.B. (2015), "Why have fatality rates among older drivers declined?"** *Accident Analysis & Prevention*.
   https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/bibliography/ref/2086
   - Fragility (death risk given a crash) accounted for **77% of the elevated fatality rate for 75–79** and 68% for 80+ vs middle-aged drivers (2005–08).
   - Crash-risk declines (fewer crashes per mile) drove most of the improvement over time.

Supporting (secondary): KFF Health News/Paula Span (Sep 2026) on older pedestrians — "aren't injured at a greater rate… but more likely to die" — same fragility mechanism, used for one line of context only.

## Original contribution
1. The 50%-up/47%-down paradox arithmetic, with the population denominator made explicit (14.6M → 41.7M) — the "crisis" is demography, not deteriorating driving.
2. The 85+ (7.9) vs teen (7.5) per-mile comparison, framed by the fragility decomposition: same rate, opposite causes (recklessness vs breakability).
3. The "who dies" inversion (57% self vs 37% self) — reframes the policy debate from "protect us from old drivers" to "protect old drivers from crashes."
4. The compounding: the most fragile drivers ride in the oldest, smallest, least-equipped vehicles — a fixable mismatch, not fate.

## Strongest counterargument
Per-mile rates for 85+ rest on a small mileage denominator (10.2B miles, NHTS survey-based, not odometer readings) — the 7.9 figure is noisy. And 43% intersection involvement points to real driving errors (gap/speed misjudgment, per Cicchino & McCartt 2015), not pure fragility — so "it's not their driving" overstates the case. The truth is both: they crash somewhat more per mile AND die far more per crash.

## Limitations
- FARS captures fatal crashes only; injury-only crashes invisible.
- NHTS mileage is survey-estimated; ±uncertainty largest for the 85+ group (fewest miles).
- Cicchino 2015 decomposition uses 2005–08 data; fleet safety has improved since (side airbags, ESC now near-universal in new cars).
- Vehicle-age correlation (retirement vehicles) is observational; income and rurality confound.
- 2024 is the latest final FARS year; 2025 estimates are preliminary.

## Actionable insights (required)
- For drivers 70+: the car matters more than at any other age — ESC, side curtain airbags, and larger/heavier vehicle are the three highest-leverage choices. The 16-year-old sedan is the worst possible "retirement vehicle."
- For families: the conversation shouldn't start with "take the keys." Start with "upgrade the car." A 5-star, ESC-equipped, side-airbag vehicle cuts the fragility penalty substantially.
- Intersections are the specific danger zone (43% for 80+): protected left turns, intersection-assist / cross-traffic tech, and route planning that favors signalized turns.
- Check VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls — older cars have had more time to accumulate unrepaired recalls.
