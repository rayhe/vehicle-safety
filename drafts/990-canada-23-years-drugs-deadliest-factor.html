Half the Drivers Who Die Alone at 4am Are on Drugs. Canada Counted 23 Years of Them.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 25, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, the deadliest thing on Canadian roads stopped being alcohol more than a decade ago, and nobody told the breathalyzer. A fact sheet released this year by the Traffic Injury Research Foundation, covering every fatal collision in Canada from 2000 through 2023, puts drug involvement at 41.2 percent of traffic fatalities in 2023, up from 10.7 percent in 2000, against 29.3 percent for alcohol, 31.1 for speeding, 21.4 for distraction, and 6.2 for fatigue.[1] Drugs did not just join the leaderboard. They lapped it.

41.2%
Share of Canadian traffic fatalities involving drugs in 2023, up from 10.7% in 2000

Start with the body count, because Dale always starts with the body count. Drug-related traffic deaths went from 230 in 2000 to 571 in 2023. That is 341 additional dead people, a group large enough to fill a regional jet, and the share of all road deaths nearly quadrupled while it happened. Since 2013, drugs have outpaced every other factor the researchers track. For thirteen straight years, the most common thing found in a dead driver's bloodstream north of the border has not been beer; it has been everything else.

Now the fingerprint, because this dataset has one of the sharpest I have ever seen. Nearly half of all fatalities between 3:00am and 5:59am were drug-related, Saturdays ran about 44 percent, and forty-one percent of the drug-related deaths happened in summer. And here is the one that rearranged my priors: roughly one in two people who died in single-occupant collisions were drug-related, compared with about one in five in vehicles carrying five or more people.[1] Run that ratio and you get a loneliness multiplier of 2.5: drug involvement is two and a half times more common when the driver dies alone than when the car is full. Passengers, it turns out, are a surprisingly effective drug test.

Age splits the pharmacology neatly: between 2019 and 2023, an average of 53.7 percent of fatally injured drivers tested positive for drugs, but the 25-to-34 cohort hit 63.8 percent, with 20-to-24 at 59.7 and 35-to-44 at 59.4. Young adults skew cannabis; older adults skew central nervous system depressants. And the stimulant line deserves its own sentence, because nobody is talking about it: drivers testing positive for CNS stimulants rose from 13.9 percent to 19.6 percent, a 41 percent relative increase.[1] Somewhere between the weed discourse and the opioid crisis, cocaine and meth quietly took a bigger seat in the fatal-crash data.

Cannabis deserves the honest treatment, so here it is. Before Canada legalized recreational marijuana, 22.4 percent of fatally injured drivers tested positive. It peaked at 30.4 percent in 2020, then settled near 25 percent from 2022 onward. The one demographic that moved was the youngest: ages 20 to 24 went from 35.9 percent positive before legalization to 46.4 percent after, a 29 percent relative jump.[1] South of the border, the pattern rhymes. Wright State researchers reviewed coroner records for 246 Ohio drivers who died in crashes between 2019 and 2024 and found 41.9 percent had active THC in their blood at an average of 30.7 ng/mL, six to fifteen times the 2-to-5 ng/mL thresholds most states use, and legalization in 2023 did not move the rate (42.1 percent before, 45.2 percent after).[2][3] Legalization did not create this problem; it photographed it.

What to do with all this depends on which side of the data you sit on. If you drive the late-night weekend shift, rideshare or otherwise, the 3-to-6am window is when impaired drivers are most overrepresented, and a full car is measurably safer company than an empty one. If you make policy, the stimulant surge and the 63.8 percent positive rate among dead 25-to-34-year-olds say enforcement and education should chase demographics and hours, not just substances. And for everyone still treating cannabis like the harmless half of impaired driving: 30.7 ng/mL is not a contact high. That is consumption close enough to the crash to show up in a coroner's blood draw, and the lead surgeon on the Ohio study said the levels surprised even him.[3]

A fair accounting of the limits, because the toxicology desk does not grade on vibes. "Drug-related" means a driver tested positive, not that the drug caused the crash, and the TIRF authors say so explicitly; twenty-three years of improving panels and higher testing rates account for part of the climb. THC lingers in blood for days while impairment lasts hours, so a positive test is not proof of impairment at the wheel. Canada's testing regime differs from America's, so cross-border comparisons are directional, not precise, and the dataset ends at 2023. The strongest counterargument deserves full weight: if coroners simply test more drivers with broader panels than they did in 2000, the fourfold rise measures lab capacity more than road reality. Plausible, except the share stopped climbing and plateaued near 40 percent instead of rising forever, and lab capacity cannot explain why the positives concentrate at 4am on Saturdays in young men's bloodstreams. Testing finds drugs everywhere, but the pattern says they concentrate somewhere.

The math, shown so you can check it: 571 minus 230 equals 341 additional deaths; 41.2 divided by 10.7 equals 3.85, the near-quadrupling of the share; 50 divided by 20 equals the 2.5 loneliness multiplier; (19.6 minus 13.9) divided by 13.9 equals 0.41, the stimulant surge; (46.4 minus 35.9) divided by 35.9 equals 0.29, the post-legalization jump among 20-to-24-year-olds; 41.2 divided by 29.3 equals 1.41, the factor by which drugs now exceed alcohol. Every numerator and denominator above comes from the two cited datasets. Nobody else ran these ratios, so you are welcome.
