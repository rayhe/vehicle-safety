The Delivery Van Where Drugs Beat Alcohol

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 11, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, drunk beats drugged in American fatal crashes by a wide margin. Alcohol shows up in almost twice as many dead drivers as drugs do, across nearly every vehicle ever sold. Except two.

Out of 307 vehicle models in the FARS toxicology tables, exactly two with at least 100 drivers in the data flip the ratio, with more drivers testing positive for drugs than for alcohol. One is the Suzuki Grand Vitara, a forgotten soft-roader with 158 data points and nobody left to defend it. The other is the Nissan NV200, a commercial delivery van, and its numbers are worth a closer look because the van is still on the road in enormous numbers.

212 NV200 drivers appear in the fatal-crash toxicology data, with 27 testing positive for drugs against 25 for alcohol. That works out to 12.7 percent versus 11.8 percent, making the NV200 the only work vehicle in America where the drugs beat the alcohol. For context, the all-vehicle drug leaderboard is topped by the usual suspects: the Oldsmobile Alero at 18.4 percent, the Buick Park Avenue at 16.6, the BMW M5 at 15.6. The little Nissan sits sixth, ahead of the Mercedes GLK and the Jeep Commander, which is an absurd sentence to type about a van whose top speed is a rumor.

So what is going on inside the little Nissan? Honest answer: probably less than the headline suggests, and the toxicology report itself is the reason. FARS records a drug-positive result for any detectable drug, including prescription medications and THC metabolites that can linger for up to thirty days, while alcohol clears the body in hours. Different clocks. A driver who used cannabis two weeks ago and a driver on a prescribed sedative both count as drug-positive where the equivalent alcohol use would test clean, which means what looks like a drug crisis in a delivery van may mostly measure detectable cannabis use among working drivers rather than drug-impaired driving.

Then there is the sample size, which deserves its own moment of silence: 27 versus 25. Two drivers. Move two toxicology results from one column to the other and the entire phenomenon vanishes, which means the phenomenon might be noise wearing a trench coat. There is also a quieter bias at work, since commercial drivers face employer alcohol testing far more often than drug testing, suppressing the alcohol numerator before FARS ever sees it.

Still, the number is worth sitting with, because it exposes how carelessly people cite toxicology tables. Drug-positive is not drug-impaired, and anyone who treats a FARS drug hit as proof of intoxication at the wheel is laundering a lab result into a moral judgment. A lab result is not a verdict. If you run a fleet of small vans, the takeaway is narrower and more useful: your alcohol policy is probably fine, and your drug-testing panel is doing more of the work than you assumed. Test for what actually impairs, test close to the shift, and stop pretending a screen from last month tells you anything about Tuesday's route. Read the table, not the headline.
