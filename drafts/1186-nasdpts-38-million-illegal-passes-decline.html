Trend Watch

38.4 Million Illegal School Bus Passes, Down Two Years Running/h1>
Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

I ran the numbers on the National Association of State Directors of Pupil Transportation Services' 2026 illegal-passing census.[1] Then I ran them again, because the headline figure — 38.4 million illegal passes of stopped school buses in a single school year — deserved a second look, and the second look found the actual story: America's most-ignored traffic law is being ignored less: illegal school bus passings are down 15 percent in two years.

38.4 million
illegal passes of stopped school buses projected for the 2025–26 school year, down from 45.2 million two years earlier

Here is the census, because the methodology is the story. In a voluntary one-day count during the 2025–2026 school year, 101,334 school bus drivers — 26.2 percent of the nation's total, across 39 states and the District of Columbia — reported 56,024 vehicles blowing past their stopped buses.[1] NASDPTS extrapolated that to every bus driver in America: roughly 213,800 illegal passes per school day, or 38.4 million across a 180-day school year. Two school years ago the projection was 45.2 million; last year it was 39.3 million.

Scale it to the fleet and the number gets personal. About 20 million children ride roughly 500,000 school buses each day, which works out to one illegal pass per bus every two or three school days — your kid's bus, twice a week, with someone blowing the stop arm.[3] Delaware ran its own one-day count this month: 1,108 drivers reported 572 violations, and nearly 300 of them came from behind the bus, where a crossing child is least visible.[5] The policy machine has noticed. In March, the Governors Highway Safety Association and BusPatrol published the first 50-state action plan on illegal passing, with 69 recommendations.[3] The Brake for Kids Act (H.R.2348/S.2812) would order NHTSA to run a national awareness campaign.[4] And at this month's National Association for Pupil Transportation conference, NTSB and FMCSA officials sat down with the bus people to talk federal priorities.[2]

Now the cold water, because this column does not do victory laps on voluntary surveys. Participation changes every year, and the extrapolation assumes the 74 percent of drivers who didn't participate see the same violation rate as the 26 percent who did — an unproven assumption that could easily manufacture a trend out of sample noise. Worse, violations are not deaths. From 2000 through 2022, illegal passing of stopped school buses killed 55 people — about two and a half a year — against roughly 40 million annual violations.[6] The buses themselves remain the safest vehicles on the road; what the census measures is disrespect for the law, not carnage. And nothing in the data says what caused the decline — cameras, awareness, or luck are all undefeated hypotheses. Still: between 2014 and 2023, 169 pedestrians died in school-bus-related crashes versus 111 bus occupants, so the curb remains the dangerous place.[6] National School Bus Safety Week starts Monday.

If you drive: red lights flashing plus stop arm extended means stop, both directions, in all 50 states — and the Delaware numbers say the driver approaching from behind the bus is the one most likely to endanger a child. If you're a parent: teach the ten-foot rule, wait for the driver's hand signal, make eye contact with the driver before your kid steps off the curb. If you run a district: stop-arm cameras work — cited drivers in Florida's Lee County reoffend at under 10 percent[7] — and the Brake for Kids Act deserves your form letter. 38.4 million is still an embarrassing number for a country that claims to love its children. It's just, for the second year in a row, a smaller embarrassing number — and in road safety, smaller is the whole game.
