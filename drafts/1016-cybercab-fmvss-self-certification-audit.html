Tesla Certified a Car With No Brake Pedal. The Law Says the Brakes Need a Foot Control. NHTSA Wants Sworn Answers by Tomorrow.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 29, 2026

On September 3, Tesla began charging riders in Austin for trips in the Cybercab, a two-seat robotaxi with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no mirrors.[4] At 3:42 p.m. Eastern that same afternoon, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Audit Query AQ26002 into how Tesla certified the vehicle as compliant with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards.[1] On September 10 the agency escalated to a formal Special Order compelling sworn answers to 21 requests by September 30. That deadline is tomorrow.[2]

21
Sworn requests Tesla must answer by September 30, under a Special Order carrying a $139,356,994 civil penalty ceiling

The collision here is textual. FMVSS 135, section S5.3.1: “The service brakes shall be activated by means of a foot control.”[5] The Cybercab has no foot control. FMVSS 111 requires an inside rearview mirror and a driver-side outside mirror, each of unit magnification; the Cybercab has cameras where mirrors would be. FMVSS 203 and 204 govern steering column displacement, and there is no steering column. FMVSS 208 builds its occupant protection math around a driver's designated seating position, and there is no driver. Somewhere in Tesla's certification file sits a determination that each of these requirements is simply “inapplicable” to a car built without the hardware the requirements describe. NHTSA's audit asks, in so many words, to see the homework.

One of the 21 requests is reportedly the most uncomfortable sentence in the file: whether temporary steering wheels or pedals appeared during testing and vanished before deployment.[2] Read that twice. Then consider the road Tesla declined to take. Amazon's Zoox spent roughly four years navigating the Part 555 exemption process, winning commercial clearance on July 30, 2026, capped at 2,500 vehicles a year.[3] Tesla skipped the line entirely. Self-certification is the same process every automaker uses, the company will surely note, and inapplicability determinations are routine engineering judgments. True. Also true: nobody has ever used that process to certify a production car with no brake pedal.

The strongest defense is arithmetic, not law. Tesla's road-going fleet barely registers in the fatal-crash ledger: across FARS 2014 through 2023, the Model 3, Model Y, Model S, and Model X account for 278 deaths combined, against estimated fleets totaling roughly 3.7 million vehicles.[6] An audit query is not a defect finding. A Special Order is oversight doing its job, not a verdict. The Cybercab has carried paying passengers for weeks with no reported crash. If the car itself is safe, this paperwork fight is theater, and Zoox's four-year exemption odyssey is the broken process, not Tesla's shortcut.

What to do with this: if you took a Cybercab ride in Austin, you rode in a vehicle whose legal right to the road is now under sworn federal examination. That does not make the ride unsafe. It makes the compliance story unfinished. Watch what lands after September 30, because NHTSA's ruling will write the template for every wheel-less car that wants your city's streets next. Tesla's gamble is that “inapplicable” is a box the manufacturer gets to check on its own. Tomorrow, under oath, it has to defend the checkmarks.

What this article did not prove: Tesla's September 30 responses are not public yet; this piece reports the questions, not the answers. Whether any FMVSS was actually violated is undetermined, and AQ26002 is an audit, not a defect investigation. The Special Order's 21 requests are described through secondary reporting (Electrek first, then WebProNews and TechTimes); the order text itself was not independently reviewed. FARS Tesla figures cover 2014 through 2023 only, and raw death counts are not exposure-adjusted safety rankings. “No reported crash” for the Cybercab fleet reflects an absence of reporting on a weeks-old fleet of dozens of vehicles, not a safety finding.
