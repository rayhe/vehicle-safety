**Working title:** "IIHS Asked Delivery Vans to Stop for a Motorcycle. Most of Them Did Nothing."

**Kill test:** PASS. Second wave of IIHS's brand-new commercial-vehicle rating program, released Oct 8, 2026 (6 days old). The pipeline already covered wave 1 (occupant-protection checklist, #June/July) — this is a distinct, newer dataset: crash-avoidance (front crash prevention vehicle-to-vehicle + pedestrian). Nobody has run this yet in the queue. Novel angles:
1. **The motorcycle is invisible.** The motorcycle target was the single biggest challenge; several systems "did not issue warnings or slow at all in most of the motorcycle trials." The Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 — an electric van purpose-built for last-mile delivery — recorded ZERO speed reduction and NO timely warning in any of its 6 motorcycle runs. 0-for-6 against a target it was designed to detect.
2. **Two ancient vans have no AEB at all.** The Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500 earned "Poor" in BOTH tests because they ship without automatic emergency braking — only optional forward collision warning. These are among the most common work vans in America, still on sale, and the IIHS just graded them poor/poor.
3. **The child-dummy disappearing act.** Pedestrian tests showed vehicles detecting a child-sized dummy then losing track of it as they approached — likely tall front ends and sensor positioning. A system that sees the kid and then forgets.
4. **The low-beam paradox.** Some systems performed noticeably worse with low-beam headlights than high beams in nighttime pedestrian tests — so the lighting recall pipeline (cf. #1054, #1067) and the avoidance pipeline meet.
5. **Nobody got Good in both.** Of 12 vehicles, exactly zero earned Good in both vehicle-to-vehicle and pedestrian tests. The best V2V (Sprinter 2500, Good) only managed Acceptable pedestrian. The best pedestrian (BrightDrop 400, Rivian RCV, Good) were Acceptable and Marginal in V2V respectively. The top performers in each category were cargo vans, per IIHS.

## Original contribution
1. **The warning-braking divorce:** The ProMaster 2500 is the only vehicle with a timely warning in every car and motorcycle run — and it barely braked at speed. Warning systems and braking systems are graded as one package, but the data shows they fail independently: some vans see you and do nothing (ProMaster), some do nothing and see nothing (Rivian vs. motorcycle). The composite score hides two different failure modes.
2. **Motorcycle invisibility map:** Turning the motorcycle runs into the headline metric: across published per-vehicle results, the motorcycle target accounts for the majority of Poor ratings. Rivian 0/6, Transit "regardless of speed and position." The vehicles least likely to see you are the ones in your lane most often (last-mile delivery vans log the most urban miles).
3. **Still legal to sell:** The Express 2500 and Savana 2500 remain on sale with no AEB while passenger cars effectively must have it — a two-tier safety market the federal rule hasn't closed. Cross-reference: NHTSA's own 2023 cost-benefit (155 lives/year) has sat unfinalized for 3+ years.

## Limitations
- IIHS tested a defined cohort of current-generation models; results don't generalize to all commercial vehicles, older model years, or configurations with different option packages.
- Track testing uses instrumented targets and controlled speeds (31/37/43 mph); real-world motorcycle encounters involve motion, lean angles, and speeds the test doesn't replicate. A Good rating here is not a guarantee of real-world detection.
- The "timely warning" threshold (2.1 s) is an IIHS construct; manufacturers dispute its real-world relevance.
- I did not independently verify the per-vehicle ratings against IIHS's own rating pages; scores are as reported by secondary press coverage of the IIHS release. I link IIHS's ratings page for verification.
- No crash or fatality data is attributed to these specific AEB gaps; the 6,215 figure covers all crashes involving heavy/medium-duty vehicles or light vans, not AEB-preventable ones.

## Counterargument (strongest)
The commercial vehicle market's AEB gap reflects a different operating reality, not just negligence: Class 3+ vehicles and work vans are bought by fleets on total cost of ownership, and the sensors that work on a Camry don't transplant cleanly to a 14,000-lb GVWR van with a tall, flat nose — the child-dummy tracking loss is an engineering problem, not a conspiracy. Fleets also replace vehicles on longer cycles, so today's ratings will take years to reach the fleet. And the raw numbers cut both ways: the Sprinter 2500's Good V2V rating proves it CAN be done on a tall van, which weakens the "it's just hard" defense — but a single Good in a 12-vehicle cohort is also thin evidence that it's cheap or easy.
