The Bus That Brakes Itself Is Back. Nova's Loose Air Lines Didn't Stay Fixed.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 29, 2026

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. In September 2026, NHTSA logged campaign 26V604000: certain 2024–2025 Nova Bus LFS transit buses whose parking brake may unintentionally activate.[1] The remedy is an inspection and a proper tightening of the air line connections, with owner letters going out November 21. It looks, bolt for bolt, like the same ten-minute wrench turn Nova Bus prescribed in 2024, which raises the obvious question: what happened to the first one?

5,680
Nova LFS buses built over 27 model years whose low-air warning light fails with the engine off

Back in 2024 it was 24V518: 901 LFS and LFS Artic buses, model years 2021 through 2024, with loose connections on the parking brake valve's air lines.[2] The cause, in Nova's own filing, was incorrect torque applied to the connector. A loose fitting can leak air suddenly, and a sudden air leak applies the parking brake automatically: no brake lights, and no warning beforehand. Nova found the defect during a predelivery road test, which is the good-news version of this story: the bus braked itself before it had passengers.

What happened next is the bad-news version. Nova Bus has no dealer network, so the recall went to transit agencies, not service bays.[3] Six months after owner notification, 528 of 900 buses had been remedied.[4] Do the subtraction yourself. 372 buses were still rolling with the loose fitting, in revenue service, with standing passengers. You cannot check a bus's VIN; you board it.

Between the two parking-brake recalls came the quiet one. In June 2026, Nova filed 26V355: 5,680 buses, model years 1997 through 2024, whose low brake air pressure telltale fails to illuminate when the engine is off.[5] That is a direct violation of FMVSS 121, which requires a visual warning when the ignition is on and air pressure drops below 60 PSI. Nova traced it to incorrect dashboard firmware, supplied by Actia Corporation. Read that again: for up to 27 years, the dashboard on these buses would not tell the driver the brakes were losing air. The one instrument designed to catch exactly this failure mode was broken across the fleet.

Here is how air brakes work, briefly, because the paradox needs it. Compressed air holds the spring brake off. Lose the air and the spring wins; the brake applies. It is a fail-safe, and it is correct engineering: better a bus that stops itself than a 15-ton bus with no brakes at all. The problem is the fail-safe firing by accident, at speed, in traffic, with no brake lights to warn the car behind and no warning for the driver up front. A city bus carries 40 or more people, and almost none of them are belted. In a sudden uncommanded stop, the standing passengers are the crumple zone.

Now the strongest defense, stated at full strength, because it deserves it. The parking brake applying on an air leak is the system working as designed; the alternative is genuinely worse. Nova caught the 2024 defect in its own testing, filed voluntarily, and the fix takes ten minutes per bus. Transit buses remain among the safest vehicles per passenger-mile on American roads, and no crashes, injuries, or deaths appear in the public filings for any of these campaigns.[8] That is the good news. Meanwhile, the fitting came loose again, the warning light was broken for decades, and two out of five buses missed the first fix entirely.

What you can do is limited, which is the point. You cannot run a VIN check on the bus you boarded this morning. But your transit agency is a public body with a maintenance ledger, so ask it whether its Nova LFS fleet is current on 24V518, 26V355, and 26V604. If a bus ever brakes hard without warning, file the complaint at nhtsa.gov anyway; the defect pipeline runs on complaint volume. And if you run an agency: the fix is a torque wrench and ten minutes. Those 372 buses that never got the 2024 fix are still out there.
