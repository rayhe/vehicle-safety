One Bad Hose, 33,930 Vehicles: The Cummins L9 Cascade Reaches Your City Bus

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

On October 23, Gillig recalled 1,853 Low Floor transit buses because a fuel hose can separate from its fitting, leak diesel, and stall the engine.[1] Two days earlier, Autocar recalled 1,339 garbage trucks for the identical hose.[2] Your city bus and your garbage truck now share a part number, and I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, because the total kept climbing: 33,930 vehicles recalled downstream of a single Cummins hose.

33,930
Vehicles recalled in ten downstream campaigns, all traced to Cummins L9 hose part D563027BX03

Every campaign counts exactly once, so here is the ledger. PACCAR leads with 26,729 Kenworth and Peterbilt trucks.[3] Daimler Truck follows at 2,592, then Gillig at 1,853, Autocar at 1,339, Nova Bus at 453, Sutphen fire trucks at 410, Shyft at 373, Tiffin motorhomes at 177, Battle Motors at 4, and Temsa at zero.[4] Add them up: 26,729 + 2,592 + 1,853 + 1,339 + 453 + 410 + 373 + 177 + 4 + 0 = 33,930. PACCAR alone is 78.8 percent of the total, which makes Gillig, at 5.5 percent, easy to miss. It is also the largest passenger-vehicle installment in the whole cascade, bigger than Nova Bus by more than four to one, and it went uncounted in every prior tally because nobody wrote it up.

Upstream sits the supplier recall that started all of this. Cummins filed equipment recall 26E066 on September 28, covering 39,052 L9 engines built between December 2020 and April 2026, and it was the fifth Cummins recall of the same hose in five years.[5] Cummins estimates only 3 percent of those engines actually have the defect, which works out to roughly 1,172 bad engines by arithmetic, a figure the filing does not state itself. That is the paradox at the center of the cascade: about a thousand defective hoses produced nearly 34,000 recalled vehicles, because nobody can tell which thousand without replacing all of them.

Three names on Cummins’s own buyer list still have no vehicle recall on file. Cummins named thirteen buyers in its Part 573 filing; ten have filed, and New Flyer Industries, Big Rig, and Grove Worldwide had not surfaced in NHTSA’s weekly roundups through November 3.[5] Maybe their filings are already drafted. Maybe their buses run different hose routings. Either way, the cascade is not finished until the list is closed, and five weeks after the supplier filing, three buyers remain open. Temsa, meanwhile, filed a recall for zero vehicles, which reads like a joke until you realize it is the opposite: a manufacturer filing paperwork for a population of none, just in case.

Credit where the ledger demands it. Cummins found the defect, filed promptly, named every buyer, and every downstream manufacturer filed within five weeks with free remedies. No injuries, fires, or crashes appear in any of these campaign records, and the risks are stated as potential outcomes, not events. A 3 percent defect rate means roughly 97 percent of the recalled vehicles were never in danger. Gillig’s own history even argues for the system: its 2024 steering-spline recall, fasteners improperly tightened on the same Low Floor bus, was caught and fixed the same way.[6] But that history cuts both ways, because a manufacturer that once shipped loose steering fasteners has now shipped a loose fuel hose, and the common thread is not the part, it is the torque wrench.

What this tally cannot prove matters as much as what it can. No campaign record publishes a crash, injury, or fire count for this defect, so zero is an absence of a published count, not a measured zero. The 3 percent figure is Cummins’s estimate, not a measured rate, and no per-manufacturer defect share is published. Gillig’s owner-letter date was not stated in the NHTSA record summaries consulted, so this article states none. And the unfiled-buyer claim is dated: it holds as of the November 3 roundups, and a filing could land tomorrow.

If you manage a transit fleet, run every 2021–2026 Gillig Low Floor VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now, because Cummins does the hose replacement free and the buses carry your riders. If you run refuse trucks, do the same for 2022–2027 Autocar ACX Xpeditors, whose owner letters go out December 17.[2] If you ride the bus, you cannot check a VIN from the sidewalk, but you can smell diesel, and a bus that stalls and restarts is telling you something: report it to the transit agency, not just the driver. And if your vehicle carries a Cummins L9, check the engine, not just the badge, because the hose does not care whose name is on the grille.
