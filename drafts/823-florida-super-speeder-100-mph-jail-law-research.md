## News peg
- Florida HB 351 created s. 316.1922, F.S. ("dangerous excessive speeding"), effective **July 1, 2025** — ~14 months ago.
- A person commits the offense by (a) exceeding the speed limit by **50 mph or more**, or (b) driving **100 mph or more** in a manner threatening persons/property or interfering with other vehicles.
- Penalties: first conviction — up to **30 days jail**, **$500 fine**, or both; second/subsequent — up to **90 days**, **$1,000**, or both; repeat within 5 years — license revoked 180 days to 1 year. Drivers cited for 50+ over must appear in court; can't just pay online.
- First month: **26 motorists** apprehended (Herald-Tribune).
- April 2026 arrests: woman doing **123 mph** in Bonita Springs ("thought it was OK because she was driving a Supra"); motorcyclist doing **116 in a 40-mph zone** in Sarasota (radar photo published); woman arrested at **111 mph** across the Bayside Bridge, Pinellas County (FHP video); Ocoee street-racing pair at 100+ mph.

## The national backdrop (NHTSA, primary)
- 2024: **11,288** people killed in speeding-related crashes — **29%** of all traffic fatalities; 316,757 injured (NHTSA, "Speeding Catches Up With You" campaign release).
- 2025 early estimates: speeding-related fatalities projected down **11%** to ~**10,035** — back to pre-pandemic 2019 levels (same NHTSA release).
- Q1 2026: fatality rate 0.99/100M VMT, second-lowest Q1 on record (NHTSA early estimates).

## Novel contribution #1 — the kinetic energy math at the law's thresholds
KE = ½mv², so energy scales with the SQUARE of speed. For the same car:
- 100 mph vs 70 mph (typical FL interstate limit): (100/70)² = **2.04x the kinetic energy**. Twice the killing energy for 30 mph of thrill.
- 100 mph vs 55 mph: (100/55)² = **3.31x**.
- 116 mph in a 40 zone (Sarasota motorcyclist): (116/40)² = **8.41x** the energy of a law-abiding driver on the same road.
- 123 mph Supra: (123/70)² = **3.09x** vs. the flow of traffic.
Stopping distance (same tires, same road, d = v²/2μg) scales identically: the 100-mph driver needs **twice the pavement** to stop as the 70-mph driver — and at 100 mph you're covering **147 feet per second**, so 1.5 seconds of reaction time eats **220 feet** before the brakes even engage.

## Novel contribution #2 — the criminalization-as-confession frame
Before July 2025, doing 100 mph in Florida was a **civil traffic infraction**: fine, points, drive home. The legislature's own bill summary shows the vote (Senate 37-0, House 75-38) — near-unanimous agreement that the existing framework was a joke at triple digits. The new law's structure is a confession: the state is not deterring speeders with money; it is now deterring them with **jail cells and license revocation**. The threshold design is also a tell — 50-over and 100 mph are not "speeding" thresholds, they are "you have left the social contract" thresholds.

## Novel contribution #3 — the Virginia counterfactual (deterrence skepticism, feeds counterargument)
Virginia has criminalized 85+ mph as Class 1 misdemeanor reckless driving (up to 12 months jail) for decades — far harsher on paper than Florida's new law. If severity deterred, Virginia's triple-digit problem would be solved. It is not. The research consensus (deterrence literature) is that **certainty of enforcement beats severity of punishment**. Florida's real innovation may not be the jail time but the **mandatory court appearance** — the end of pay-online-and-forget.

## Actionable insights (required)
1. **Driving in Florida:** triple digits or 50-over is now a criminal arrest, not a ticket — mandatory court date, possible jail, possible license loss. Set cruise control; the margin for "flow of traffic" excuses is gone.
2. **The physics doesn't care about the statute:** at 100 mph you need twice the stopping distance of the cars around you at 70. If someone blows past you at triple digits, give them the whole lane — their crash is already written; don't co-author it.
3. **What actually works:** automated speed enforcement and road design beat jail threats in the literature. Support your city's speed-camera and traffic-calming programs — they're the unsexy version of this law that catches the 15-over drivers who kill far more people than the 100-mph outliers.

## Limitations
- Only ~14 months of enforcement data; no published before/after study from FLHSMV on whether the law changed behavior or deaths. Arrest anecdotes are vivid, not statistical.
- The KE/stopping-distance math is illustrative physics for an identical vehicle — real outcomes vary with mass, tires, road surface, and what gets hit.
- NHTSA's 2025 speeding numbers are early estimates, subject to revision; "speeding-related" in FARS includes cases where speed was a factor, not necessarily the cause.
- Georgia's 2010 "Super Speeder" law (the name's origin — a $200 state fee add-on) is the naming precedent; not analyzed in depth here.

## Strongest counterargument
Criminalization may be theater. The people doing 116 in a 40 are not doing cost-benefit analysis — they are, almost by definition, undeterrable by statute. Virginia's decades of criminal reckless-driving law didn't end triple-digit driving. And the thresholds are so extreme they miss the actual epidemic: the everyday 12-over driver, who kills orders of magnitude more people than the Supra at 123. Meanwhile speeding deaths fell 11% nationally in 2025 WITHOUT any new criminal law — enforcement campaigns and safer roads were already bending the curve. Florida may have built a gallows for a problem that was already shrinking, while the real killers (moderate speeding, everywhere, daily) remain a civil infraction you can pay online.
