Same Death Rate, Two Different Autopsies

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 10, 2026

Every fatality rate you have ever read on this site is a compound fracture of two separate injuries. I split them apart, all 337 models, and the correlation between the two halves is 0.29. Practically strangers. Your car's death rate is the product of how often it gets into fatal crashes and how often somebody inside it dies when it does, and those two numbers barely know each other.

0.29
Correlation between crash-involvement rate and per-crash lethality across 337 models

Exhibit A: Ford's E-350 work van and the Buick Century sedan post death rates of 2.51 and 2.41 per 100 million miles, identical for actuarial purposes. Underneath, they are photographic negatives. It racks up 6.1 fatal-crash involvements per 100 million miles, triple the national average, because it is a 9,000-pound box driven all day in traffic by tired men on deadlines. But when it crashes, only 41 of every 100 involvements produce an occupant death. The Century's? 3.1 involvements per 100 million miles. Respectable. Almost safe. Except 77 of every 100 end with somebody inside dead, which means one vehicle dies by volume and the other dies by precision.

Across the whole ledger the pattern repeats, starting with the Chevrolet Tahoe (2.49) and the Chevrolet Cavalier (2.43): the Tahoe gets into 4.9 involvements per 100 million miles and kills 51 per 100 of them; the Cavalier gets into 2.8 and kills 86. Then the Camry (2.03) against the Chevrolet HHR (2.12): 3.4 involvements and 59 dead per hundred versus 2.7 and 80. And the Corvette (1.52) against the Suburban (1.36): the Vette crashes rarely at 1.7 per 100 million miles but buries 88 occupants per 100 involvements, the highest lethality of any vehicle in the database, while the Suburban crashes 50 percent more often and kills barely half as many per crash.[1]

The industry treats the rate as one number and prescribes one medicine, but it is two diseases. In March, IIHS announced it will finally start rating commercial vehicles, beginning with cargo vans and heavy pickups, on crash avoidance and the presence of modern belts and airbags rather than structure alone.[5] Six thousand five hundred thirty-five people died in crashes involving work vehicles in 2023, sixteen percent of all road deaths, and most of the dead were in the other vehicle. For a high-involvement machine like the E-350, the van's own data says avoidance is the lever: AEB, blind-spot monitoring, and better belts will move the rate, and another airbag will not. For the high-lethality crowd, the Century, the Cavalier, the HHR, the prescription is harsher. No driver aid rescues a structure that kills 77 percent of the time once the crash starts. Honest recommendation, four words: buy a newer car.

One caution before the dealerships call their lawyers. That low lethality number is partly a bookkeeping trick of mass, not an engineering triumph. A 9,000-pound van in a two-car fatal crash often kills the other driver, which registers as an involvement with no occupant death.[4] The old sedans skew toward single-vehicle tree-and-pole impacts where there is nobody else available to die instead. Crash-type mix and mass confound the comparison, and the VMT estimates behind every number here carry roughly 15 percent uncertainty for low-volume models. Still. A shopper who only sees the headline rate, 2.51 versus 2.41, has no idea which of the two crimes they are buying, and now you do.
