# Research: #1084 — "The Two Safest States for Drunk Driving Make Every Convicted Drunk Driver Blow Into Their Car"

## News peg
Sept 26, 2026: Asbury Park Press (app.com) reported a new study by Steve Gimblin Personal Injury & Car Accident Lawyers analyzing NHTSA FARS data (2020–2024, released April 2026) ranking all 50 states by drivers killed with BAC ≥ 0.08 per 100,000 licensed drivers. New Jersey ranked #1 (lowest) at 1.33; Montana ranked worst at 7.17 — a 5.4x gap. Top 10 lowest: NJ, NY, MA, Alaska, Utah, MN, RI, HI, WA, PA.

## Primary sources (8)
1. **Asbury Park Press / app.com, Sept 26, 2026** — the ranking, methodology, NJ numbers (440 deaths over 5 years, avg 88/yr, 6,626,757 licensed drivers; 2022 deadliest year at 100, 2023 fewest at 79; Mid-Atlantic sub-rankings). https://www.app.com/story/news/local/new-jersey/2026/09/26/drink-driving-accident-death-rate-new-jersey/91764827007/
2. **NHTSA FARS, 2020–2024** — underlying dataset (FARS Query System, cdan.dot.gov). https://www.nhtsa.gov/research-data/fatality-analysis-reporting-system-fars
3. **NJ Legislature, Chapter 201 (Ricci's Law)** — pub.njleg.gov: all persons convicted of first, second or subsequent drunk-driving offense must install an ignition interlock; took effect Dec 1, 2019. https://pub.njleg.gov/bills/2008/PL09/201_.PDF ; statute text at https://law.justia.com/codes/new-jersey/2020/title-39/section-39-4-50-17/
4. **NY Leandra's Law (VTL §§1193/1198)** — signed Nov 18, 2009, effective Aug 15, 2010: mandatory interlock for every DWI conviction including first offenses. https://www.legalsurvival.com/interlock-systems-required-for-ny-drunk-drivers/
5. **Massachusetts Melanie's Law (mass.gov IID program doc)** — mandatory interlock for 2+ OUI convictions with lifetime lookback (minimum 2-year restriction); since July 1, 2021, certain first offenders on hardship licenses too. https://www.mass.gov/doc/iid-program-document-0/download
6. **CDC Community Guide, systematic review of 15 studies (AJPM, 2011)** — ignition interlocks reduce re-arrest rates by a median of 67% vs. suspended-license comparison groups while installed. https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/media/releases/2011/p0222_ignitioninterlocks.html
7. **Kaufman & Wiebe (2016), via responsibility.org** — all-offender interlock laws associated with a 15% reduction in alcohol-involved crash deaths (≈915 lives saved). https://www.responsibility.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Ignition-Interlock-Devices-for-all-DUI-Offenders.pdf
8. **Utah Highway Safety Office** — Utah first state to lower per se BAC to 0.05 (HB 155, effective Dec 30, 2018). https://highwaysafety.utah.gov/05-bac-law/ ; counterpoint: CrimRxiv 2026 (Olson & Adams) synthetic-control study found null effects of the 0.05 change alone on alcohol-involved fatal outcomes. https://www.crimrxiv.com/pub/gvzy47kr/release/1

## The novel thread (original contribution)
Nobody cross-tabulated the fresh state ranking against state interlock regimes. The result:
- **#1 NJ and #2 NY are both mandatory-interlock-for-every-convicted-offender states.** NJ's Ricci's Law (Dec 2019) means the 2020–2024 study window is essentially the first full observation period of all-offender interlock in the nation's #1-ranked state. NY's Leandra's Law has run since 2010.
- **#3 MA runs the strictest repeat-offender interlock regime in the country** — lifetime lookback (every prior OUI counts, no matter how old) since 2005.
- The hardware-vs-statute contrast: the mechanical fix (interlock, 67% re-arrest reduction, 15% fatal-crash-death reduction) has a stronger evidence base than the statutory fix (Utah's 0.05 BAC law, where a 2026 synthetic-control study found null effects on fatal outcomes while a CSU study found declines — genuinely contested).
- Montana worst-status convergence: independent analyses across decades agree — NHTSA 2004 (Montana highest, 0.98), NHTSA 2006–07 (Montana highest, 0.94), Forbes Advisor (8.57 drunk drivers in fatal crashes per 100k licensed drivers), Simmrin Law (31.42% of fatal-crash drivers drunk, highest in nation). The Gimblin study is the latest confirmation, not an outlier.

## Arithmetic (original, all verified)
- NJ: 440 deaths / 6,626,757 licensed drivers × 100,000 / 5 years = 1.328 → **1.33** ✓
- Montana/NJ ratio: 7.17 / 1.33 = 5.39 → **5.4x** ✓
- NJ 2022 vs 2023: 100 vs 79 deaths = 21% year-over-year drop within the window
- CDC effect size context: a 15% all-offender-law reduction applied to the ~12,429 annual alcohol-impaired deaths implies the scale of the prize

## Limitations
- The study metric counts **drivers killed** with BAC ≥ 0.08 — the drunk driver's own death, not victims. It understates total alcohol carnage: NHTSA's national alcohol-impaired figure counts all deaths in crashes involving an impaired driver (12,429 in 2023). A state where drunk drivers survive but kill passengers would look artificially good.
- Correlation is not causation: NJ, NY, MA are also among the densest states in the country (NJ is #1 in density) with the most transit and the shortest average trips. Density alone could explain most of the ranking. The interlock correlation rides on top of urbanization.
- Could not verify NJ's rank before Ricci's Law took effect (Dec 2019); NJ was very likely already near the top on density alone. The study window (2020–2024) cannot isolate the law's contribution.
- The Gimblin study is a law-firm marketing analysis, not peer-reviewed. Its methodology depends on FARS BAC reporting, which varies by state toxicology-testing rates.
- FARS 2024 is the newest available data — roughly a two-year reporting lag.
- Utah's 0.05 evidence is genuinely contested (CSU study positive, CrimRxiv 2026 null); do not present the limit change as proven.

## Strongest counterargument
Density explains the whole leaderboard without any policy story. The four densest states (NJ, RI, MA, CT) all sit at the safe end of any drunk-driving table; Montana (48th in density) sits at the other end. Drunk-driving death tracks rural high-speed miles, long bar-to-home distances, and zero transit — none of which a statute fixes. Worse for the policy story: the most rigorous evaluation of the boldest BAC law in America (Utah's 0.05, synthetic-control, 2026) found no detectable reduction in alcohol-involved fatal outcomes — deterrence statutes don't reliably move the needle, and interlocks only work while installed (CDC: re-arrest rates revert after removal). Montana's problem may be culture and enforcement, not the absence of a device mandate. The honest read: interlocks are the best-supported tool we have, but the ranking mostly measures how far your bar is from your house.

## Actionable takeaway
1. Check whether your state mandates interlocks for first-time offenders — fewer than half do (NCSL via KU study). If yours doesn't, that's the specific policy gap to push your legislators on; the evidence base (CDC 67%, Kaufman & Wiebe 15%) is unusually strong for traffic safety.
2. If you live in a Montana-class state (rural, long drives, no transit): the rational personal response is a keychain breathalyzer and a rideshare budget, because the infrastructure that protects NJ drivers doesn't exist where you are.
3. Buying a used car? Ignition interlocks are aftermarket-installable (~$75–100 install, ~$70–100/month) — a voluntary install is cheaper than a DUI and, per the data, mechanically prevents the exact crash this ranking measures.
4. Check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls regardless — free, and unrelated to this ranking.
