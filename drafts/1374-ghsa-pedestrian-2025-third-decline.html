By The Numbers

6,732 People Walking Died in 2025. That Is Progress With a Ceiling

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 3, 2026

I ran the numbers twice. Both runs agreed, which is how you know the good news is real and the bad news is structural. Governors Highway Safety Association projections put pedestrian deaths at 6,732 for 2025, down 7% from 2024.[1] That is 505 fewer people killed while walking, the largest single-year reduction since the decline began, and the third straight annual decrease. Measured per 100,000 population, the rate fell below 2.0 for the first time since before the pandemic.

Projected pedestrians killed in 2025, down 7% from 2024, but still 5% above the 2019 pre-pandemic level

Now run the same decline three ways, because each cut tells a different story. By count, it is a win. By rate, it is a win. By composition, it stalls. SUVs and pickups account for more than half of all pedestrian fatalities, a share that has not budged with the totals.[1] More than 1,100 people, 15% of pedestrian deaths, died on freeways and interstates, roads that were never designed for anyone on foot. Pedestrians crossing expressways produced only 4,170 crashes over five years but the highest fatality rate in the data at 56.7%. Fewer crashes, far deadlier ones.

Two more shares moved the wrong way while the total fell. Drivers who fled the scene killed 1,669 pedestrians in 2024, down 4% in raw count from 2023, yet hit-and-runs still made up 23.8% of all pedestrian fatalities.[1] Distracted drivers were involved in 21.7% of 2024 pedestrian deaths, up from 15.6% in 2019. Put plainly: fewer people are dying, and a growing share of the deaths that remain involve a driver who was not paying attention or did not stay.

National context backs up the direction, with a small honest discrepancy worth noting. NHTSA's early estimate puts all 2025 traffic deaths at 36,640, down 6.7% from 39,254 in 2024, with a fatality rate of 1.10 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, the second lowest on record.[2] NHTSA's July update pegged the pedestrian drop at about 8%, versus GHSA's 7% projection. Different methods, different vintages, same direction. Every one of NHTSA's ten regions is projected to decline, and the fourth quarter of 2025 would be the 15th consecutive quarterly decrease since mid-2022.

Credit belongs where the data puts it. State highway safety offices point to enforcement, data analysis, technology, education, and infrastructure changes, and three straight years of declines across nearly every category is not a rounding error.[1] Nor is it a solved problem. A total still 5% above 2019, with a vehicle mix dominated by tall front ends and a distracted share still climbing, reads less like a victory lap and more like a trend approaching its ceiling. Something has to change about the mix, not just the total, for the next leg down.

What to do, concretely. If you are shopping for a family vehicle, weight the front-end height and pedestrian crash prevention ratings alongside the occupant scores, because the vehicle you choose decides how survivable a mistake is for someone outside it. If you walk, treat freeway-adjacent routes and expressway crossings as the highest-risk segments on any map, not as shortcuts. If you drive, speed and distraction are the two factors in this report that are fully under your control, and both show up in the shares that are getting worse. Check your state's numbers in the GHSA tables before assuming your state follows the national trend, since state-level results vary widely and one state had a reporting issue in the preliminary data.
