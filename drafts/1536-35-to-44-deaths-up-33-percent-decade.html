The Fastest-Growing Death Toll on American Roads Belongs to 35-to-44-Year-Olds

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 19, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they did not get better. Between 2015 and 2024, American traffic deaths rose from 35,484 to 39,254, a gain of 3,770 funerals in a decade when cars got safer and driver-assist tech went standard, even as the fatality rate fell for a third straight year, to 1.19 per 100 million miles. Somebody has to account for those 3,770 deaths. Turns out almost all of them belong to two age groups, and one of them is a group nobody writes about.[1][3]

88.2%
Share of the entire national increase in traffic deaths between 2015 and 2024 that came from just two age groups: 35-to-44-year-olds and everyone 65 and older. Every other age group combined added 443 deaths, net.

Start with the group the headlines never name. In 2015, 4,707 people aged 35 to 44 died in traffic crashes. In 2024, the count was 6,252. That is 1,545 additional deaths, a 32.8 percent increase, and the largest absolute increase of any age group in the table, with no other band coming close. People 65 to 74 added 988, and people 75 and older added 794. People 25 to 34, still the single largest group at 6,921 deaths, added only 577, a 9.1 percent rise that trails the national growth rate of 10.6 percent. Read that again, because the group with the biggest body count is treading water while the group with the momentum is a decade older and almost entirely absent from the road-safety conversation.[3]

Look at the bracket around that group instead. On one side of 35-to-44 sits the 21-to-24 group, down 318 deaths, a 9.2 percent decline. On the other side sits the 45-to-54 group, down 341 deaths, a 6.4 percent decline. Children under 16 fell 8.2 percent. Teenagers 16 to 20 rose only 4.1 percent. So the two cohorts immediately flanking 35-to-44 both got safer in absolute terms, and the youngest drivers in the dataset improved, too. Whatever is happening to 35-to-44-year-olds is not a tide lifting every working-age boat. It is specific to that band, and it is moving in the opposite direction from its neighbors.[3]

Half the story is seniors, and the senior half needs a correction before anyone panics. People 65 and older accounted for 8,019 deaths in 2024, one in five of every traffic death in the country, up from 17.6 percent of the total in 2015. Their count rose 28.6 percent over the decade. But their licensed-driver population grew faster, up 36 percent, from a base that now makes older drivers 23 percent of everyone licensed to drive, against 18 percent in 2015. And when NHTSA divides fatal-crash involvement by licensed drivers, the 65-and-older group posts 15.60 involvements per 100,000 licensed drivers, the lowest rate of any age group, which means per driver, they got safer. That distinction matters, because it is exactly the distinction the data cannot yet make for 35-to-44-year-olds.[2][6]

One year of data points the same way. In 2024 alone, fatalities in crashes involving young drivers aged 15 to 20 fell 370, a 6.6 percent drop, while fatalities in crashes involving drivers 65 and older rose 115, a 1.4 percent increase. Alcohol-impaired-driving fatalities fell 478 to 11,904. Speeding-related fatalities fell 625, and unrestrained occupant fatalities fell 775. Nearly every behavioral category NHTSA tracks improved in 2024, which makes the decade-long drift toward 35-to-44 harder to explain away as a bad year, because a bad year does not last ten years.[1]

Fairness demands the strongest case against this article, and it is a good one. These are counts, not rates: NHTSA publishes no exposure denominator for 35-to-44-year-olds in the sources used here, no licensed-driver count, no miles driven, no population figure for the band, so nobody can say from this table whether a 35-year-old's individual risk rose 32.8 percent or whether the cohort simply got bigger. It did get bigger: the 35-to-44 band in 2024 is mostly millennials, born 1980 to 1989, a large generation, while the same band in 2015 was mostly Generation X, born 1971 to 1980, a smaller one. Cohort size plausibly explains part of the increase. Add that the age table counts the age of the person who died, driver, passenger, pedestrian, or cyclist, while NHTSA's young-driver and older-driver figures count fatalities in crashes involving drivers of those ages, a different measure entirely. And 2024 comes from the FARS Annual Report File, subject to small revision when the Final File lands. A careful reader could look at all of that and conclude this is mostly demographics wearing a safety costume.[3]

That careful reader would be half right, and the half matters. Demographics explain the senior count, where the exposure numbers exist and the per-driver rate fell. For 35-to-44, the exposure numbers do not exist in the public sources, so the demographic explanation is a hypothesis waiting on data, not a settled answer. What is settled is the composition of the national death toll: it shifted toward the middle of working life and toward old age, and away from the young. Teen-driver programs, graduated licensing, and a decade of lectures aimed at twenty-somethings coincide with a 9.2 percent decline in the 21-to-24 group. Nothing equivalent has ever been aimed at 35-to-44-year-olds, because nobody thought they were the problem. They are now the fastest-growing part of it.

What to do: if you are 35 to 44, the risks are the unglamorous ones, and the 2024 numbers say the fixes work. Alcohol-impaired-driving deaths fell 3.9 percent in a single year. Speeding deaths fell 5.2 percent, and unrestrained occupant deaths fell 7.4 percent. Buckle up, slow down, and do not drive impaired; those three choices cover the categories that moved the national total down in 2024. If you buy or borrow a car, check the model-year fatality data before you check the cupholders. If you set road-safety policy, the decade table is a rebuke to a strategy that aims every campaign at teenagers and seniors while the growth sits in the middle: fund a 35-to-44 program with the same seriousness as the teen-driver programs that coincided with the 21-to-24 decline, and demand that NHTSA publish licensed-driver and mileage exposure by age band so the next decade table can say whether the risk rose or only the headcount did.
