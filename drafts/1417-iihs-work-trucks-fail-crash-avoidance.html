IIHS Tested 11 Work Trucks and Vans. Seven of Them Couldn't Stop for a Car in Front of Them.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

On October 8, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety published the first crash-avoidance ratings ever given to commercial vehicles, and the results read like a confession.[1] Eleven cargo vans and Class 3 pickups went through the same vehicle-to-vehicle and pedestrian front-crash-prevention tests as passenger cars. Seven of the eleven earned a Poor rating for vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention, and not one pickup earned a Good in either test.[4]

9 of 22
ratings came back Poor across the two tests: 7 of 11 for vehicle-to-vehicle, 2 of 11 for pedestrian. Only 3 of 22 were Good.

Start with the scoreboard. Across all 22 ratings, the tally is 3 Good, 4 Acceptable, 6 Marginal, and 9 Poor. Only the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned a Good for vehicle-to-vehicle prevention, and it was the only vehicle with no Poor or Marginal anywhere on its report card. At the other end sit the Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500, Poor in both tests because neither one offers automatic emergency braking at all, just an optional forward collision warning, on a platform introduced in 1996 and still sold new in 2026.[1][4]

Motorcycles broke the systems hardest: several trucks did not warn or slow at all in most of the motorcycle trials, the single biggest failure in the whole program. Then came the child dummy. In daylight, several vehicles, including the Acceptable-rated Ford F-350 and Ram 3500, struggled more with the child than with the adult at night, because the sensors sit near the rearview mirror and the front ends are so tall that the child dummy was detected at a distance and then, in IIHS's own word, disappeared as the truck closed in. As for the pickups, the F-350 could avoid the passenger-car target only at 31 mph, and in one 43-mph test it failed to reduce speed at all, while the Silverado 3500HD failed to avoid the car in all six vehicle-to-vehicle tests.[1][4]

Behind the ratings sits a regulatory hole big enough to drive a box truck through. In May 2024, NHTSA finalized FMVSS No. 127, requiring automatic emergency braking on new light vehicles by September 1, 2029, and vehicles over 10,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating were excluded entirely.[3] A joint NHTSA and FMCSA proposal from July 2023 would have covered them, estimating 19,118 crashes prevented, 155 lives saved, and 8,814 injuries reduced every year. It was never finalized, the incoming administration sent it back for a second look, and the 2026 agenda lists a supplemental proposal instead, restarting public comment and pushing a final rule to 2027 or 2028 at the earliest.[2] Until then, IIHS ratings are the only public scorecard for the vehicles involved in 6,215 deaths in 2024, 16 percent of every person killed on American roads.[1]

Fairness demands the other side of the ledger. Track tests are controlled scenarios, not real-world crash outcomes, and a Poor rating means a system failed IIHS's protocol, not that the vehicle is proven dangerous on public roads. Commercial vehicles are mostly driven by professionals who brake earlier than a test assumes, and fleet buyers weigh payload, uptime, and cost against a safety rating. FARS carries no per-model fatality data for these vans and trucks, so this article cannot cross-check the ratings against real death rates, and the 6,215 figure counts crashes involving heavy vehicles, not crashes caused by them. All of that is true, and none of it changes the fact that the heaviest vehicles on the road are the ones least able to stop themselves, while the only federal rule that would have forced the issue is three years old and still a draft.

Three things are worth doing with this news. Fleet buyers: the Sprinter 2500 is the only tested work vehicle with no Poor or Marginal rating, and the BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 are the only ones with a Good pedestrian rating, while anyone still buying Express or Savana vans should know those trucks have no AEB at all. Motorcyclists and pedestrians: the vehicles most likely to hit you are the ones least able to stop themselves, so treat a delivery van's brake lights as a rumor, not a promise. Everyone else: ask your representatives why the 10,000-pound line still exists, because a sedan gets a federal braking mandate in 2029 and the 14,000-pound pickup behind it gets nothing.
