Recalls Aren't Rising. They're Detonating.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 17, 2026

I ran the numbers, then ran them again, and they didn't get better. Sedgwick's H1 2026 Product Safety and Recall Index puts U.S. recall volume at 941.2 million units for the first half of the year, a 345.5% jump over H1 2025, with the annual pace on track to exceed one billion recalled units for only the fourth time in fifteen years.[1] Here is the part that made me run them a third time: the number of recall events barely moved. Regulators recorded 1,634 recalls in H1 2026 against 1,636 in H1 2025, the same number of announcements with four and a half times the stuff.

941.2 million
U.S. units recalled in H1 2026 alone, already more than all of 2025

Now the automotive slice, because that is what this site is about. In Q1 2026 automakers recalled roughly 12.2 million units, the highest quarterly total in two years.[2] Context matters. Full-year 2025 saw defective automotive units fall to an eleven-year low, but that trough was not a trend.[4] The industry went from its quietest recall year in over a decade to its loudest quarter in two years within about six months. It was a pause, and the pause is over.

Across the Atlantic the pattern shows up in events instead of units. EU and UK automotive recalls rose 13.2% in H1 2026 to 445 events, the highest first-half total in more than twelve years, against a twelve-year average of 267.[3] Injuries were the predominant trigger, accounting for 331 events. Fire risk set a record at 82 events, up from 75 a year earlier. Different measurement, same signal.

Record fire-risk automotive recall events in EU/UK H1 2026

Defects are not getting more frequent; they are getting bigger. The mechanism is boring and well understood: shared platforms, shared electronics, shared software. One bad ECU revision or one bad batch of a common component now blankets multiple models and multiple model years in a single filing. Sedgwick's own analysts put it plainly: a single defect can increasingly affect multiple models at once. Recall counts are a count of decisions and unit volume is a count of consequences, and while the decisions are holding steady, the consequences are compounding.

Run the two series side by side and the shape is unmistakable. Events: flat at roughly 1,635 per half, while units ran from 211.2 million to 941.2 million year over year. If you fit a line to the events it is horizontal. If you fit a line to the units it points at the ceiling. Fifteen years of Sedgwick data contain only four billion-unit years, and 2026 is one of them with three months still on the clock.

The strongest counterargument

A billion recalled units does not mean a billion dangerous cars. Pharmaceuticals drove the surge: 471.6 million units, up 914% year over year. Medical devices added 313.1 million, up 516.8%.[1] One recalled pill bottle counts the same as one recalled pickup truck, and a handful of mega-recalls can move the headline number without meaning anything about your driveway. Aggressive recalling is arguably the system working: companies finding defects and pulling product before injuries accumulate. And one loud quarter does not make a trend. The 2025 automotive trough could be noise, and the Q1 2026 spike could be noise in the other direction. Regression to the mean wears a lot of disguises.

Limitations

Sedgwick's public releases do not include an automotive-specific U.S. unit figure for H1 2026. The automotive numbers above are Q1 2026 (~12.2 million units, two-year quarterly high) and full-year 2025 (eleven-year low); the H1 unit surge is cross-industry and dominated by pharma and devices. Cross-industry unit totals are apples-to-oranges: a bottle of pills, a pacemaker, and a crossover do not represent equivalent risk. The EU figures count recall events (regulatory alerts), the U.S. figures mix events and units, so the two regions' headline numbers are not directly comparable. The eleven-year-low claim comes from Sedgwick's February State of the Nation report on 2025, using Sedgwick's own methodology, which is not independently verifiable from the press release.

What to do

Bigger recalls, not more recalls, has a practical consequence owners can feel: when your model is recalled, it is increasingly a mega-population recall, and those historically strain dealer parts supply and stretch repair wait times, so do not assume a same-week fix. Register your VIN for email alerts at nhtsa.gov/recalls. With event counts flat but volumes surging, recalls are less likely to surface in your news feed and more likely to arrive as a letter you ignore, so the database is the reliable channel. And treat the EU fire-risk record as a used-car signal: injury and fire-cause recalls in the EU frequently precede or parallel U.S. campaigns on shared global platforms, so a fire-related campaign on your model's European twin deserves a VIN check even before NHTSA posts one.
