Existential Dread

We Put Cameras on the Back of Every Car. The Deaths Moved to the Front.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 22, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Between 2008 and 2020, the number of children killed by vehicles moving forward at low speed in driveways and parking lots more than doubled, from 251 to 526 a year. During that same stretch, the federal government spent a decade mandating that every new car in America get a camera. It pointed the camera backward.

526
children killed in frontover incidents in 2020, per DOT data, up from 251 in 2008

The rearview camera rule is a triumph of regulatory patience. Congress passed the Cameron Gulbransen Kids Transportation Safety Act in 2007, named for a two-year-old killed when his father backed over him in the family driveway. NHTSA finalized the rule, delayed it four times, and full compliance arrived in May 2018: eleven years from statute to every dashboard showing you what is behind you, which is a genuinely good thing.

Meanwhile, out front, a different geometry problem was growing unchecked. Consumer Reports measured the front blind zones of 15 popular models and found that full-size pickups hide a zone 11 feet longer than a sedan's, and 7 feet longer than many popular SUVs. Some large vehicles have dead zones extending 16 feet past the grille. NBC News once lined up children single file in front of a truck and needed eleven of them before the last one's head cleared the hood. Eleven.

This is not an accident of physics; it is an outcome of commerce. Pickup hood heights rose 11 percent between 2000 and 2018, and average curb weight climbed 24 percent over the same span. Trucks and SUVs now account for roughly 80 percent of new vehicle sales. Over two decades, the American driveway fleet got taller, longer, and heavier, and the blind spot in front of it grew at exactly the rate the market demanded. Nobody passed a law requiring the front of your truck to be a fortress; the fortress just sold better.

Victim profiles are almost absurdly specific: according to the advocacy group Kids and Car Safety, which has tracked these deaths since the 1990s, the typical frontover victim is between 12 and 23 months old. Old enough to run, too young to understand a driveway. More than 60 percent of the time, the person behind the wheel is the child's own parent, often in the scenario the group calls "bye-bye syndrome": the toddler slips out of the house unnoticed and follows a parent to the car. People who love these children the most become, in an instant, the instrument of the thing. There is no cosmic justice in the data, just toddlers and torque.

Now here is the part that should make you put the coffee down. In December 2024, Senator Richard Blumenthal reintroduced the STOP Frontovers Act, which would order NHTSA to write a federal standard for front visibility within two years: cameras, sensors, driver alerts, automatic intervention. Front "trail cameras" are already sold on pickups so you can see rocks while off-roading, and every car built since 2018 already has a dashboard screen because the rear camera is mandatory, which settles the hardware argument; the screen is sitting right there.

But read the timeline like a historian, not a press release. Consider the rear camera fight: eleven years from law to lot, with four delays, and that was for technology everyone agreed worked. For now, the STOP Frontovers Act is still just a bill. If it passes and follows the historical cadence, mandatory front detection arrives around 2037. At 526 deaths a year, the waiting room holds roughly seven thousand more children before the rule bites. Even under the bill's own optimistic two-year schedule, the average American car is twelve and a half years old, which means your driveway stays blind through the entire 2030s no matter what Congress does this year. The law moves at the speed of law, and toddlers move faster.

Steel-man the cynics: frontovers are a supervision failure wearing a vehicle-design costume; mandating sensors taxes every new car for a problem a thirty-second walk-around solves, and the parents who most need the alert are precisely the ones rushing out the door. Steel-manning complete. The rebuttal is the history lesson: backup cameras are mandatory and backovers still happen, because a screen only helps the driver who looks at it, which is true, and it is also what everyone said about rear cameras in 2010, and the rear cameras got built anyway.

And the deeper absurdity: nobody can even agree on what to count. There is no official federal definition of a "frontover"; the Blumenthal bill has to order NHTSA to invent one. What passes for non-traffic fatality data is a patchwork, and the agency's public summaries lag years behind. We regulate what we measure, and we barely measure this. A death that happens in a driveway instead of on a highway lives in a statistical borderland, too slow for the crash testers and too common for the actuaries.

So the existential ledger reads like this: we spent eleven years and four delays putting a camera on the back of every car in America, which was the right thing to do, and while we did it the front of the fleet doubled as a killing zone for one-year-olds. The fix exists, the screen exists, the bill exists. Missing: the rule. And the rule is ten years away if history is any guide. Your truck can show you a rock on a trail in Moab. It cannot show you your child in the driveway. Sleep well.

What you can do: If you are shopping, ask specifically about front-view or surround-view cameras and front parking sensors tied to low-speed object detection, not trail cameras marketed for off-roading. They are usually optional equipment, rarely safety-marketed, and almost never standard on the trim you want. Until then, walk around the vehicle before every driveway departure, because the peak victim age is 12 to 23 months: old enough to follow you outside, invisible from the driver's seat.
