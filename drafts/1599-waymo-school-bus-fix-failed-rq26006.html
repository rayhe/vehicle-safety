Waymo Fixed the School Bus Problem. Then Its Cars Kept Passing School Buses.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

A fun fact for your morning commute: the car with no driver can see the school bus, stop for the school bus, ask a human whether that is a school bus, be told no, and then drive past the school bus anyway, while its stop arms are still out and children are still loading.[3] On November 6, NHTSA opened recall query RQ26006, covering 2,895 Waymo vehicles, to ask a question no regulator ever wants to ask about a recall: did the fix actually work?[1] Waymo had already recalled these cars once, in December 2025, for passing stopped school buses with red lights flashing and stop arms extended, and the company said the software remedy was complete on every affected vehicle by November 17, 2025.[2] NHTSA's closing document for the original investigation says the majority of the incidents happened after that date, and adds, in the driest sentence a federal agency ever wrote, that this calls into question the effectiveness of the recall remedy.[1] 94.4 percent of the recalled fleet is now back under investigation for the same behavior it was recalled for.

94.4%
Share of the 3,067 vehicles in recall 25E084 that are covered by the new recall query RQ26006: 2,895 of 3,067, nearly the entire recalled fleet.

Start with the timeline, because the timeline is the indictment. On September 22, 2025, in Atlanta, a Waymo approached a stopped school bus from a side street, stopped, then drove around the front of the bus, past its extended crossing control arm near students getting off, and down the road past the stop arm on the other side.[4] NHTSA opened preliminary evaluation PE25013 on October 17, 2025, noting that the fleet was driving about two million miles a week and that, given those miles, the likelihood of other prior similar incidents was high.[1] Waymo's own Part 573 report is unusually frank about what went wrong: the cars saw the buses, they stopped or started to stop, and then they sometimes proceeded before the bus turned off its lights or pulled in its arm, because the system decided it was impeding a priority vehicle and moved to get out of the way.[7] That behavior arrived with the August 20, 2025 driverless software release, which was broadly deployed in mid-September, and Waymo knew of 12 citations tied to it, issued between September 25 and November 14, roughly one every four days.[7]

Waymo pushed the remedy out as a hotfix between November 5 and November 17, 2025, and every affected vehicle had it by the 17th.[2] Twenty-three days later, on December 10, the company formally filed recall 25E084, covering 3,067 units of its fifth-generation automated driving system.[2] Read that sequence again, slowly, because it matters: the fix went out before the recall was filed, the recall was paperwork ratifying a software push that had already happened, and there were no dealer visits and no owner letters, because Waymo owns every car.[7] A hardware recall takes months and leaves a paper trail of appointments and loaners. A software recall can be declared complete on a Tuesday and filed three weeks later, and nobody outside the company can tell whether anything actually changed, which is exactly the problem NHTSA spent the next 354 days discovering.

Austin is where the fix fell apart in public. Austin's school district logged 19 violations in the 2025-26 school year, and its general counsel wrote NHTSA on November 20, 2025, that five of them happened after Waymo's November 5 letter assuring the district that the software updates were in place.[8] Five of nineteen is 26 percent, and those five are the ones that count, because they happened after the company said the problem was solved. District officials asked Waymo to suspend operations during school pickup and drop-off hours, and Waymo refused.[5] By January 15, 2026, the district's count had reached 24, including passes on December 11, 12, and 19, and again on January 12, which brings us to the incident the NTSB chose to examine in detail, because it is the one that shows the failure was not confined to the driving code the recall patched.[8]

January 12, 2026, 7:55 a.m., East Oltorf Street, Austin. An unoccupied 2024 Jaguar I-Pace, running Waymo's fifth-generation system, was the first vehicle to stop for an Austin ISD bus loading students, red lights strobing, two stop arms extended, speed limit 35.[3] It did everything right, and then it asked remote assistance a question, in plain language: is this a school bus with active signals? A remote assistance agent in Novi, Michigan, answered no. It resumed travel and passed the bus with its stop arms still extended, a passenger vehicle following it did the same, and six vehicles passed that bus in total, with no crash.[3] Passing a stopped school bus with active signals violates Texas Transportation Code section 545.066, and the NTSB's preliminary report, released March 3, 2026, says the investigation remains open, with a probable cause determination and safety recommendations still to come.[3] Sit with that for a second. The robot saw the bus, stopped, asked a human for help, and the human was wrong. Every layer of the system worked except the one that was supposed to catch the others failing, and that layer is a person in Michigan looking at a screen, a job description no recall of driving software will ever touch.

There is a second probe, and it is worse. NHTSA opened PE26001 on January 28, 2026, after a Waymo struck a child who ran out from behind a double-parked SUV two blocks from a Santa Monica elementary school during drop-off.[8] That investigation is separate from RQ26006, with its own facts, and it is not part of the recall query, but it is part of the same story, which is that the school-bus problem was never one bug in one software release. Waymo's position is that it has made significant improvements around school buses and has received no citations since March 2026, and the company says its vehicles experience twelve times fewer injury crashes involving pedestrians than human drivers.[5] Both claims may be true. Neither answers the question RQ26006 asks, which is not whether Waymo is safer than a human driver, but whether the fix Waymo filed in December 2025 fixed the thing it was filed to fix.

Now the counterargument, at full strength, because it deserves it: Waymo is being held to a standard no human driver meets. Human drivers illegally pass stopped school buses constantly, and the NTSB's own January 12 account shows five human drivers passing the same bus the Waymo passed, which means the robotaxi was the sixth-worst driver at that intersection, not the first. Waymo fixed the software before the paperwork was even filed, which is faster than any hardware recall in history, and its fleet-wide pedestrian injury record, by its own data, is far better than humans. All of that is true, and all of it misses what RQ26006 actually asks. NHTSA is not comparing Waymo to human drivers. It is comparing Waymo's fix to Waymo's fix. Waymo said the problem was solved on November 17, 2025. Most of the incidents happened after that date. A fix that does not fix the thing it was filed to fix is a failed fix, whatever the baseline, and the January 12 failure did not even come from the driving code the recall patched, which means the recall could have worked perfectly and the bus would still have been passed, because the failure lived in the human backup layer the recall never touched.

What this does not prove matters as much as what it does. A recall query is a review, not a finding of fault and not a new recall, and NHTSA has not ruled that the remedy definitively failed. NHTSA's closing document counts 27 incidents from outside sources, a media report from Atlanta and traffic citations from Austin ISD, while the number Waymo reported and the overall total are marked confidential, so the true incident count is not public. Waymo's claim of no citations since March 2026 is the company's own statement, not a confirmed federal finding. NTSB's January 12 account is preliminary and subject to change, and the Santa Monica child-strike incident is a separate investigation. Per the recall report, no collisions resulted from the recalled behavior, so the danger documented here is exposure, cars passing buses where children were loading, not a crash count. And the twelve-times-fewer figure is a company claim about its overall record, not a finding about school-bus behavior.

What to do with this starts at 7:55 a.m., because if you live in a Waymo operating city, the school-bus rule is the one that matters when your kid is standing in the road. A robotaxi that stops for a bus and then goes anyway is not a hypothetical, it happened in Atlanta, it happened at least 19 times in Austin, and the regulator is still asking why. If your kid rides a bus in Austin, know that the district's buses carry stop-arm cameras that photograph violators, and that footage is what forced the federal investigation in the first place. The cameras work; the question is whether the cars do. And the broader lesson is about recall remedies, not robots: a software recall can be complete on every vehicle and still not work, because deployed is not the same as verified, and when a company says a recall is done, the right question is the one NHTSA finally asked almost a year later, which is whether it actually took.
