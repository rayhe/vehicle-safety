By The Numbers

GM's Recall Fix Was Thicker Oil. 473 Engines Failed Anyway.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They did not get better. In April 2025, General Motors recalled 597,630 trucks and SUVs for defective crankshafts in its 6.2-liter L87 V8, and the prescribed fix for most owners was a different grade of motor oil. On August 20, 2026, NHTSA escalated the case to Engineering Analysis EA26005, the last stage before the agency can demand a new recall, with 499 complaints alleging the engine failed after the remedy was completed.[1][2]

94.8%
Share of NHTSA's 499 post-remedy engine-failure complaints that came from the oil-change arm (473 of 499), per the EA26005 opening resume.

Start with the defect, because it determines whether the fix was ever plausible. GM's federal filing blames a supplier quality issue: sediment in the connecting rods or crankshaft oil galleries, and crankshafts with improper dimensions or surface finish. A crankshaft that is dimensionally wrong does not get fixed by more viscous oil. Oil cushions; it does not re-machine.[3] To be fair to GM, thicker oil is a legitimate engineering response to bearing wear, since higher viscosity thickens the hydrodynamic film that keeps metal off metal, so the remedy was not irrational, though the post-remedy failure counts suggest it was insufficient.

NHTSA's resume splits the 499 post-remedy complaints by remedy arm: 473 from the oil-viscosity change, 26 from engines that received a complete engine replacement.[1] That is 94.8 percent versus 5.2 percent: the cheap fix is the failing fix, by an order of magnitude. But do not skip the second number. Twenty-six brand-new factory replacement engines failed anyway, which means swapping the entire engine did not eliminate the failure mode either.

Two more numbers widen the frame. First: GM itself reported 6,953 post-remedy failure complaints to the regulator, per Reuters, which divided by the 499 in the federal file gives 13.9. The NHTSA resume everyone cites is the tip of an iceberg the manufacturer counted itself.[3] Second: the resume cites 191 L87 failures in engines built after the recall's production window. GM estimated 3 percent of the recall population was defective; those 191 post-window failures say the boundary was wrong, which is why the investigation now covers 997,743 vehicles, 67 percent more than the original recall.[1][4]

Some humility before the panic. Complaint counts are self-selected, and 499 federal complaints against roughly 600,000 recalled vehicles is a small absolute rate, though these are high-mileage work trucks and some of these failures are ordinary wear wearing a recall costume. ODI's own records cite one crash and one injury, which is not nothing but is not a body count either.[6] And we cannot compute per-arm failure rates, because GM never published the denominator: how many vehicles got the oil versus the replacement. What the numbers do support is narrower and still damning. The agency opened a recall query in January, watched the complaints keep arriving, and escalated to an engineering analysis in August. Regulators do not do that for fixes that worked.

Drive a 2021 through 2026 Silverado 1500, Tahoe, Suburban, Sierra 1500, Yukon, or Escalade with the 6.2-liter L87? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for recall 25V-274, and watch for a new recall out of EA26005. If you got the oil change, use 0W-40 at every change, keep every receipt, and treat any new knock or tick as urgent, since 473 of the 499 post-remedy complaints came from your group. If you got a replacement engine, you are in better shape but not safe shape: document everything anyway. And the Silverado with the 5.3-liter V8 is not in this investigation. This is engine-specific, not model-specific, so check the engine, not the badge.[1][2]
