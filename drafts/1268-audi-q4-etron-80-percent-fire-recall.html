The Gap

Audi's Electric SUV Gets the 80% Haircut. The Fix Does Not Exist Yet.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
January 1, 2028

Before you sign anything on a used Audi Q4 e-tron, you might want to see this. Audi recalled 9,048 Q4 e-tron and Q4 Sportback e-tron SUVs, model years 2024 and 2025, because the high-voltage battery may fail and catch fire. Until a remedy is developed, owners are told to charge to a maximum of 80 percent and wait. That is not a repair. That is a fifth of your range, held hostage, with no end date attached.[1]

9,048
2024-2025 Audi Q4 e-tron and Q4 Sportback e-tron SUVs recalled under NHTSA campaign 26V698
80%
Maximum charge owners are advised to use until a remedy is developed

Audi filed the recall with federal regulators on October 21, 2026. Vehicle identification numbers became searchable on October 23. Interim owner letters are not expected until December 18, nearly two months after the filing, and those letters will contain no repair either. Audi's own number for the campaign is 93SK. If you own one of these cars, the calendar matters as much as the defect: you will spend the rest of 2026 driving a car whose official safety instruction is to use less of it.[2]

Pay attention to what Audi did not say. Regulators list no do-not-drive order and no park-outside order for this campaign. Contrast that with the Hyundai Genesis seat-motor fire recall the same month, which told owners to park outside, or the Kalmar yard-truck recall, which did the same. Audi's silence on parking could mean its engineers believe capping charge genuinely bounds this failure mode. It could also mean Audi is unwilling to tell premium buyers to leave their car on the street. Federal filings do not resolve that question, and buyers should not mistake a missing parking order for proof that charging at 80 percent indoors is safe. It is an instruction, not a guarantee.[1]

Context makes this recall bigger than 9,048 cars. Volkswagen Group has already used the 80 percent cap on the VW ID.4 battery recalls covered three times on this site. Same corporate parent, same electric platform family, same interim answer. When a stopgap migrates from the mass-market sibling to the premium badge, it stops looking like a one-model glitch and starts looking like a battery question the group has not answered. Audi buyers paid a premium precisely to avoid inheriting Volkswagen's problems. On this defect, they inherited the same haircut.[4]

Fairness requires the other side, stated plainly. No fire count, failure count, or defect rate appears in the published record for this campaign. Nine thousand cars is a small recall next to the 94,622 Hyundai Genesis vehicles recalled for seat-motor fires in the same month. A cautious reader could call this a careful, small-footprint action: flag the risk early, cap the charge, engineer a fix. That reading is reasonable. It is also incomplete, because a small recall with no fix still leaves every affected owner with the same daily cost: less range, indefinite waiting, and a resale value that now carries an asterisk until campaign 93SK shows a completed remedy on that specific VIN.[2]

Do not confuse this campaign with an earlier Q4 e-tron recall over an onboard charger that could strand drivers by draining the 12-volt battery. That was a different defect, a different campaign, and a different fix. Mixing them up helps no one, least of all a used-car shopper checking a VIN report. For campaign 93SK, the action list is short. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now instead of waiting for the December letter. Set the charge limit to 80 percent today and leave it there. If you are shopping used, treat any Q4 e-tron without a completed 93SK remedy as a car still wearing its haircut. Call Audi at 1-800-253-2834 with questions and cite recall 93SK.[3]
