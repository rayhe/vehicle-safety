# Research: #1420 — NHTSA Drowsy-Driving Survey (Nov 4, 2026)

## Angle (1 sentence)
NHTSA's first national drowsy-driving survey in 24 years finds 55% of drivers have fallen asleep at the wheel, a third of them within the first hour of driving, and the official death count (633) is a fiction the survey itself explains.

## News peg
- NHTSA released the National Survey of Drowsy Driving Knowledge, Attitudes, and Behaviors on **November 4, 2026** (announced on NHTSA's LinkedIn the same day).
- Traffic Tech summary: **DOT HS 813 895** (Blenner & Watson, Nov 2026). Full report: **DOT HS 813 887** (Dorazio et al., Nov 2026).
- Last similar NHTSA survey was fielded in **2002** (Royal, DOT HS 809 566) — a 24-year gap, and the 2020 Federal Register notice (85 FR, Docket NHTSA-2020-0024) explicitly says so.

## Verified survey findings (DOT HS 813 895)
- **55%** of respondents reported having ever fallen asleep while driving. Average **0.23 times** in the past 30 days (never-fallers included as zeros).
- Sample: **3,765 respondents**, nationally representative, address-based probability sample, administered **2022**. Oversampled African American, Hispanic, younger respondents, and residents of states with drowsy-driving laws/campaigns. Weighted to target population. (Note: the ROSA P "Related Documents" blurb describes the full report as sampling 10,700 U.S. adults — a different figure from the Traffic Tech's 3,765; the article uses the Traffic Tech's 3,765 and does not merge the two.)
- **Duration before most recent fall-asleep:** 30–59 min 17%, <30 min 16% → **33% within the first hour**. More than 6 hours: only 15%.
- **Road type:** **62%** were on multilane highways with speed limits above 55 mph.
- **Time of day:** midnight–5:59 a.m. 27% (largest), 6 p.m.–midnight 25%, 6 a.m.–11:59 a.m. 12%.
- **Sleep the night before:** 6–7 hours most common (24%); more than 8 hours least common (7%).
- **Law awareness:** over **80%** did not know whether their state had a law against drowsy driving.
- **Predictors (regressions):** lower likelihood/frequency where rideshare/taxis available, for people who drive as part of their job, who get more sleep, or who live in Arkansas or New Jersey (the only two states with drowsy-driving laws). Higher likelihood/frequency with more frequent night driving, difficulty sleeping, commutes over 30 minutes.
- **Messaging paradox:** respondents who recalled hearing drowsy-driving warnings in the past year had INCREASED likelihood and frequency of drowsy driving. Authors' offered explanation: campaigns are more salient/memorable to people who already do the behavior.
- **Theory of planned behavior:** stronger subjective norms (important people disapprove) and perceived behavioral control → lower future intent to drive drowsy.
- **Limitations (stated in report):** low response rate, potential non-response bias, social-desirability and recall biases. Generalizability affected.

## Original calculations (the contribution)
1. **132 million Americans have fallen asleep at the wheel.** 55% × 240M licensed drivers (FHWA 2024) = 132.0M. Inputs shown; rounding to nearest million.
2. **~660 million fall-asleep episodes per year.** 0.23 times per driver in the past 30 days × 12 months = 2.76 per driver-year; × 240M licensed drivers = 662.4M. Annualization assumes the 30-day rate holds year-round; flagged as a rough extrapolation, not a measured count.
3. **37% → 55% over 24 years.** +18 percentage points, +49% relative. Same question concept (ever nodded off/fallen asleep while driving), different survey modes (2002 telephone vs 2022 address-based); flagged as not strictly comparable, directionally damning.
4. **First hour vs sixth hour: 33% vs 15%.** More than twice as likely to fall asleep in the first hour of a drive as after six-plus hours. Kills the "long trip" framing outright.
5. **A 4-second microsleep at 65 mph covers 381 feet blind.** 65 mph = 95.3 ft/s × 4 s. Arithmetic illustration, labeled as such; the survey does not measure microsleep duration.
6. **Six hours is the modal sleep before falling asleep (24%) — and it is not enough.** Only 7% had slept more than 8 hours. The "I got six, I'm fine" cohort is the modal victim.
7. **The official count misses ~90%.** FARS 633 (2023) vs AAAFTS-modeled ~5,967/yr (2017–2021 avg) and GHSA 6,326 (2023). The survey explains why: the behavior is near-universal (55%) and leaves no biomarker, so police reports code it only when a survivor admits it.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
- Self-reported surveys overstate socially undesirable behavior less often than they understate it, but this one has a low response rate and recall bias: people misremember when they last nodded off, and "fallen asleep" is self-defined. The 55% could be inflated by loose interpretation (a two-second nod counts) or deflated by shame. Either way, the number is a survey artifact, not a census.
- The 2002→2026 comparison is methodologically shaky: different sampling frames, different modes, different wording eras. The +18 points may partly reflect willingness to admit it, not more actual sleeping.
- The messaging paradox is correlational: heavy drowsy drivers notice warnings more; it does not prove warnings cause drowsy driving. NHTSA's own offered explanation says as much.
- The 660M-episodes extrapolation annualizes a single 30-day recall window and applies a survey of adults to all licensed drivers; treat as order-of-magnitude, not a count.

## Limitations (dedicated accounting)
- Survey fielded in 2022, published November 2026: the behavior it measures is four years old, and driving patterns (post-pandemic commute mix) have shifted since.
- FARS captures fatal crashes only; drowsy driving's injury toll (~100,000 crashes/yr per NHTSA's long-standing estimate) is not in this article's numbers.
- No biomarker for drowsiness exists, so every death estimate (633 official, ~6,000 modeled) is an inference, and the modeled numbers carry their own uncertainty.
- Sample-size discrepancy: Traffic Tech says 3,765; ROSA blurb for the full report says 10,700. Article uses 3,765 and notes the full report exists (DOT HS 813 887) without asserting the larger figure.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- If your commute is over 30 minutes or you drive at night regularly, you are in the survey's highest-risk groups — plan sleep like fuel, not like a luxury.
- Six hours of sleep is the modal night before a fall-asleep episode. Treat under 7 hours as not fit to drive, not "a bit tired."
- The first hour is the danger zone, not hour six: if you feel drowsy 20 minutes in, pull over then — the "I'll wake up once I'm going" instinct is exactly backwards.
- Only Arkansas and New Jersey criminalize drowsy driving, and 80%+ of drivers don't know their state's law. Check yours; the law is not the deterrent, the nap is.
- No test, no app, no lane-keep system substitutes for sleep. Driver-monitoring that catches eyelid closure is the only tech that addresses this directly — check whether your car has it before your next night drive.
