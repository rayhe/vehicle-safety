The Gap

A Supplier Changed One Spring in 2021. Toyota Is Still Finding the Seatbacks It Broke.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026
☕ 3 min read

Your second-row seatback clicked into place when you adjusted it. Your hands said locked. On about 3.5 percent of the second-row seats Toyota tested, the seatback was lying.[4]

Inside the recliner assembly, two springs negotiate: a locking spring drives the teeth into the ratchet, and a return spring resists. In 2021, a seat-recliner supplier changed the design without recalculating the force balance between them. Depending on the return spring’s orientation and the clearance between the recliner guide and the ratchet, the teeth don’t fully engage.[5] You feel the lever move. You hear the click, but nothing actually locks.

636,474
vehicles across the US, Canada, and Australia traced to one supplier’s unbalanced spring

This is the part that bothers me. A blown headlight announces itself. A seatback that isn’t locked looks exactly like one that is. You adjust it, it stays where you put it, and the failure mode is invisible until the exact moment it matters: a crash at higher speeds, when an unrestrained seatback collapses forward and takes its occupant with it. Every restraint in the car assumes the seat is attached. It isn’t.

Toyota found it the boring way: a routine inspection at the factory in October 2023 caught a seatback skipping its first locking position, and engineers traced the failure to the 2021 supplier design change.[4] In a field survey of 343 second-row seats, 12 wouldn’t stay locked. Apply that 3.5 percent to the 550,007 Highlanders and Highlander Hybrids under US recall 26V128000, and you get roughly 19,000 seatbacks that can’t be trusted.[2] That is an upper bound, the surveyed vehicles were pre-screened as suspect, but even a fraction of it is a lot of unlatched seatbacks.

This week, Toyota Australia expanded the same campaign for the Kluger, the Highlander’s Australian twin. March’s original recall covered 34,377 Klugers built between March 2021 and November 2023; the build range now runs to June 2025, adding 12,090 vehicles and taking the total to 46,467.[5] Add roughly 40,000 Canadian Highlanders, and the census hits about 636,000 vehicles, every one of them traced to one supplier’s unrecalculated spring.[6]

If you drive a 2021–2024 Highlander or Highlander Hybrid, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, campaign 26V128000.[3] Dealers replace the return springs with improved ones, free. And here is a test Toyota won’t put in the letter: after adjusting a second-row seatback, shove it forward hard. If it skips a lock position or feels soft, get it inspected before you learn about it in a crash. Used-Highlander shoppers: run the VIN before you sign anything.

A spring is a thirty-cent part with a thirty-line calculation behind it. They changed the geometry and skipped the calculation. Toyota caught it on an assembly line in October 2023 and is still widening the recall in October 2026. Somewhere between those two dates, 12 out of every 343 seatbacks stopped being seats and became suggestions.
