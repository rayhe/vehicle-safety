Hyundai Deleted the Immobilizer. 35 States Just Made Them Put It Back in 9 Million Cars.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 25, 2026

Your car carries a part you have never seen, and its entire job is to decide whether a teenager with a USB cable can drive your car away. In roughly nine million Hyundais and Kias sold in America between 2011 and 2022, that part was never installed. Buyers in Canada and Mexico got it as standard equipment the entire time.[1]

The part is an engine immobilizer. It costs the manufacturer a few dozen dollars, sits silently in the ignition system, and refuses to start the car unless it recognizes the key. Starting in 2021, TikTok videos showed millions of viewers how to bypass its absence with a screwdriver and a USB cable. Federal regulators linked the trend to at least 14 crashes and eight fatalities as of 2023.[2]

On December 16, 2025, 35 state attorneys general, led by Minnesota's Keith Ellison, announced the bill had come due. Hyundai and Kia agreed to install free zinc-reinforced ignition cylinder protectors in more than four million vehicles, including cars that had previously gotten only a software patch. They will pay up to $9 million in restitution to consumers and the states. And they agreed to fit engine immobilizers to every future vehicle sold in the United States.[1][2] Read that last part again, because no federal motor vehicle safety standard requires an immobilizer. The European Union mandated them in 1998; NHTSA never did, so 35 attorneys general wrote the regulation themselves, in a settlement, without a single rulemaking. This is the same NHTSA that blew past a congressional deadline to require drunk-driving prevention tech and has produced zero final rules (see story #942). The states got tired of waiting.

Now the math, because the math is the insult. Ellison put the total repair cost at more than $500 million. Divide by nine million eligible vehicles and the retrofit runs about $55 a car. Fitting the part on the assembly line would have cost less than retrofitting it in a dealer service bay a decade later, which means Hyundai and Kia saved something under $55 per vehicle and bought themselves a half-billion-dollar liability, a multi-state investigation, and a theft wave that killed people. At his press conference, Ellison was joined by a woman whose mother was killed when a stolen Kia crashed into her parents' car.[1]

The fixes work, when people get them. The Highway Loss Data Institute found the anti-theft software upgrade cut whole-vehicle theft claims by 52 percent, and 2022-24 Hyundais and Kias, most of which have immobilizers standard, now get stolen at rates below the all-vehicle average.[3] The problem is uptake: as of December 2024, only about 40 percent of eligible vehicles had received the upgrade, and unpatched 2011-22 models were still being stolen at roughly five times the all-makes rate.[5] Nationally, thieves still took 31,712 Hyundai Elantras in 2024, the most-stolen vehicle in America.[4]

Here is what to do: if you own a 2011-2022 Hyundai or Kia with a turn-key ignition, check your VIN on the settlement site or the automakers' recall lookup now, not when the letter arrives. You have one year from the companies' notice to get the free hardware repair, and the repair window runs from early 2026 through early 2027, which means the clock is already ticking.[1] Got the earlier software update? You still need the zinc-sleeve protector; the settlement explicitly covers cars that only got software. Until then, park in lit areas and consider a steering-wheel club: cheap, ugly, effective.

A fair accounting of the limits: nobody knows the true body count. FARS, the federal crash database, has no field for "vehicle was stolen," so the 14 crashes and eight fatalities is a 2023 regulatory statement and almost certainly a floor, not a total. Theft counts measure stolen cars, not crashes, and most stolen cars never crash. And the honest counterargument deserves its full weight: no US regulation required immobilizers, so Hyundai and Kia complied with every rule on the books. The theft wave needed three ingredients, the missing part, the viral videos, and the kids who chose to steal the cars. Immobilizers are not theft-proof either; relay attacks defeat keyless systems routinely. This settlement fixes one specific vulnerability; it does not fix car theft.

"This crisis started in a boardroom, traveled through the internet and ended up in tragic results when somebody stole those cars," Ellison said.[1] He is right, and if you own one of the nine million, the free fix with your name on it is sitting at a dealership right now.
