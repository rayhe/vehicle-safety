The Week Aimed at Reckless Driving Caught 33 of Them. It Caught 691 Speeders Instead.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

Every year the Commercial Vehicle Safety Alliance picks a week, picks a target, and sends officers out across North America to catch it. This year the target was reckless, careless and dangerous driving, and the week was July 12 through 18, with 36 states, seven Canadian provinces and Mexico taking part. Officers interacted with 8,083 drivers and wrote 2,604 citations, plus 3,968 warnings. For the offense the whole week was built around, they wrote 33 citations.[1]

1.27%
of all citations written during Operation Safe Driver Week 2026 were for reckless, careless or dangerous driving, the behavior the week was organized to catch

Run the division yourself, because the program published the table and the table does the talking. Thirty-three reckless-driving citations out of 2,604 total is 1.27 percent, about one citation in every 79 written. Speeding produced 691 citations, 26.5 percent of the total, more than twenty times the focus offense. Seat-belt violations produced 311. Texting produced 181. Failure to obey a traffic control device produced 344 citations, ten times the focus offense. A week organized to catch reckless driving mostly caught people going too fast, because speeding is the violation a radar gun can prove in three seconds and reckless driving is the one a lawyer can argue about for three months.[1]

Now the second table inside the first one, the split between who got warned and who got ticketed. Commercial drivers collected 3,409 warnings against 1,956 citations, which means warnings were 63.5 percent of everything officers did to them. Passenger-car drivers collected 559 warnings against 648 citations, so tickets were 53.7 percent of what happened to them. Same week, same officers, same roads. A professional driver stopped during Safe Driver Week was more likely to leave with a lecture. An amateur was more likely to leave with a fine.

Passenger cars also took the speeding crown, which was not supposed to be theirs. Car drivers received 349 of the 691 speeding citations, 50.5 percent, while accounting for only 1,207 of 6,572 total enforcement actions, 18.4 percent. Truckers took 342. So a week aimed at commercial drivers wrote more speeding tickets to the cars passing the trucks than to the trucks themselves. Two more categories were nearly all trucker: seat-belt citations went 270 to 41, and traffic-control-device warnings went 652 to 24.[1]

Some scale, from the federal data, before anyone calls this a rounding error. Speeding contributed to 11,288 American traffic deaths in 2024, 29 percent of all fatalities that year, and the vans and trucks in this story are not bit players in the death count. In a decade of FARS data, the Ford E-350 van carries 776 deaths and an estimated rate of 2.51 per 100 million miles, roughly seventeen times the rate of its pickup sibling, the F-350, at 0.15. Read that as scale, not indictment: FARS counts fatal crashes, not stops, and its E-350s are mostly passenger and shuttle vans, not the commercial fleet CVSA inspects. Still, 776 dead in one van model is the size of the thing officers are standing on the shoulder for.[1][4]

Fair objection, stated at full strength, because this article is built on a press release and a press release is not a study. Eight thousand stops in a week when Americans drive roughly 60 billion miles is a rounding error. Reckless driving is hard to prove roadside and easy to write up as speeding instead, so the 33 citations measure the difficulty of the charge, not the rarity of the behavior. Warnings versus citations reflect enforcement culture, educate the professional and ticket the amateur, rather than any measured difference in how the two groups drive. Speeding leads every tally because a radar gun proves it in seconds. All of that is true. But the claim here is not that the blitz measured American driving. The claim is that the program's own scorecard shows its named target missing by a factor of twenty, its warnings landing on professionals and its tickets on amateurs, and its impairment enforcement touching 26 drivers in a week when alcohol-impaired driving kills 11,904 Americans a year. Those are the program's numbers, published by the program, and they are worth reading exactly as written.[1]

What to do with it is mostly unglamorous. If you drive a passenger car near trucks, slow down: the data says the radar is pointed at you, not just at them, and you took half the speeding tickets on less than a fifth of the stops. Buckle the belt on every trip, including the short ones, because belts saved an estimated 14,955 lives in 2017 and another 2,549 people who died that year would have lived if they had been wearing one. Commercial drivers should treat a warning like a ticket, because warnings are logged contacts and they accumulate. And check whether your state even took part: 36 states did, which means 14 did not, and enforcement intensity varies by state and by week.[1]
