IIHS Asked Delivery Vans to Stop for a Motorcycle. Most of Them Did Nothing.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 13, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. In March the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety announced it would finally grade commercial vehicles, starting with an occupant-protection checklist that asked whether work vans even had airbags.[1] On October 8 the institute released wave two: actual crash-avoidance testing, eleven vehicles aimed at a stopped car and a stopped motorcycle at 31, 37, and 43 mph, plus pedestrian runs against adult and child dummies by day and night. Several systems did not warn or brake at all in most of the motorcycle trials, and the Rivian delivery van recorded zero speed reduction and zero timely warnings in all six of its motorcycle runs.[1][4]

Seven of the eleven vehicles earned a Poor rating for vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention.[2] Only the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned a Good. The Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 managed Acceptable; everything else was Marginal or worse. And exactly none of the eleven earned a Good in both tests, which is a fun way to discover that nobody currently sells a commercial van that is excellent at not hitting things.

Now for the part that made me rebuild the chart. IIHS scores each vehicle on a composite: two-thirds speed reduction, one-third warning timing, with a warning counted as timely only if it arrives at least 2.1 seconds before the projected impact.[4] That blend hides two completely different diseases. The Ram ProMaster 2500 issued a timely warning in every single car and motorcycle run, then braked little or not at all at the higher speeds. It sees you, and simply chooses violence by omission. Rivian's delivery van has the opposite problem: against the car target it avoided four of six runs, nearly avoided a fifth, scrubbed 38 mph in the sixth, and warned on time every time. Against the motorcycle it did literally nothing, six times in a row: no warning, no braking. A composite of Marginal averages heroic car performance with total motorcycle blindness, which tells a fleet manager roughly nothing about what will happen when the van meets a bike.

Two vehicles need no such nuance, because the 2025-2027 Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500 earned Poor in both tests: they ship without automatic emergency braking at all, just optional forward collision warning.[2] These are among the most common work vans in America, and you can still buy one new in 2026. Your sedan effectively has to have AEB, but the van delivering your packages does not.

The pedestrian tests had their own horror subplot. Some vehicles detected the child-sized dummy, then appeared to lose track of it as they closed in, probably thanks to tall front ends and sensor placement.[6] A system that sees the kid and then forgets. Nighttime runs exposed another wrinkle: some automatic braking systems performed noticeably worse with low-beam headlights than with high beams, which means the same lighting supply chain this site keeps covering for contamination defects is quietly degrading the sensors behind it.

Context matters, so here it is: in 2024, 6,215 people were killed in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans, accounting for 16 percent of all U.S. roadway fatalities.[3] Back in June 2023, NHTSA estimated that a federal automatic-emergency-braking requirement for heavy vehicles could save 155 lives, prevent 8,814 injuries and more than 19,000 crashes a year, worth close to $2 billion annually.[2] That rule still is not finalized, more than three years later. Advocates for Highway and Auto Safety called the new ratings "a clear warning that too many commercial vehicles lack adequate front crash prevention" and demanded quick action to finish the standard.[2]

To be fair, and it pains me: the motorcycle target is genuinely hard. Bikes present a small radar cross-section, they lean, they move unpredictably, and a 14,000-pound van's sensor suite was never designed around them. Fleet buyers purchase on total cost of ownership over long replacement cycles, so today's ratings will take years to reach the road no matter what the scoreboard says. And the BrightDrop 400 avoiding the motorcycle in four of six runs proves the engineering is possible, which undercuts any "it's just physics" defense while also showing it isn't free or easy.

What to do: if you manage a fleet, stop treating AEB as a checkbox and start asking for the IIHS scorecards. The Sprinter 2500 and BrightDrop 400 prove a tall van can brake for people; the Express and Savana prove you can still buy one that won't. If you ride a motorcycle, ride like every Amazon van is the Rivian in this test: completely blind to you, six runs out of six.

Limitations

IIHS tested a defined cohort of current-generation models; results do not generalize to older model years, other trim packages, or the full commercial fleet. Track tests use instrumented targets at fixed speeds (31/37/43 mph); real motorcycles lean, accelerate, and change lanes, and the test does not replicate that. A Good rating here is not a guarantee of real-world detection. The 2.1-second timely-warning threshold is an IIHS construct that manufacturers dispute. Per-vehicle ratings are as reported by press coverage of the IIHS release; I did not independently re-verify each rating on IIHS's own pages. The 6,215 deaths cover all crashes involving heavy/medium-duty vehicles or light vans, not AEB-preventable ones specifically.
