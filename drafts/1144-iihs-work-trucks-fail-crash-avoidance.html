IIHS Asked 11 Work Trucks to Stop for a Child. Most of Them Didn't.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
August 28, 2027

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: the vehicles most likely to kill you on the road are the worst at avoiding you. On October 8, IIHS released its first-ever crash-avoidance ratings for commercial vehicles: eleven cargo vans and Class 3 pickups, tested like passenger cars, by driving them at dummies and counting who stops.[1] Seven of the eleven earned a Poor rating at the basic task of not rear-ending another vehicle. Not a motorcycle, not a child, just a car.

6,215
people killed in 2024 in crashes involving medium- or heavy-duty vehicles or light vans: 16% of all US road deaths[4]

Only one vehicle, the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500, earned a Good rating for vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention. The pedestrian test went slightly better, and the winners tell their own story: the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 and the Rivian RCV-Delivery 500, both electric, both rated Good at not running over pedestrians. Neither is a diesel workhorse. Zero of the nine combustion vehicles managed a Good pedestrian rating, which says something about which century the designs come from.

Now the part that kept me up: in the pedestrian tests, several trucks detected the child-sized dummy at a distance, then lost it as they got closer, because the sensors sit up by the rearview mirror and the hoods are tall, and somewhere in the last fifty feet a kid simply falls out of the machine's world, which IIHS clinically describes as the child "disappearing." The Acceptable-rated F-350 and Ram 3500 still struggled more with a child in daylight than an adult at night, and motorcycles fared worse still. In most motorcycle trials, several systems issued no warning and applied no braking at all, which is worth sitting with if you ride.

The Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500 have no automatic emergency braking whatsoever: forward collision warning is optional. They earned Poor in both tests, which tracks for vans whose basic architecture predates the Obama administration, and they are still for sale. You can still buy a brand-new van that cannot brake for anything.

The strongest case for the defense is that this is the first round. Passenger cars have had two decades of IIHS ratings pressure; these trucks have had four days. Heavy vehicles are genuinely harder to instrument, with tall fronts and varying loads, and the Sprinter, BrightDrop, and Rivian already clearing the bar proves the problem is solvable, not physical.

What the ratings don't tell you: they measure avoidance systems, not crashworthiness, so a Poor-avoidance truck may still protect its driver fine. Standardized dummies and speeds are not your loaded work truck in the rain. My EV cross-tab runs on a sample of two electrics, which is suggestive rather than conclusive.

What you can do: if you buy or spec work vehicles, the Sprinter 2500 is the only Good at not hitting cars, and the BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 are the only Goods at not hitting pedestrians. Ask whether a van has AEB before you sign, because the Express and Savana 2500 don't have it. NHTSA has sat on a heavy-vehicle AEB proposal since 2023 whose own math promises 155 lives and 19,000 crashes a year; advocates are using these ratings to push it forward.[3] And if you ride a motorcycle or walk anywhere near a work site, assume the truck does not see you, because in several trials it literally didn't.
