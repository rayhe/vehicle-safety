America Celebrated Fewer Road Deaths. 183 Counties Got Deadlier Anyway.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 22, 2026

NHTSA spent 2026 telling America the roads are getting safer, and the national numbers back it up. Deaths fell for a third straight year in 2024, down to 39,254, and the early estimate for 2025 drops further, to 36,640.[2][3] Zoom in one level, though, and the celebration thins out. In 183 of America's 286 largest counties, the road death rate was higher in 2024 than it was in 2019.[1] The country got safer, but most of the places where Americans actually live did not.

183
of America's 286 largest counties had a higher road death rate in 2024 than in 2019 — nearly two-thirds of the counties where most Americans live.

Censal Research ran the comparison county by county, using NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System and Census Bureau population estimates, and published the map on November 5. The split is stark: big counties, home to 64 percent of Americans, saw their death rate rise 9.5 percent, from 8.3 to 9.1 per 100,000 residents. Everywhere else, the rate did not move at all: 16.1 in 2019, 16.1 in 2024. Rural roads are still far deadlier per resident, but the cities started catching up.[1]

Seven counties cleared the statistical bar, and their rises are too large to be noise, even after correcting for testing 286 counties at once:

Two counties did most of the damage. Harris County, Texas, added 162 deaths, and Maricopa County, Arizona, added 154. Together that is 316 more deaths than in 2019, over a tenth of the entire national increase of 2,899, from two counties alone. And the Bronx, the safest story in the table and the scariest at once, nearly doubled, from 27 deaths to 52. Its rate is still 3.7 per 100,000, a third of the national average, while Manhattan and Brooklyn sit at 2.4, the lowest of any million-plus county. Absolute risk and trend are different stories, and the Bronx is both at once.[1]

Who died changed faster than how many. Pedestrian deaths rose 12.9 percent, cyclist deaths 25.6 percent, motorcyclist deaths 23.4 percent, and deaths in speeding-related crashes rose 17.7 percent, all against 8.0 percent for road deaths overall and 3 percent population growth. Read that stack again: every group outside a car got killed faster than the country as a whole, and motorcyclists alone account for 1,181 of the 2,899 additional deaths, about 41 percent of the entire increase. Whatever recovery the national numbers describe, it is a car-occupant recovery.[1]

Fairness requires the other side: deaths fell three straight years from the 2021 peak, every quarter since mid-2022 is down year over year, and the 2025 fatality rate of 1.10 per 100 million miles is the second-lowest ever recorded. Choosing 2019 as the baseline flatters the bad news, because 2019 was the low point before the pandemic spike. Compare 2024 with 2021 and nearly every county looks better. But county data for 2025 does not exist yet. When it arrives in early 2027, it will answer the only question that matters: did the surge unwind, or did it move? Until then, the map says the national victory lap was premature in 183 counties.[3]

Look up your own county, because Censal publishes all 3,133 of them, with 2019 and 2024 rates and a rank against the state. If yours is one of the 183, the improvement skipped you, and the decisions that fix it — speed management, pedestrian infrastructure, enforcement — are made at the county and city level, not in Washington. If you walk, bike, or ride a motorcycle, the mode split is your warning: the roads got more dangerous specifically for people outside a car. Wear the hi-vis, take the separated route, and show up to the meeting where the arterial gets redesigned.[1]

The study compares two years, 2019 and 2024; deaths peaked in 2021 and have fallen since, so a two-year comparison cannot show whether a county's 2024 elevation is a persistent new level or the tail of the 2021 peak. Rates are per resident, not per mile driven, and deaths are counted where the crash happened, not where the victim lived, so counties with heavy through traffic or many visitors can show high rates. The study is associational: it shows where rates changed, not why. NHTSA's 2025 figures are early estimates, revisable when the FARS Final File for 2024 and the Annual Report File for 2025 arrive in early 2027, and county-level 2025 data is not yet published.
