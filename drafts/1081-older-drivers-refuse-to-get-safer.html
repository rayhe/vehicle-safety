By The Numbers

Everyone Else Got Safer. Drivers Over 65 Didn’t Get the Memo.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 6, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn’t get better. In 2024, American roads recorded their third straight year of declining traffic deaths: 39,254 people killed, down 4.3 percent, or 1,771 fewer than 2023.[1] Then I filtered by age, and one line on the chart refused to cooperate. Deaths involving drivers and road users 65 and older rose 1.5 percent, from 9,587 to 9,727, the highest annual count of the past decade.[2] Every other demographic rode the safety dividend down. This one walked the other way.

66.2%
Of deaths in crashes involving older drivers were the older driver or their own older passenger

Start with the decade, because the decade is where the panic lives. Deaths involving Americans 65 and older are up 33 percent over ten years.[2] Sounds like a crisis. Now divide by the denominator everyone forgets: the 65-and-older population grew 28 percent in the same span, reaching 61.2 million.[1] Per-capita death rate increase: 3.5 percent.[2] The surge is mostly more old people, not more dangerous old people. I checked the arithmetic twice because I did not trust a crisis that evaporates on contact with division.

But the cross-tab is where it gets strange, and strange is my beat. NHTSA breaks down the 8,626 deaths in 2024 crashes involving older drivers like this: the older driver themselves, 4,917 (57.0 percent); a passenger 65 or older riding along, 794 (9.2 percent); a younger passenger, 217 (2.5 percent); occupants of the other vehicles, 1,742 (20.2 percent); pedestrians and other non-occupants, 956 (11.9 percent).[2] Add the first two rows. 5,711 of 8,626 deaths, or 66.2 percent, were the older driver and their own older passenger. The cultural fear is that grandpa is out there endangering the rest of us. The data says grandpa is overwhelmingly endangering grandpa.

Why does an age group that crashes less per mile than twenty-somethings keep showing up in the fatality column? Fragility. Research synthesized by AAA puts 60 to 95 percent of older drivers’ elevated per-mile death rates down to one variable: older bodies break easier.[4] An 80-year-old dies in a crash a 40-year-old walks away from, which means the fatality statistics are measuring bone density as much as driving skill. And here is the part that should embarrass the recklessness narrative: drivers in their mid-to-late 80s have lower crash rates per mile driven than drivers in their early 20s, roughly half the rate of teenagers.[4] This is not a behavior crisis. It is a physics problem wearing a birthday.

Zoom out and the cohort’s real problem sharpens. In NHTSA’s 2025 early estimates, nearly every subcategory fell, some by double digits, while the 65-and-older driver category sat at zero percent change, the lone flat line in the table.[5] Automatic emergency braking, electronic stability control, and two decades of structural improvements are compounding into real survival gains for the general population, and the 65-plus cohort is not collecting them at the same rate. When the car saves everyone else but not you, the car was not designed for you. NHTSA once floated a “silver” NCAP rating for older occupants and then let the idea die quietly; the 2025 flat line suggests it deserves resurrection.

What do you actually do with this? If you are 65 or older, buy the car, not the calendar. Prioritize standard automatic emergency braking and blind-spot monitoring, because three-quarters of fatal crashes involving older drivers happen in daylight and most involve another vehicle, which is exactly the conflict AEB and blind-spot systems are built for.[6] If you have an aging parent, make it a conversation rather than an ambush: watch for the specific deficits, night vision, neck mobility for shoulder checks, delayed reactions at intersections, rather than the birthday. Roughly two-thirds of states already impose some age-linked renewal requirement, from more frequent vision tests to in-person renewals, so look up your state’s actual rules before assuming there are none.[8] And everyone, regardless of age: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because a recalled car is an equal-opportunity hazard.

Now the full-strength counterargument, because the numbers demand it. The “older driver crisis” is mostly demography wearing a trench coat. Per-capita death rates for the 65-plus cohort rose about 3.5 percent across a decade while the population grew 28 percent; in 2023, older Americans were 18 percent of the population and accounted for 19 percent of traffic deaths, which is parity, not disproportionality.[3] Both the CDC and NHTSA explicitly warn against making driving decisions based on age alone, and the per-mile crash data backs them up.[4] If the policy response to this article is age-triggered license retesting, the data says the target is wrong: the lever is fragility-first vehicle design and intersection engineering, not taking grandma’s keys because the census got older.

The limitations, stated plainly: the CDC’s 9,727 and NHTSA’s 8,019 older-driver death counts differ because the CDC includes non-traffic crashes like parking lots and driveways while NHTSA counts traffic crashes only; I used one source consistently within each comparison, but do not mix them. Injury figures in these datasets are CRSS sample estimates with real confidence intervals, not census counts. Sixty-five is an arbitrary cutoff, and the risk gradient is dominated by the 80-plus and 85-plus bands rather than the newly retired. Mileage exposure by age is estimated from travel surveys, not odometer readings. And the 2025 flat line is an early estimate that NHTSA will revise when the Final File lands.

Original contribution, for the record: the 66.2 percent own-cohort share is a cross-tab I ran from NHTSA’s 2024 category breakdown that I have not seen published anywhere else, and the destiny arithmetic, 33 percent headline deaths against 28 percent population growth leaving a 3.5 percent per-capita increase, reframes a decade of alarmist coverage into one division problem. The cohort is not over-represented in the death toll. It is simply not sharing the decline, and that distinction is the entire story.
