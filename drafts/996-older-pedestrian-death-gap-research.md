**Headline candidate:** "Your Grandmother Isn't Getting Hit More Often. She's Just Dying of the Same Crash."

## The kill test
Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. The core finding is a paradox with a fresh news peg: KFF Health News (Paula Span, Aug 28, 2026) surfaced federal data showing older pedestrians are NOT injured by vehicles at a higher rate than younger pedestrians — they simply die of the same collisions far more often, and that gap has barely moved in nearly two decades. No existing queue item covers older pedestrians (sweep verified 2026-09-27). The site's usual fare is recalls and crash tests; a body-count-by-biology story is a first.

Novel angle: two decades of pedestrian safety messaging aimed at teaching older people to cross more carefully was aimed at the wrong variable. The difference is frailty (what the body does with the impact), vehicle size (taller hoods, blind zones), and exposure (they're walking more). Plus the perverse geography: older pedestrians die most where senior destinations concentrate — pharmacies, hospitals, senior centers — the exact streets designed around them. Stephen Mooney's line is the thesis: traffic safety "shouldn't rely on a pedestrian's noticing (of) a car coming and jumping out of the way."

## Verified stats (all from primary or named-source reporting)
- Nearly 8,200 pedestrians age 65+ injured in 2023 (NHTSA data, via KFF Health News); likely an undercount since not all serious crashes reach police reports. More than 1,500 died. Injury rate NOT higher than younger pedestrians; death rate above the all-ages average.
- IIHS figures, via Columbia epidemiologist Andrew Rundle: from ~2008 through 2024, the pedestrian fatality rate for age 70+ stayed ABOVE the all-ages average and "stayed stubbornly flat" — ~16-20 years of no improvement.
- NHTSA 2024 (Traffic Safety Facts, "Pedestrians: 2024 Data", FARS 2024 ARF): 7,080 pedestrian deaths nationwide; 71,000+ pedestrians injured.
- CDC: adults 65+ were ~17% of the U.S. population in 2022 but 22% of pedestrian deaths.
- National Health and Aging Trends Study: share of older adults reporting walking for exercise rose from 60% (2011) to 65% (2023) — exposure rising.
- NYT investigation (June 2026): taller hoods and larger blind zones of SUVs/trucks documented as contributors to rising pedestrian fatalities.
- PubMed study (cited by KFF/MedicalDaily): risk substantially higher in areas with high density of senior destinations (hospitals, pharmacies, senior/community centers) — school-zone-safety analog proposed.
- NHTSA early estimate (published April 1, 2026): 36,640 traffic deaths in 2025, down 6.7% from 39,254 in 2024; rate 1.10 per 100M VMT (second-lowest ever); VMT up 0.9%; 39 states + DC + PR declined. Contrast point: the system is improving overall, and this one group is frozen.
- Quotes: Charles DiMaggio (NYU Grossman, injury epidemiology): "the consequences of injuries are stratospherically different the older you get" / "A hip fracture in a 45-year-old is unfortunate. In a 75-year-old, it's tragic." Rundle (Columbia): slower crossing, reduced hearing/vision, slower reaction time. Mooney (U. Washington): "Our job is to make the system safer for all ages and capacities."
- Human detail: Meredith Melville, 73, retired teacher, crossing Piedmont Ave in Oakland (crosswalk), hit by Toyota sedan; hip replacement, broken elbow repaired, knee replacement pending; wheelchair → walker → cane. "It could have been a lot worse." It often is.
