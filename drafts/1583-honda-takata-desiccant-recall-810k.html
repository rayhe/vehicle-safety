The Desiccant Was Supposed to Fix Takata. Honda Just Recalled 810,592 Cars Anyway.

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
November 25, 2026

Six years ago, Honda's engineers ran the numbers on the airbag inflators in 810,592 of its cars and concluded the danger would not arrive before 2027, but it arrived on November 19, 2026, the date Honda filed recall 26V765 with NHTSA, pulling back 2012-2013 Civics and CR-Vs, 2013 Accords, and 2013 Acura ILXs and RDXs because the propellant inside their Takata airbag inflators is degrading. These were not the original Takata inflators; they were the fixed ones, the desiccated ones, the inflators Honda and NHTSA agreed in 2020 were safe enough to leave in cars. The fix had an expiration date, and nobody printed it on the box.[1]

100%
Estimated share of the 810,592 recalled vehicles carrying the defect, per Honda's own filing with NHTSA

Inside each inflator sits a pellet of phase-stabilized ammonium nitrate, PSAN, the same chemical family that turned Takata into a bankruptcy case. Honda's version added a desiccant, a drying agent called 13X, meant to keep moisture out of the propellant for the life of the car. Moisture was the killer in the original crisis: humid air and repeated temperature swings made the propellant porous and brittle, so it burned too fast on deployment and blew the metal inflator housing apart like a grenade. The desiccant was the industry's answer to that chemistry problem, and for six years the answer held. In June 2020, Honda submitted its final analysis to NHTSA and reported no degradation in parts recovered from the field. Its statistical model said the propellant would not degrade enough to risk a rupture before 2027, at the earliest.[1]

Then the simulations started talking back. In May 2025, Honda ran inflators through severe environmental testing and saw signs of propellant degradation, though the parts stayed within normal operating parameters. Eighteen months of further analysis followed, and Honda's own filing says the results did not show a statistically significant deviation from the 2020 prediction, which is worth reading twice, because the data did not deviate from the model and Honda recalled the cars anyway. On November 19, 2026, the company determined a safety defect existed. Somewhere between the model and the metal, the prediction failed, and Honda chose the recall over the argument.[1]

Half the recall is one car: the 2012-2013 Civic, 413,125 of them, more than 50 percent of the total, with another 197,045 CR-Vs and 165,225 Accords behind it. Acura's ILX and RDX, the luxury end of the list, account for 14,643 and 20,554. Add them up and the population is 810,592, and Honda's filing lists the estimated percentage with the defect as 100 percent. Not some. Not most. Every one.[1][5]

Only some of these cars are recalled, and the map explains why. Honda limited the campaign to vehicles sold or ever registered in nine states and five territories where heat, humidity, and big temperature swings do their worst: California, Texas, Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, South Carolina, Hawaii, plus Puerto Rico, Guam, American Samoa, the Northern Mariana Islands, and the U.S. Virgin Islands. It is the same climate map as the original Takata crisis, drawn a decade later, and desiccant bought time, not immunity. In Florida, time ran out first.[1]

Honda was not alone that week. Toyota filed its own Takata recall on November 12, campaign 26V748, covering 314,029 vehicles in the United States and 790,000 worldwide, including old friends like the 2003-2008 Corolla and the 2005-2007 Sequoia. Together the two recalls cover about 1.6 million vehicles. Step back far enough and the scale gets absurd: the Takata recall has already touched almost 70 million vehicles in the United States alone, and roughly 400 people in this country have been injured by rupturing inflators. Nine years after Takata went bankrupt, the company that no longer exists is still recalling cars.[2][3]

Honda's remedy is the most honest sentence in the filing. Dealers will replace the inflator free, and the replacement comes from a different supplier using a propellant with a different chemical composition, one that does not degrade over the long term in heat and humidity. That is not a repair; that is a surrender. Honda is not fixing the PSAN inflator; it is abandoning the propellant family entirely. When a manufacturer swaps both the supplier and the chemistry, it is telling you the problem was never a bad batch. It was the recipe.[1]

Give Honda its due, because the other side of this story is real: nobody has been hurt by these inflators, and as of November 19, Honda had received no warranty claims and no reports of injury or death tied to them. The monitoring program Honda agreed to with NHTSA and Exponent in December 2021 caught degradation in simulation in May 2025, before a single inflator ruptured in the field. A precautionary recall of 810,592 cars with zero injuries attached is what a functioning safety regime is supposed to look like. The desiccant bought six years of safe service for inflators that would otherwise have been pulled in 2015. If you want to call that a failure, you have to explain what success was supposed to look like.[1]

What to do, concretely. If you drive a 2012-2013 Civic or CR-V, a 2013 Accord, ILX, or RDX, and the car was ever sold or registered in one of those nine states or five territories, check the VIN now at recalls.honda.com, recalls.acura.com, or nhtsa.gov/recalls, and do not wait for the letter. Honda's mailed notifications do not start until December 18, and these cars are thirteen years old, which means many of them are on second or third owners who never saw the first Takata letter. The repair is free, and this time the replacement part is not another Takata inflator. It is a different propellant from a different company, and Honda's phone line is (888) 234-2138. Eighteen years after the Takata crisis began, the lesson is the same one it always was: the airbag that saves you in a crash is only as safe as the chemistry inside it, and chemistry has a shelf life.[4]
