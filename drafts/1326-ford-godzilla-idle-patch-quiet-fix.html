Ford Already Fixed the Godzilla V8. It Just Didn't Tell Anybody.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 29, 2026

On October 23, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened defect-petition review DP26011 into Ford's 7.3-liter "Godzilla" V8. The allegation that matters is not the camshaft wear. It is that Ford reportedly fixed the problem with a software update raising idle speed to 1,000 rpm, never notified the owners of roughly 54,787 trucks and vans, and issued no recall.[1][2]

6 psi
Alleged oil pressure at low idle — too little to keep the cam lobes and lifter rollers alive

A fix that exists but was never announced is a confession written in code. Think about what the idle bump implies: someone inside Ford decided the engine could not survive its own idle, wrote the calibration that proves it, and shipped it to dealers without a word to the people driving 54,787 vans, chassis trucks, and F-750s. If the engine was fine, why did idle need raising?

The paper trail is two-tracked, and that is the tell. Since July 2025, Ford dealers have been replacing worn cams and tappets under Technical Service Bulletin 25-2322, service kit LC3Z-6250-D, for anyone who complains about a ticking engine at idle.[3] TSBs are not recalls, and no letter goes out. Owners learn about it only if they hear the tick, bring the truck in, and the service writer knows where to look. The fleet owner in North Carolina who reported five dead engines out of eleven trucks in February 2024 figured it out the expensive way, failures landing between 45,745 and 103,177 miles.[4]

Now the counterargument, stated at full strength, because it deserves it. One reported incident is a thin reed for a federal probe. Hundreds of thousands of Godzillas are in service, and lifter wear follows bad oil-change intervals, cheap oil, and idling habits as reliably as it follows low oil pressure. A 0.04% observed complaint rate (twenty ODI complaints against the 2021 E-350) could mean the fleet owner's 45% failure rate is the outlier, not the canary. Ford's idle calibration might be routine drivability tuning. The TSB might be exactly what it looks like: a repair procedure for a rare problem. If NHTSA grants the petition and finds the failure rate unremarkable, "we fix the ones that tick" will turn out to have been the rational response all along.

What it does not prove is what matters most. This is a petition review, not a finding of defect. NHTSA's first summary lists no crashes, fires, injuries, or fatalities, and the petition itself is not public — everything above runs through the agency's summary and four days of auto-press reporting.[5] What is documented is the timeline: a repair procedure for cam wear has existed inside Ford's dealer channel for over a year while the public record held no recall and, per the petitioner, owners were never told why their trucks idle faster now. Silent for most owners, surgical for the ones who complain. That is a two-track remedy, whether or not it is a cover-up.

If you own a 2021-2022 E-350, 2021 F-59, or 2021 F-750 with the 7.3L: listen for an upper-engine tick at idle. If you hear it, ask the dealer to follow TSB 25-2322 by name and keep every repair order. File an NHTSA complaint and cite DP26011, then check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today — not because a recall exists, but because the day one does, you will want to be first in line, not last to hear.[6]
