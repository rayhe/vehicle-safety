One Every 44 Minutes: Drunk-Driving Deaths Fell 4%, and So Did Everything Else.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 17, 2026

According to the press releases, America's drunk-driving problem is finally getting better. NHTSA kicked off its annual Drive Sober or Get Pulled Over campaign this month with the good news: alcohol-impaired driving deaths fell nearly 4% in 2024, to 11,904, as Administrator Jonathan Morrison declared the agency is "using every tool we have to make our roads safer."[3] Confetti time, except then I divided by the total.

30.3%
Share of U.S. traffic deaths involving an alcohol-impaired driver in 2024, versus 30.2% in 2023.

In 2023, 12,382 alcohol-impaired driving deaths out of 41,025 total traffic fatalities: 30.2%.[1][2] In 2024, 11,904 out of 39,254: 30.3%. Alcohol deaths fell 3.9%. All deaths fell 4.3%. The tide went out, and the drunk drivers went out with it at exactly the same speed. Nobody's enforcement campaign gets to claim this one. Run the metronome yourself: 525,600 minutes in a year divided by 11,904 gives one alcohol-impaired death every 44 minutes, then and now, like a clock that does not care about your press conference.

Sports fans get box scores; I get FARS tables. Ages 21 to 24 had the highest share of alcohol-impaired drivers in fatal crashes (28%), and male drivers ran at 22% versus 16% for females.[1] NHTSA's own campaign materials narrow it further: men ages 21 to 34 are the largest demographic, and fatal crashes involving an impaired driver happen at triple the rate at night.[3] The risk profile has not changed in a decade. Young men, late at night, alcohol. If impaired driving were a baseball stat, this would be a guy batting .300 for ten straight seasons while the announcers celebrate each groundout that stays in the infield.

Here is the strongest case for the other side, stated at full strength: 478 fewer dead people is 478 families that did not get the call, and those are real lives, not shares. It is also entirely possible that interlocks, checkpoints, and the rideshare economy are working, but their effect is being masked by other rising risks, since speeding alone factored into 29% of fatalities.[4] A flat share does not prove enforcement failed. It proves the topline cannot tell us whether it worked.

What to do with this, since "be concerned" is not an action. If you are out after dark, the data has one instruction: arrange the ride before the first drink, because the demographic curve and the clock are both against you. Policy-wise, the tools that move the number are unglamorous and proven. AAA's June 2026 state briefs put ignition-interlock mandates for all offenders at up to 26% fewer fatal impaired-driving crashes, and a 0.05 per se BAC limit at roughly 11% fewer impaired-driving deaths.[5] Utah already did the 0.05 part. Everyone else is still celebrating a 4% decline that is, statistically speaking, the sound of one hand clapping.

What this did not prove

Alcohol-impaired here means at least one driver with BAC at or above 0.08 in a fatal crash, per the FARS definition; both years' figures use multiple imputation for missing BAC values (Rubin & Schafer, 1998), so each carries imputation uncertainty. Drug-impaired driving is a separate, murkier bucket and was not part of this calculation. These aggregates cannot attribute the decline to any specific cause: enforcement, ADAS adoption, fleet turnover, and VMT mix are all candidates. The share comparison uses final FARS/ARF totals (39,254 and 41,025); the early estimates differed slightly (39,345 and 40,901), which does not change the result to one decimal place.
