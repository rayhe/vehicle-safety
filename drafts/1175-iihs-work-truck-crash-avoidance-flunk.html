The Gap

The Biggest Trucks on the Road Can't Avoid Crashing Into You. IIHS Just Graded Them.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 15, 2026

The test dummy never saw it coming, which is the entire job description of a test dummy. In IIHS testing published October 8, a 2025 Ford F-350 rolled toward a stationary passenger-car target at forty-three miles an hour, and its crash-prevention system did nothing. No warning chime, no automatic braking, not even a token scrub of speed. It just kept rolling, which is what four tons of truck does when nobody tells it to stop.[1] In 2024, crashes involving medium- and heavy-duty vehicles or light vans killed 6,215 people in the United States — sixteen percent of every road death in the country.[2]

Class 3 pickups that earned better than Marginal at avoiding a collision with another vehicle — three scored Poor

Read that again, because the symmetry is the story. Vehicles dealing out sixteen percent of America's road fatalities are the ones least able to avoid dealing them out.

October 8 marked the second act of IIHS's commercial-vehicle program, which launched in June with a driver-protection checklist. We covered that round: four of nine vehicles passed, and the 1996-vintage Chevrolet Express failed to protect its own driver. This time the institute graded what these machines do to everyone else — eleven vehicles, four Class 3 pickups and seven cargo vans, run through front crash-prevention tests against passenger cars, motorcycles, semi trailers, and pedestrians.[3]

Flunked is the word for the pickups: Silverado 3500HD, Poor; F-350, Poor; Sierra 3500HD, Poor; Ram 3500, Marginal — which in IIHS grading is the polite way of saying better than disaster. Not a single pickup managed a Good in either test. Chevy's system failed to avoid the passenger-car target in all six of its vehicle-to-vehicle trials. Ford's could manage an avoidance at thirty-one miles an hour and then gave up entirely; at forty-three, as noted, it watched the collision happen at full speed.[1]

Pedestrian results were marginally less grim. F-350 and Ram 3500 earned Acceptable; the two GM twins managed Marginal. Lighter and lower, the cargo vans did better as a class: the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned the only Good in vehicle-to-vehicle prevention, while the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 took Good in the pedestrian test. At the bottom of the van pile sit the Express and GMC Savana at Poor and Poor, the predictable result when your safety technology dates to the Clinton administration. Neither van offers automatic emergency braking at any price; forward collision warning is optional, and braking for you is simply not on the menu.[1][4]

Two details from the test program deserve more attention than the headline ratings got. First, motorcycles, which the sensors largely cannot see: IIHS ran the trucks against motorcycle targets too, and most systems failed to warn the driver or slow the vehicle, with none producing warnings early enough to satisfy the institute.[5] Second, children, who can vanish entirely from the sightline: testing showed drivers can lose sight of child-sized pedestrians in front of these trucks as they approach, the kid simply gone from view, with the sensors not picking up the slack.[5]

Now the math nobody ran, because the ratings alone undersell the physics. Kinetic energy is one-half mass times velocity squared. At the 14,000-pound class ceiling, a truck moving 43 mph (63.1 feet per second) carries roughly 865,000 foot-pounds of energy: one-half times 434.8 slugs times 63.1 squared. A 3,200-pound sedan at the same speed carries about 198,000 foot-pounds. That is a 4.4-to-1 ratio, and the F-350 that failed to reduce its speed at all at 43 mph arrived carrying more than four times the destructive energy of the family sedan in the next lane — with an avoidance system that had exactly one job.

The energy jump between the 31-mph test the F-350 passed and the 43-mph test where it never touched the brakes

Inside the truck's own performance envelope, the picture darkens further. Ford's system avoided the target at 31 mph but not at 43, and since energy scales with the square of velocity, (43/31)² means the truck carried 1.9 times the energy in the test it ignored versus the test it passed. Twelve miles an hour separates stopped from did-not-slow-down-at-all. That is not a gradual degradation curve; that is a cliff.

Plot the two gradients together and the pattern is unmistakable: as gross vehicle weight rating climbs, crash-prevention performance falls, while lethality to everyone outside the vehicle rises. Heaviest class tested, worst scores. Lightest vans, best scores. Danger dealt and danger avoided run in opposite directions — the kind of finding that should make regulators reach for a pen.

About those regulators: IIHS vice president of vehicle research David Kidd told the Detroit Free Press that wide gaps separate what federal standards require of passenger vehicles from what they require of commercial vehicles. Airbags, advanced restraints, automatic emergency braking that detects vehicles and pedestrians — required for your Camry, simply not required for the box truck behind it.[6] His seven-word summary of the asymmetry: when the big ones crash, they deal out the harm. IIHS president David Harkey called the results a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers, which is institute-speak for we are about to make your life difficult.[3]

Fairness demands the counterargument at full strength. These are work vehicles, often driven by professionals who log more careful miles in a month than most commuters log in a year, and the ratings grade the technology rather than the drivers. Calibrating automatic emergency braking for a vehicle that might weigh 8,500 pounds empty and 14,000 loaded, possibly towing, is a genuinely harder engineering problem than calibrating it for a Civic. Test speeds sit near the physical limits of what any system can do with that much mass, and a system that merely mitigates — slowing the truck before impact — still saves lives even when it cannot avoid. Comparing a one-ton dually to a sedan was always going to flatter the sedan.

All true, and all beside the point in one specific way: until October 8, nobody had graded these vehicles at all. The wonder is not that a 14,000-pound truck scores worse than a Camry. The wonder is that the sixteen-percent-of-all-deaths vehicle class went untested while regulators mandated the technology for everyone else and then exempted the trucks. Somewhere in the thirty years the Express has been on sale without automatic emergency braking, "the physics is hard" stopped being an explanation and became an alibi.

Fleet buyers shopping cargo vans should know the Sprinter 2500 earned the only Good in vehicle-to-vehicle prevention, while the BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 earned Good for pedestrians. Cross the Express and Savana off the list: Poor and Poor, vans you can still buy new today that cannot brake for a pedestrian because they were designed before braking for pedestrians was something anyone asked a van to do. Heavy-duty pickup shoppers get a least-bad pick in the Ram 3500. Motorcyclists should internalize that the truck behind them probably cannot see them, electronically or otherwise — ride like its sensors are decorative. Parents: keep small children out of the front blind zone of work trucks and delivery vans, because IIHS found they can vanish from the driver's view and the electronics will not save them. And before assuming any work vehicle has crash-prevention tech, check the actual IIHS ratings — presence of the hardware is not the same as competence of the hardware.[7]

What this analysis did not prove, stated plainly. IIHS ratings come from controlled track tests against standardized targets, and real-world performance varies with load, maintenance, weather, and the driver. Only eleven vehicles were tested, and the pickup sample is four models — thin, though it covers the segment's volume sellers. The 4.4-to-1 energy ratio uses the 14,000-pound class ceiling against a 3,200-pound sedan; at typical curb weights the ratio is closer to two and a half to one, still lopsided, just less cinematic. FARS 2024 is the latest complete year available, and its 6,215 deaths are all fatalities in crashes involving these vehicles, not deaths caused by them — some of those 6,215 were the truck drivers themselves. Ratings measure avoidance, so a truck that slows from 43 to 20 before impact has still failed the test and still saved a life; that mitigation does not show up in a Poor.
