Honda's Steering Software Divides by Zero. The Failsafe's Answer Is to Stop Steering.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

Somewhere inside 635,808 Honda and Acura steering racks, a piece of software is doing arithmetic it learned in elementary school and failing it. On November 10, 2026, Honda recalled the 2023–2025 Pilot, the 2022–2024 MDX and the 2021–2025 TLX because the electric power steering software can perform a floating-point division by zero while the car is driving straight, and when the math breaks, the system's response is to switch the power steering off entirely.[1]

Read that mechanism twice, because Honda's own filing describes two separate bugs wearing one recall number. Bug one lives in the Driver Attention Monitor road-condition compensation calculation, written by supplier Robert Bosch Automotive Steering of Florence, Kentucky: under certain conditions, driving straight, the software divides by a zero denominator and throws an internal error.[1] Bug two is the error handler. Somebody configured the response to an internal error as: disable power steering assist. The filing calls that response "improperly configured," which is the driest possible way to say the safety net was wired to the trapdoor.

635,808
Vehicles recalled, and Honda estimates 100% of them carry the software

And here is the part that should keep you up at night, or at least keep you in the right lane. The calculation that divides by zero belongs to the Driver Attention Monitor. That is the system whose entire job is watching whether you are paying attention to the road. It loses track of the road, does impossible math, and the punishment lands on the driver: a steering wheel that suddenly weighs as much as a steering wheel did in 1974, at speed, on a straight road, with the dashboard lit up like a slot machine. One owner of a 2025 Pilot with 5,700 miles on it told NHTSA the steering failed at 25 to 30 mph and every warning on the dash fired at once.[4] Another, in June 2025, reported the wheel becoming "entirely unresponsive" before a restart brought the assist back, temporarily.[5] Complaints are unverified, but the failure they describe matches the filing's signature exactly: straight road, sudden heaviness, lights everywhere.

Then there is the fuse. Honda received the first report on November 29, 2021. For two years, from August 2023 to August 2025, investigators examined returned parts and found nothing, because the error codes had been deleted by the dealer or the supplier before anyone looked. The filing reports the deletion without saying how many codes were deleted or why. It took a supplier market-return analysis in August 2025 to identify the failure, dashcam footage in December 2025 to recreate it, and February 2026 to confirm it only happens driving straight. Defect determination came October 22, 2026: 1,788 days from first report to recall, with 146 warranty claims, one injury and zero deaths in between.[1] Two years of that delay were spent staring at parts whose evidence had already been erased.

Zoom out and the pattern is worse than the incident. This is Honda steering failing in its third different way in three years: a mechanical worm gear that swelled and stuck (recall 24V-744, about 1.69 million vehicles, October 2024), a calibration error that tripped the same failsafe on the 2025 RDX (recall 25V582, 17,334 vehicles), and now a divide-by-zero that trips it on 635,808 more.[6] Same three nameplates, the Pilot, MDX and TLX, also carried a January 2025 software recall for an engine-control error that could stall the car outright. Steering used to be a mechanical system with software assist. It is now a software system with a mechanical fallback, and the software keeps finding new ways to quit. Every one of the 635,808 carries the bug; only a tiny fraction will ever hit the exact conditions. That is not reassuring. That is a lottery where the prize is manual steering at highway speed.

The counterargument, at full strength: one injury and zero deaths across 635,808 vehicles and four and a half years is, by any honest measure, a rare event. 146 warranty claims is a 0.023% rate, and the divide-by-zero needs specific straight-driving conditions that almost never align. Honda found it, reported it, and the fix is a free reprogram. Disabling assist on an internal error is also a defensible engineering choice: an EPS system that does not know its own state could apply wrong assist torque at highway speed and steer the car into the next lane, while no assist just makes the wheel heavy. The filing calls the response "improperly configured" but never says what the correct response should have been. And the deleted error codes may be routine, dealers clear codes during diagnosis as a matter of course, not a cover-up. Nothing here proves malice, and the body count proves the risk is small.

What this does not prove: the Part 573 filing was read in full, but NHTSA complaints are unverified owner accounts and are not tied to this recall's fault by the agency. Honda has not disclosed how many of the 146 warranty claims are confirmed instances of this defect. The filing says the affected models are "no longer in production," yet Honda sells a 2026 Pilot, and the filing does not say whether that model carries the corrected software. Do not claim either way.

What This Means for You

If you own a 2023–2025 Pilot, a 2022–2024 MDX or a 2021–2025 TLX, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls starting November 11, 2026. Do not wait for the letter, which is scheduled for December 14.[2] The signature symptom is steering that suddenly goes heavy while driving straight, with EPS warning lights on the dash. The car is still steerable, just much harder, so pull over when it is safe, restart (owners report assist can return temporarily), and call the dealer. The fix is a free reprogram of the EPS control unit: no parts, no cost, no reason to delay. Shopping a used Pilot or MDX from these years? Verify recall 26V-721 is complete before signing, because the population includes vehicles built as recently as December 2025.
