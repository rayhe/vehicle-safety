## News peg
NHTSA's final 2024 overview (DOT HS 813 791, April 2026), Table 9: state-by-state
fatalities and alcohol-impaired fatalities, 2023 vs 2024. Cross-checked against
Fatal Crash Data's independent ranking of the same FARS 2024 Annual Report File

map that moved in both directions at once.

## Verified facts (all from Table 9 / Fatal Crash Data)
- National: 39,254 deaths in 2024, down 1,771 (-4.3%) from 41,025 in 2023.
  Alcohol-impaired deaths 11,904 (30% of total), down 478 (-3.9%).
- 33 states + Puerto Rico down; 17 states + DC up.
- Largest absolute drops: California -293 (-7.0%), Florida -237 (-7.0%),
  Georgia -207 (-13%), Texas -134 (-3.1%), Tennessee -126 (-9.5%),
  Kentucky -107 (-13%).
- Largest absolute rises: Minnesota +75 (+19%), New Jersey +65 (+11%),
  Maine +42 (+31%), North Carolina +42 (+2.7%), Nebraska +24 (+11%),
  Massachusetts +21 (+6.1%).
- Largest percentage moves: Maine +31% (135 to 177); Rhode Island -27%
  (71 to 52); Wyoming -26% (144 to 107); North Dakota -15% (106 to 90).
- Alcohol: 19 states saw alcohol-impaired deaths rise. Largest: Oklahoma +48
  (+27%, 177 to 225). Largest fall: Georgia -102 (-24%, 427 to 325).
- Texas alcohol deaths exactly flat: 1,676 to 1,676 (0.0%) while total fell -134.
- Highest alcohol shares of state deaths, 2024: Vermont 41% (24 of 59),
  Texas 40% (1,676 of 4,160), South Carolina 40% (417 of 1,038).
- Per-capita rates (Fatal Crash Data): Mississippi 25.6/100k (highest),
  New Mexico 19.2, Arkansas 19.5, South Carolina 18.9, Alabama 18.7;
  lowest Rhode Island 4.7, Massachusetts 5.1, New York 5.5. National 11.5.
- Minnesota's rate is only 8.2/100k despite the +19% jump; New Jersey 7.1
  despite +11%. Big percentage moves in low-rate states are small-body
  swings, not new danger zones.

## Original calculations (novel contribution — computed here, not quoted)
1. **The divergence arithmetic.** Risers added +356 deaths combined
   (AK +9, AR +7, CT +2, DC +3, HI +9, ME +42, MA +21, MI +2, MN +75,
   MS +20, NE +24, NV +11, NH +6, NJ +65, NC +42, SD +6, VA +3, WI +9).
   Decliners removed 2,127. Net: -1,771. The national decline happened
   *despite* 356 extra deaths in the worsening states, not because every
   state improved.
2. **The eight-state alcohol paradox.** In 8 states total deaths fell while
   alcohol-impaired deaths rose: Alabama (+3), Idaho (+10), Kansas (+9),
   Montana (+4), Oklahoma (+48), Utah (+10), Vermont (+7), West Virginia
   (+19). Combined alcohol increase: **+110 deaths** in states that got
   safer overall. Their non-alcohol improvement was larger than the headline
   suggests, and it was entirely offset on the alcohol side.
3. **Oklahoma's share flip.** Oklahoma total deaths fell 711 to 645 (-9.3%)
   while alcohol deaths rose 177 to 225 (+27%). Alcohol share of Oklahoma
   deaths jumped 25% to 35% in a single year. One in three Oklahoma road
   deaths is now alcohol-impaired, up from one in four.
4. **California's flat alcohol line.** California cut 293 deaths (-7.0%) but
   alcohol deaths moved only 1,316 to 1,311 (-5). California's improvement
   was entirely a non-alcohol story; its alcohol share actually rose 32%
   to 34%.
5. **The rate-vs-count gap.** Mississippi has the highest per-capita death
   rate (25.6/100k, 5.4x Rhode Island's 4.7) while Texas has the most bodies
   (4,160). A Mississippian is roughly twice as likely to die on the road as
   the average American (11.5/100k) and more than five times as likely as a
   Rhode Islander. The three biggest states hold 28% of deaths but only
   about 27% of the population — the body-count ranking mostly tracks
   population; the rate ranking tracks risk.

## The paradox / angle
The national number is a weighted average of two opposite maps. A third of the
country got deadlier in the same year the country got safer, and in eight
states the improvement and the alcohol problem moved in opposite directions.
The headline "deaths fell 4.3%" is true for the country and false as a
description of what happened in any given state. Policy lives at the state
level — speed limits, DUI enforcement, alcohol taxes, licensing — so the state
map, not the national average, is where the story is.

## Limitations (honest)
- 2024 figures are the FARS Annual Report File, subject to revision when the
  final file publishes (2023 was revised 40,901 to 41,025 between ARF and
  final). Small-state swings (Maine +42, Vermont alcohol +7) are noisy:
  a dozen crashes either way flips the percentage.
- Alcohol-impaired counts are NHTSA estimates (BACs imputed where test
  results are unknown), not lab-confirmed counts.
- Per-capita rates use resident population, not miles driven or exposure;
  states with heavy through-traffic (e.g., Wyoming interstates) look worse
  than their residents' risk alone would suggest.
- One year of state movement is not a trend. Minnesota's +19% follows years
  of declines; whether it persists is unknowable from this table alone.

## Strongest counterargument
"Thirty-three states improved and the national total fell by 1,771. The map
split is mostly noise: small states swing on a handful of crashes, and the
risers added only 356 deaths against 2,127 removed. Celebrate the decline;
don't manufacture a crisis out of Maine's 42 extra deaths." Fair on the
arithmetic — the decliners dominate. But the counterargument concedes the
point that matters: the improvement is not uniform, and the states that got
worse got worse for specific, fixable reasons (Minnesota +75, New Jersey
+65, Maine +31%). A national average that hides 356 extra deaths in 18
jurisdictions is a bad dashboard for the people who set speed limits and
DUI policy state by state. And the alcohol paradox in eight states is not
noise: +110 alcohol deaths in states whose totals fell is a signal that
the thing killing people is changing even where the total is improving.

## Actionable insights (gate)
- If you live in a riser state (MN, NJ, ME, NC, NE, MA): the national
  "roads are safer" story does not apply to you. Ask your state highway
  safety office what changed in 2024 — the NHTSA table is public.
- If you live in Oklahoma, Idaho, Kansas, Montana, Utah, Vermont, or West
  Virginia: your state got safer overall while drunk driving got worse.
  That is an enforcement and alcohol-policy story, not a road-design story.
  Support ignition-interlock expansion and DUI enforcement funding; the
  data says that is where your state's remaining deaths live.
- Everyone: check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls. And
  remember the rate ranking, not the body-count ranking, is your risk:
  Mississippi 25.6 vs Rhode Island 4.7 per 100k is a 5.4x gap in the same
  country, same year, same federal safety standards.
