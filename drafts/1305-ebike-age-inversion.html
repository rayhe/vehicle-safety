The Gap

Kids Fill the ERs. Older Riders Fill the Morgues. Same Machine.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 28, 2026

An e-bike is a bicycle the way a moped is a bicycle: legally, yes; physically, not really. Federal injury data, analyzed in a 2026 report built from the Consumer Product Safety Commission's injury databases, puts estimated 2025 e-bike emergency room visits at 102,777, up 71% in a single year and 19 times the 2020 figure. That number alone would be a story. Inside it sits a stranger one, because the people filling emergency rooms and the people dying on these machines are barely the same population.

Start with the kids, because the curve there is not a slope, it is a wall. Injuries to riders 14 and under jumped from an estimated 6,444 in 2024 to 17,206 in 2025, a 167% rise in twelve months, and kids' share of all e-bike injuries tripled since 2020, from 5.2% to 16.7%. Riders aged 15 to 24 are the largest single group, and more than half of everyone injured was 34 or younger. One hospital study presented to pediatricians in October 2026 found e-bike injuries in children were about three times as likely to need surgery or an inpatient stay as e-scooter injuries. Nobody should be surprised that a heavy machine capable of 28 mph, sold without training, licensing, or any real age gate, hurts children more than a pedal bike does. Surprise is not the point. Scale is.

Now flip to the deaths, where the pattern inverts completely. CPSC recorded zero e-bike deaths in 2017 and 97 in 2024, 310 across the eight-year span, and e-bikes now account for 58% of all micromobility deaths, ahead of e-scooters at 39%. Among riders 65 and older who died on micromobility devices, 82% were on e-bikes, and adding the 45 to 64 bracket puts older adults over half of all e-bike fatalities. More than half of those deaths, 170 of 310, were collisions with motor vehicles. Youth crashes are a volume story: lots of new, young, fast riders learning limits in traffic. Older-rider deaths are a fragility story: slower reflexes, more fragile bones, same speed, same traffic, far less margin when it goes wrong.

Severity is climbing for everyone, which is what makes the youth surge harder to dismiss as mere popularity. In 2025, 17.6% of e-bike injuries led to hospitalization, transfer, or observation, up every year from 10.1% in 2020 and higher than e-scooters (15.1%) or regular bicycles (14.1%). Fractures were the top diagnosis at 29%. Head injuries numbered 19,001 and face injuries 10,770, so 29% of all injuries hit above the neck, while CPSC's follow-up study found only 40% of injured riders wore a helmet. A machine that weighs twice what a pedal bike weighs, stops longer, and arrives at intersections faster than drivers expect does not forgive the missing helmet the way a 12 mph fall does.

What to do with this, concretely. Parents: the American Academy of Pediatrics says no e-bikes under 12 and Class 1 only from 12 to 15, and a Class 3 bike doing 28 mph is not a child's bicycle whatever the box calls it. Check what class you actually bought, and check whether it has been modified past it, because modified bikes can top 50 mph while still wearing bicycle legal status. Every rider: wear a helmet, since head and face injuries are nearly a third of the total and the helmet numbers are embarrassing. Older riders: practice braking at full speed in an empty lot before traffic teaches the lesson, because the e-bike that gives mobility back is the same one that punishes a slow stop. Buyers: charge only with the supplied charger and never charge a damaged battery indoors, after 19 battery-fire deaths in the CPSC series. And cities: a vehicle class that went from zero recorded deaths to nearly 100 a year in seven years, with no training requirement attached, has outrun its rules, and the injury curve will keep score until the rules catch up.
