Sobriety Report

The Deadliest Car in America Has the Soberest Drivers. Blame the Car.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026

Five stars. That is what the federal government gave the Hyundai Veloster. Ten years of federal fatality data give it 8.54 occupant deaths per 100 million miles, the highest rate of any nameplate in the FARS 2014–2023 dataset, 24 times the Chevrolet Equinox. I run the toxicology desk. According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, only 17.4 percent of Veloster drivers in fatal crashes tested positive for alcohol or drugs, against a fleet average of 20.0 percent. America’s deadliest car is driven by people more sober than average. So no, it is not the drivers. It is the car.[1][2]

Veloster occupant deaths per 100M miles, FARS 2014–2023; highest of 337 nameplates

It gets worse. Run the same cross-tab on the rest of the death-rate leaderboard and the pattern holds. Second up: the Chevrolet Tracker, 7.83 per 100M miles, second deadliest, 12.7 percent impaired, the third-lowest impairment rate among every model with 300-plus toxicology reports in the dataset. Third: the Toyota Land Cruiser, 6.27 per 100M miles, 8.9 percent impaired, the second-lowest. America’s three deadliest cars per mile all come with drivers sober than the national average. Two of them are among the four soberest-driving nameplates in the country. You want to blame the nut behind the wheel. Lab work says the nut is fine and the wheel is attached to 2,800 pounds of tin.[1]

One honorable mention for the stereotype, so nobody accuses me of going soft. Then the Mustang: 6.02 per 100M miles, 2,739 dead, 21.9 percent of its drivers impaired, comfortably above average. It earned its reputation the honest way, with tequila. Veloster numbers came from a different source: physics.[1]

Physics, then. A Veloster weighs under 3,000 pounds. An F-150, fatality rate 1.04, weighs roughly 4,600. In a two-vehicle crash, the heavy vehicle shoves the light one backward, and the light one’s occupants absorb the difference. IIHS has published this for years: smaller, lighter vehicles carry a persistent fatality penalty against heavier ones, all else equal. The Veloster is also small in exactly the wrong test. NHTSA’s five-star overall score comes from full-width frontal and side barrier tests. Then the test that matters: the IIHS small-overlap, which simulates clipping an oncoming car or a pole with the front corner, which is how real people really crash, and graded the Veloster Marginal. Side impact: Acceptable. It aced the test where the crash is perfectly symmetrical and flunked the one where it is not. Guess which kind shows up in FARS.[2][3][4]

Then the equipment list, or the absence of it. On most Veloster trims, blind-spot monitoring and forward-collision warning were simply not available, not even as options. Those are the two cheapest technologies in the industry for preventing the exact crashes a 2,800-pound car cannot afford to have. Hyundai was selling a sporty-looking coupe to young buyers on the used market while withholding the sensors that keep sporty-looking coupes out of ditches. It also accumulated 18 NHTSA recall campaigns across the 2012–2021 model years, 1,285 owner complaints, including engine and ABS-module fire issues. None of that kills you at 8.54 per 100M miles. But it tells you what kind of engineering budget was behind the badge.[3][5]

Now the asterisk, because I keep score on myself too. The Veloster’s fleet is genuinely old: 39 percent of its deaths are pre-2010 model years, 75 percent pre-2015, versus 21 and 46 percent for the Equinox. Older cars are deadlier cars, and part of this column’s gap is just that. But age does not stretch 0.36 to 8.54 on its own. The Mustang, with the drunk-driving stereotype fully intact, tops out at 6.02. The Tracker and the Land Cruiser carry old fleets too, and they pair them with the two lowest impairment rates in the dataset. Age is a real confounder here. It is not the whole story. Also: FARS toxicology is post-crash testing, and states test unevenly. Alcohol gets tested far more consistently than drugs. Treat the 17.4 percent as a floor with error bars, and the 8.54 rate uses estimated vehicle miles, not odometer readings, so treat the decimals as fuzzy. Nothing fuzzy about the ranking: nothing else in 337 nameplates touches 8.54.[1]

What to do: Hyundai killed the Veloster in 2022, so this is a used-market warning. If you are shopping one, understand the five-star sticker is not a real-world death-rate claim; read the small-overlap Marginal instead. If you already own one, drive it like the crumple zone is smaller than you think, because it is, and like the forward-collision warning that would have helped does not exist, because on your trim it probably does not. Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls: 18 campaigns is a lot of paperwork to be behind on. And the general rule this column keeps rediscovering: when the toxicology comes back clean and the body count stays high, the problem is bolted to the car.[2]
