IIHS Put a Dummy in the Back Seat. 147 Cars Lost Their Good Rating.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 22, 2026
147
Vehicles that held a Good rating under IIHS's original moderate overlap test and dropped to Poor or Marginal once a rear-seat dummy was added. The cars changed not at all.

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. ForCar's Denis Kataev compiled every vehicle IIHS rated under both versions of the moderate overlap front test, and the table reads like a grading scandal: 227 vehicles earned a Good under the original test, 58 kept it under the updated one, and 147 fell to Poor or Marginal. Nobody recalled anything and nobody redesigned anything. What changed is the question: what happens to the person sitting behind the driver? Ask that, and two-thirds of the industry's top scores evaporate.

To understand why the old test stopped working, start in 1995. IIHS launched the moderate overlap evaluation that year: 40 percent of the car's front width strikes a barrier at 40 mph, with one Hybrid III dummy the size of an average man in the driver seat. It was brutal at first, and in the institute's own words, "the majority of vehicles were rated poor or marginal" when it debuted. Then three decades of engineering happened and the test became a participation trophy. IIHS again: "Today, all vehicles earn good ratings in that original evaluation." When everybody gets an A, the A measures nothing.

In 2022 the institute changed the question: same crash, same barrier, same speed, but now a second dummy rides along, a smaller Hybrid III sized like a small woman or a 12-year-old, belted into the second row behind the driver, plus new metrics aimed at the injuries rear occupants actually suffer, including chest force on the torso, whether the head stays clear of the front seatback, and the big one, submarining, which is the industry's polite word for the dummy sliding forward underneath the lap belt so the belt loads the soft abdomen instead of the pelvis. Submarine in a real crash and you are looking at internal injuries from the device that was supposed to save you.

Here is the part that should bother you at a gut level. IIHS made this change because its researchers found that in 2007-and-newer vehicles, a belted rear occupant now faces a higher risk of fatal injury than a belted front occupant, so read that twice: nothing about the back seat got more dangerous while the front seat got dramatically safer, with load-limiting belts that pay out under extreme force, pretensioners that yank the slack out before the crash fully develops, and airbags shaped around specific bodies, almost none of which ever migrated rearward. Your back seat belts are, in a depressing number of cars, the same dumb three-point webbing the industry was installing when the test launched in 1995. IIHS President David Harkey called the updated test "a challenge to manufacturers to bring those same benefits to the back seat," which is executive-speak for: you forgot a whole row of seats for thirty years.

The brand breakdown is where my eyebrows went up. Among brands with at least fifteen vehicles rated on the updated test, the Poor-or-Marginal counts run: Jeep 26 of 32, Chevrolet 22 of 30, Ford 23 of 40, Subaru 21 of 34. Expected, maybe. But then Toyota at 37 of 53 and Lexus at 12 of 15, the brands people buy specifically because they trust the safety reputation, failing this test at rates that look like everyone else's. This is not a cheap-versus-expensive problem but a front-versus-rear one, and almost nobody had solved it, because until 2022 nobody was asking.

Now the audit, because I do not trust a table that does not add up: 58 plus 147 is 205, but the census says 227, which leaves 22 vehicles unaccounted for in a table with no row for them. My money says they rated Acceptable under the updated test, the grade between Marginal and Good that the table simply omitted, though I cannot confirm that from the published table, so I am telling you instead of pretending. If you are going to cite this census at the dealership, cite the uncertainty too.

There is a genuinely encouraging trend buried in the wreckage, and I would be a bad analyst if I skipped it. By model year, the share of vehicles failing the updated test runs 77 percent for 2022 models, 70 percent for 2023, 49 percent for 2024, 40 percent for 2025, and 31 percent for 2026, a collapse from more than three-quarters to under a third in four model years, mostly because the fix is hardware the front seat has had since the nineties: rear load limiters and pretensioners, which automakers are bolting in as fast as redesign cycles allow. The September 2026 test round just put the 2027 Kia Telluride and the refreshed Tesla Model Y on the Top Safety Pick+ list with Good ratings across the board. The system is working; it just needed someone to move the goalposts first.

Counterpoint, stated at full strength, because a re-grading is not a safety decline and I refuse to let this column imply otherwise: no car in that 147 got more dangerous, a 2021 that lost its Good is exactly as safe as the day it earned it, and it would be dishonest to tell its owner otherwise. IIHS moves goalposts on purpose, since that is the entire business model, and the 77-to-31 trend is the proof the model works. Older cars should not be stigmatized for failing a test that did not exist when they were designed. The honest headline really is "the test got tougher," full stop. My answer is that the consumer advice survives the objection intact: do not sell your 2021. Just stop assuming its old Good rating says anything about the seat your kid sits in.

What to do with this, concretely: look up the updated moderate overlap rating for your exact model year at iihs.org/ratings, not the original-test rating that may still be floating around in old reviews. If you are choosing between a 2021 and a 2024 of the same model on the used lot, understand that the 2024 may carry rear belt hardware the 2021 was never designed for, a difference invisible in the price, the listing, and the federal star rating. And keep the kids in the back, always. This finding is about belt hardware measured against adult-sized dummies, and the front airbag remains a far worse threat to a child than a 1995-spec rear belt. The back seat is still the right place. It just deserves better belts.
