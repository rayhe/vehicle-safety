# Research: Low-Speed Zones Are Where Big Vehicles Kill Pedestrians Most

## Angle
At low speeds (15-25 mph) — school zones, residential streets, parking lots — pickups and large SUVs are 50-80% more likely to kill a pedestrian than a car. At high speeds, the difference shrinks because everyone dies regardless. The places with the lowest speed limits are exactly where vehicle design matters most, and where children are most likely to be walking.

## Self-Critique Gate
**Is this genuinely surprising after 700+ articles?** YES — we've covered hood height (IIHS Nov 2023), vehicle weight/size generally, and pedestrian fatality trends. But we have NOT covered the specific finding that the vehicle-type lethality gap is LARGEST at LOW speeds and SMALLEST at high speeds. This inverts the intuition: you'd expect bigger = more dangerous at highway speeds. The opposite is true. The gap is widest where speed limits are lowest.

## Primary Sources

### 1. Parajuli et al. (Feb 2026) — SSRN Paper
- "Quantifying the Threat of Bigger and Heavier Vehicles in US Pedestrian Crashes"
- Tennessee police crash data 2009-2024, plus VIN decoder + Canadian Vehicle Specs datasets
- Generalized ordinal logit models
- Key findings:
  - In low-speed settings, pickups and large SUVs associated with **50-80% higher fatal outcome odds** vs passenger cars
  - At higher speeds where most fatalities occur, odds are only **marginally higher**
  - Vehicle characteristics alone cannot explain the nearly threefold rise in pedestrian fatalities in Tennessee
  - Aging vehicle fleet + electrified large vehicles may lead to higher pedestrian injury outcomes in low-speed settings
- URL: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6143966

### 2. IIHS (Dec 2024) — Speed × Height Compound Study
- "Vehicle height compounds dangers of speed for pedestrians"
- Key data:
  - At 25 mph: median car = 9% serious injury risk, median pickup (13" taller front end) = 62% serious injury risk (7x difference)
  - As crash speed increases 15→35 mph: car serious injury goes 9%→52%; pickup goes 11%→91%
  - The speed-height interaction is multiplicative, not additive
  - Pedestrian injury risk curves shifted left for US pickups vs German vehicles (taller US fleet = more dangerous at same speeds)
- URL: https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/vehicle-height-compounds-dangers-of-speed-for-pedestrians

### 3. IIHS (Nov 2023) — Hood Height Study
- "Vehicles with higher, more vertical front ends pose greater risk to pedestrians"
- 18,000 pedestrian crashes analyzed
- Hood height >40 inches: 45% more likely to cause pedestrian fatality
- Average US vehicle: 4" wider, 10" longer, 8" taller, 1,000 lbs heavier than 30 years ago
- Some large pickups have hoods at eye level for many adults
- URL: https://www.iihs.org/topics/fatality-statistics

### 4. NHTSA Pedestrian Data
- Pedestrian deaths rose 80% from 2009 low to 2021 peak (7,342 killed — highest since 1981)
- Pedestrian deaths account for 17% of all traffic deaths
- 57% increase 2013-2022 (4,779 → 7,522)
- SUVs and pickup trucks are responsible for disproportionate share of pedestrian injuries and fatalities — 2-3x more likely to kill than passenger cars (Lefler & Gabler 2004, Roudsari et al. 2004)
- URL: https://www.nhtsa.gov/book/countermeasures-that-work/pedestrian-safety/understanding-problem

### 5. NHTSA Proposed Pedestrian Safety Rule (Sep 2024)
- First-ever federal rule for pedestrian head injury performance testing
- Would cover all vehicles ≤10,000 lbs
- Estimated to save 67 lives per year (NHTSA's own estimate)
- Human-like head dummies simulating children and adults for hood-impact testing
- URL: https://www.nhtsa.gov (rule, Federal Register)

## Novel Contribution
Cross-tabulation of Tennessee study (speed-differentiated lethality) with IIHS compound data (height × speed interaction) + FARS pedestrian trends:
- **The calculation nobody has done:** In a 25 mph school zone, a median-height pickup creates 7x the serious injury risk of a car (IIHS data). At that same speed, pickups are 50-80% more fatal than cars (Tennessee data). Yet school zones are specifically designed with low speed limits because we assume low speeds = safe. The vehicle fleet has outrun the infrastructure assumption.
- **The EV wrinkle:** Tennessee study flags that electrified large vehicles (heavier due to batteries, but silent approach) may worsen low-speed pedestrian outcomes. EV pickups (F-150 Lightning, Rivian R1T, Cybertruck) are 1,000-2,000 lbs heavier than their ICE equivalents.

## Actionable Insights
1. If you're buying a vehicle and live near a school or residential area with pedestrian traffic, consider that a sedan or smaller crossover is dramatically safer for pedestrians at the speeds you'll actually drive in your neighborhood
2. Vehicle hood height matters more than speed limit at low speeds — a tall vehicle at 25 mph is more dangerous than a sedan at 35 mph for pedestrians
3. NHTSA's proposed pedestrian head-impact rule is the first federal attempt to address this, but saves only 67 lives/year — a fraction of the ~7,500 annual pedestrian death toll
4. Check if your neighborhood or school zone has traffic calming infrastructure — speed bumps, curb extensions, raised crosswalks reduce actual speeds below posted limits

## Limitations
- Tennessee study uses state-level crash data, not national; regional variation in fleet composition and pedestrian infrastructure
- IIHS compound data uses simulated crash scenarios, not real-world; actual pedestrian crashes involve variable impact geometry
- FARS data captures only fatal crashes — the injury multiplier at low speeds may be much larger
- The EV weight factor is still being studied; few EVs in the Tennessee 2009-2024 dataset
- NHTSA proposed rule timeline uncertain under current administration

## Strongest Counterargument
Large vehicles protect their OCCUPANTS better in crashes with other vehicles. The occupant fatality rate for full-size trucks and SUVs is lower than for sedans. Families buy these vehicles BECAUSE they want to survive crashes. Punishing owners for pedestrian risk that's also driven by infrastructure design (no sidewalks, wide roads, poor lighting) shifts blame from urban planning to vehicle choice.
