NHTSA Pushed the Auto-Braking Mandate to 2031. No Car on the Road Can Pass the Test.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

On Wednesday, NHTSA filed notice that the most important car safety rule in twenty years will wait two more years.[1] A notice of proposed rulemaking, published in the Federal Register today, moves the automatic emergency braking mandate from September 1, 2029 to September 1, 2031, with small-volume manufacturers pushed to September 1, 2032.[1] When NHTSA finalized the rule in May 2024, it said the standard would save at least 360 lives a year and prevent at least 24,000 injuries annually.[2] Two years of delay is two years of that benefit deferred, and the reason for the delay is the part nobody in the press release wanted to lead with: NHTSA tested ten brand-new 2025 cars against the standard, and not one of them passed every trial.

Zero out of ten. Read that again, because the number does all the work in this story. NHTSA ran its preliminary New Car Assessment Program evaluations across a Chevrolet Equinox, a Ford Expedition, a Hyundai Santa Fe, a Jeep Grand Cherokee, a Kia K4, a Nissan Rogue, a Subaru Forester, a Toyota 4Runner, a Volkswagen Tiguan, and a Volvo XC40, ten current production vehicles spanning the affordable end of the market to the premium end, and no single model avoided collision across all mandated trials.[1] That is not one laggard dragging down an average. That is the entire tested fleet failing the same exam, which is the strongest fact in NHTSA’s favor and the most damning fact in the rule’s history, depending on which side of the desk you sit on.

Congress ordered this standard in November 2021, in Section 24208 of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, and NHTSA finalized it as FMVSS 127 on May 9, 2024.[1] From mandate to metal, the first compliant model year is now 2031, which is ten years from a congressional order to the first car that must obey it, and the rule gave automakers five years and four months of lead time when it was written, a window the delay stretches to seven years and four months.[1] Roughly 32 million new light vehicles will roll off American lines across the two delayed model years, at the recent pace of about 16 million a year, and each one will carry the older, voluntary version of automatic braking instead of the mandated one.[8] Those cars stay on the road an average of nearly thirteen years, which means the delay does not expire in 2031. It keeps driving.[9]

Price the delay in the agency’s own numbers and it comes out ugly. NHTSA’s 2024 regulatory analysis put the rule’s benefit at 362 lives a year at full effectiveness, and the press release rounded it to at least 360.[2][3] Two years of deferred compliance is two years of that benefit deferred, which is 724 lives on the agency’s own math before any discounting, and I am labeling it an upper bound because the honest version requires it: the 362 figure is a steady-state estimate once the whole fleet complies, and real benefits ramp over a decade of fleet turnover, so the true forgone benefit inside the 2029 to 2031 window is smaller than 724.[3] Smaller is not zero, and the direction is not ambiguous. Every year the mandate waits is a year the 362-a-year rule does not pay out, and the cars sold in the meantime are the ones that will still be on the road when the mandate finally arrives.

What the rule actually demands explains why nobody can pass it yet. A compliant car must stop and avoid contact with a vehicle ahead at up to 62 miles per hour, apply the brakes automatically at up to 90 miles per hour when a collision is imminent, and detect pedestrians in daylight and in darkness, braking for them at up to 45 miles per hour.[2] Most cars on the road today have some version of automatic braking, and that is the part people confuse with the mandate, because the 2016 voluntary agreement put basic systems in nearly everything. Basic is not the standard. Night pedestrian detection is the standard, and roughly 77 percent of front-impact pedestrian fatalities happen in darkness, which makes the hardest requirement the most valuable one and the delay the most expensive where it matters most.[1]

Now the other side, at full strength, because it deserves it. NHTSA is right to delay, and the zero-for-ten result proves it. A standard no production car can meet is not a safety rule. It is a ban on selling cars, or worse, a mandate for phantom braking, where cars slam on the brakes for shadows and overpasses and cause the rear-end crashes the rule exists to prevent. When the Alliance for Automotive Innovation sued in January 2025, its argument was technical, not just commercial: a full stop from 62 miles per hour with no contact sits at the edge of what radar and vision can reliably do, and a false positive at highway speed is itself a crash risk.[4] NHTSA said it plainly in the notice, warning that overly aggressive, technology-forcing standards can lead to consumer rejection or even negative safety consequences.[1] Two years of engineering buys systems that actually work instead of systems that technically comply on a test track and misbehave on the road, and the 362-lives figure assumes the systems work as specified. If they do not, the rule saves nobody and the delay costs nobody.

Both sides are pricing different risks, and the data to settle it does not exist yet, which is the part that should bother you. NHTSA’s case rests on preliminary testing it has not published in full, and the Alliance’s case rests on a lawsuit that has sat in abeyance since February 2025 while the Department told the court in March that a rulemaking was coming.[4] Meanwhile the 2016 voluntary agreement keeps doing the light work, basic braking at city speeds, while the hard work, highway-speed avoidance and night pedestrian detection, waits for a rulemaking that is still only a proposal. This notice opens a 60-day comment period, and the September 2031 date is proposed, not locked, which means the decade from mandate to metal could still stretch further.

What to do with this, practically, starts at the dealership, because if you are buying a car in 2026, 2027, or 2028, do not assume the letters AEB on the window sticker mean FMVSS 127 AEB. Ask specifically whether the car avoids a stopped vehicle at highway speed and detects pedestrians at night, the two tests no 2025 model passed across the board, and check the IIHS front crash prevention ratings, including pedestrian, which are the best public proxy until the mandate lands.[6] If you already own a 2025 or earlier car, your braking system is almost certainly the voluntary-agreement version, which helps at city speeds and is not rated to stop from 62 miles per hour. And if you have a view on whether the delay is prudence or surrender, the comment period on Docket NHTSA-2026-2476 is open now through Regulations.gov, closing in late January 2027. It will not ask for your opinion. It will ask for data, which is the one thing this fight is short on.
