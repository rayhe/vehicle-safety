The Gap

The Deadliest Vehicle in America Is Safest for the Person Driving It

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

Every three years or so, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety publishes its driver death rates, a body count with denominators. This year's edition, released July 30, carried a second number for only the second time: the "other-driver death rate," counting the drivers in OTHER cars who die when they crash into the vehicle in question. Across the whole fleet, the average car kills 53 strangers for every 37 of its own drivers per million registered vehicle years. Read that again. On average, your car is 43 percent deadlier to everyone else than it is to you.[1]

204
Other-driver deaths per million registered vehicle years for the Ram 3500 crew cab long bed 4WD, the highest IIHS has ever measured

Alone at the top, the Ram 3500 crew cab long bed four-wheel-drive sits at 204, which is nearly four times the fleet average. Behind it: the Dodge Charger HEMI at 161, the regular Charger at 136, the Chevrolet Silverado 3500 at 126, and a parade of Ram 1500s and 2500s between 100 and 124. Ten of the twenty vehicles with the highest other-driver death rates are large or very large pickups. IIHS president David Harkey blamed size and power, and then said the quiet part about how these trucks earn a living: "Even the safest small cars don't fare well in crashes with large pickups and heavy SUVs."[1][3]

But the Chargers tell the darker story. Run their two numbers side by side: the HEMI version posts 127 driver deaths alongside its 161 other-driver deaths; the base Charger posts 90 and 136. Both high lists also hold the Chevrolet Camaro (88 and 87) and Malibu (116 and 91). These are the only vehicles on the report that are dangerous in both directions, and the explanation Harkey offered for muscle cars is wonderfully blunt: "Powerful engines and marketing that attracts aggressive drivers is the issue. They're dangerous mainly because of how they're driven." A heavy-duty pickup kills you by existing near you. A Charger kills you by being driven the way the commercials suggest.[1]

Now run the ratio, because the ratio is the whole story. Divide other-driver deaths by driver deaths and you get what I'll call the threat ratio. Minicars: 55 over 158, a ratio of 0.35, meaning they are killed nearly three times as often as they kill. Very large pickups: 110 over 24, a ratio of 4.6. That 24 is worth pausing on. Very large pickups are the safest vehicle class in the report for their OWN drivers. Safest for you, deadliest for everyone else, by a factor of almost five.[1]

The single most lopsided nameplate in the entire report is the Ford F-150 SuperCrew 2WD: 7 driver deaths, 99 other-driver deaths, a threat ratio of 14 to 1. This truck is a fortress from the inside and a battering ram from the outside, and the five-star rating on the window sticker measures only the fortress. Nothing on the lot, and nothing in NHTSA's rating program, grades the battering ram. America has never had a crash-compatibility standard for how one vehicle treats the occupants of another; the IIHS table is the closest thing, and it is not a standard. It is a spreadsheet of consequences.[1]

Scan the bottom of both lists instead: the BMW X5 and X7 four-wheel-drives, the Porsche Cayenne, the Volkswagen Atlas 4WD, the Volvo XC60 and XC90 all appear among the lowest rates for drivers AND for other drivers. Consider the Toyota Tundra CrewMax: a full-size pickup that recorded zero driver deaths. Size alone does not do this. Engineering, buyer demographics, and how the vehicle gets used do most of the work, and IIHS says so itself: its rates adjust for driver age and sex but not for speed, daily mileage, or habits, which is why HD pickups that tow and haul commercial miles on rural roads carry built-in disadvantages.[1][2]

So what do you do with this? If you own a small car, your biggest asymmetric threat is not another small car; it is the large pickup, and the minicar numbers prove the danger flows one way. If you are shopping, look up the other-driver column for your short list at the IIHS table, because the lab crash rating and the death rate measure different things, and neither of them is the number that tells you what your car does to a stranger. If you own one of the fortress trucks, the actionable part is unglamorous: following distance, speed, and sobriety matter more than any bumper, since the 14-to-1 F-150 split says the killing happens in the miles, not the metal. Dealers will keep selling you the five stars. The spreadsheet says there are ten.[1][2]
