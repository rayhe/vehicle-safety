Sobriety Report

55% of American Drivers Have Fallen Asleep at the Wheel. A Third Did It in the First Hour.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

NHTSA finally asked the question nobody wanted answered. In its first national drowsy-driving survey in 24 years, the agency asked 3,765 drivers whether they had ever fallen asleep at the wheel, and fifty-five percent said yes.[1] Apply that share to America's 240 million licensed drivers and the math lands at roughly 132 million people who have nodded off while driving a car.[4] That is not a fringe behavior but the majority of the driving public, confessing to the one impairment with no blood test and no breathalyzer.

132 million
Americans who have fallen asleep at the wheel: 55 percent of 240 million licensed drivers

Respondents averaged 0.23 fall-asleep episodes in the past 30 days, counting the drivers who had never fallen asleep as zeros.[1] Annualize that pace across 240 million licensed drivers and you get roughly 660 million episodes a year, a number so large it reads like a typo until you remember the denominator. Last time NHTSA asked this question, in 2002, only 37 percent said they had ever nodded off or fallen asleep while driving.[6] Eighteen points in 24 years, and the direction is up, not down.

Everything you think you know about when it happens is wrong. A third of the drivers who had fallen asleep did it within the first hour behind the wheel: 16 percent in under 30 minutes, 17 percent between 30 and 59 minutes.[1] Only 15 percent made it past six hours first. Your body does not wait for the road trip to get dangerous. It clocks out on the commute home, twenty minutes in, while you are still telling yourself the coffee is working.

Where it happens is just as uncomfortable: sixty-two percent were on multilane highways with speed limits above 55 mph when they last fell asleep.[1] Do the physics on a four-second microsleep at 65 mph: 381 feet, blind, at highway speed, with no hands on the wheel and no foot on the brake. Midnight to 6 AM was the most common window at 27 percent, but the second most common was 6 PM to midnight at 25 percent.[1] This is not only the 3 AM trucker. It is the evening commute after a long day.

Now the body count: federal crash records list 633 people killed in drowsy-driving crashes in 2023.[5] Independent modeling from the same federal data puts the real number near 6,000 a year, and the Governors Highway Safety Association's estimate for 2023 is 6,326.[3] That is a tenfold gap, and the survey explains it without trying. Alcohol leaves a BAC reading, drugs leave metabolites, and distraction leaves a phone record timestamped to the second. Fatigue leaves nothing, and a dead driver cannot fill out a survey. Police code drowsiness only when someone survives to admit it, which means the official number counts confessions, not crashes.

Before you tattoo 132 million on your arm, the caveats. This is a self-reported survey with a low response rate, and people misremember when they last nodded off. NHTSA fielded it in 2022 and published it in November 2026, so the behavior it measures is four years old. Comparing 55 percent today with 37 percent in 2002 is tempting and not strictly fair: different sampling frames, different survey modes, different eras of willingness to admit it. Some of the 18-point jump may be honesty, not more sleeping. None of that makes the number small, because even if the survey is off by a third, the majority finding survives.

Two findings should embarrass everyone who works in traffic safety. First, drivers who remembered hearing drowsy-driving warnings in the past year reported more drowsy driving, not less. NHTSA's own reading is that the warnings are most memorable to the people already doing the thing, which means the campaign is preaching to the choir while the choir drives home tired. Second, only Arkansas and New Jersey have laws against drowsy driving, and their residents drive drowsy less often, yet more than 80 percent of drivers nationwide do not know whether their state has such a law.[1] A deterrent nobody knows about is not a deterrent but a trivia question.

So what do you do with this: sleep seven hours or more before you drive, because six to seven hours was the most common night of sleep before a fall-asleep episode, and only 7 percent of fall-asleep drivers had slept more than eight.[1] Treat the first hour as the danger zone, not the warm-up: if you feel heavy-eyed twenty minutes in, pull over then, because the data says the risk does not build slowly, it arrives early. If rideshare or a taxi is available, take it after a late night; the survey found availability alone predicts less drowsy driving. And check whether your car watches your eyelids. Driver-monitoring systems are the only technology that addresses a problem with no chemical test, and they are still optional on most cars.
