One Houston Owner Petitioned NHTSA. Now 80,418 Palisades Are Under Federal Review.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 19, 2026

A Houston man took his 2020 Hyundai Palisade to the dealer on June 24, 2026, because the air conditioner blew hot air. Dealer said the compressor and the expansion valve needed replacing, and with his warranty expired, he paid for it himself. Then he did the thing almost nobody does: he petitioned the federal government. On November 16, NHTSA opened Defect Petition DP26013, and 80,418 Palisades are now under federal review because their windshields may not defog.[1][2]

80,418
2020 Hyundai Palisades named in Defect Petition DP26013, opened on the strength of four owner reports. Zero crashes, zero injuries, and still a federal docket.

A windshield defogger is an air conditioner in disguise. Its compressor pulls moisture out of the air before the vents blow it at the glass, which is why the defroster works in winter and why it matters in Houston, where the air is wet enough to drink. Kill the compressor and the defroster still blows, except now it blows humid air at a fogged windshield, which is like trying to dry a towel with a wet one. NHTSA files this under VISIBILITY, not comfort, and the petitioner’s own words make the same point: in high-heat Houston, a performance issue becomes a safety hazard.[1][2]

Hyundai already knew, because technical service bulletin 24-HA-003H, issued in 2024, tells dealers that certain 2020–2025 Palisade vehicles “may experience reduced A/C performance due to an out of specification A/C compressor pulley and hub assembly,” and hands them a procedure to inspect it and replace the compressor or just the pulley. Read that scope again, because it runs six model years while DP26013 names one. NHTSA’s population is 80,418 cars, while Hyundai’s own bulletin covers the same out-of-spec part across every Palisade built from 2020 through 2025. Grant the petition and the investigation follows the bulletin’s scope, not the petition’s, which means the population at issue could be several times what the petition lists. One petition is the tip, and the bulletin is the iceberg.[2]

A defect petition is the citizen’s lever. Under federal rules, any person can ask NHTSA to open a defect investigation, and the agency has to answer: grant or deny. Most petitions get denied, which is why this one is worth watching: four ODI reports, zero crashes, zero injuries, and the agency still opened a docket. Petition landed September 17, docket opened November 16, sixty days from a Houston mailbox to a federal case number. Grant means a preliminary evaluation, then possibly an engineering analysis, then possibly a recall, and a recall means reimbursement for repairs already paid, which is exactly what the petitioner asked for in writing, so keep your receipts.[1][2]

Fairness first, because the other side has a real case. Four complaints across 80,418 vehicles is a 0.005 percent report rate, with zero injuries, and the car still has a heater-based defroster that works without the compressor. Hyundai published a bulletin and a service kit, dealers knew what to do, and the petitioner got his car fixed. A reasonable reader could call this a warranty gripe in safety clothing, filed by an owner who wants his money back. That reader would be wrong about one thing: Hyundai’s own bulletin concedes the pulley is out of specification across six model years, NHTSA’s own code files this under VISIBILITY, and a defogger that cannot dehumidify is a visibility failure, which is the kind of slow, unsexy failure that kills people in rain and humidity without ever producing a dramatic crash report.

What to do: if you own a 2020 Palisade, or a 2021–2025 one since the same bulletin covers your car, and your A/C blows warm or the compressor will not engage, ask the dealer to inspect under TSB 24-HA-003H and get the answer in writing. If your defogger cannot clear the windshield, file a complaint at nhtsa.gov, because ODI complaints are the raw material that opened this petition, and four reports got a federal docket number, so yours could be the fifth.[4]
