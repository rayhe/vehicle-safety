A Jury Priced a Garbage Truck's Underride Guard at $21.9 Million

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 22, 2026

In November 2019, an Acura carrying three 18-year-olds hit the back of a Consolidated Disposal garbage truck in the north valley of Los Angeles. Two of them died, and the third will live with a traumatic brain injury. Seven years later, a Chatsworth jury finished the arithmetic: the truck's rear underride guard was not compliant with the law, and the price was $21.9 million.[1][2]

$21.9M
Los Angeles jury award in Gallegos v. Consolidated Disposal Service, after a two-month trial in which the company and its driver were found 35% at fault.

Across the back of every truck runs a steel bar, the ICC bar, and its only job is to stop a car from sliding underneath in a rear-end crash. Consolidated Disposal, a Republic Services subsidiary, argued it was not required to have a compliant guard at all. Its case, in essence: the standards are so old they cannot protect a modern sedan, so failing them should not count. Jurors heard that argument for two months and rejected it. Their split was precise: 65 percent of the fault on Eugne Oh, the 18-year-old driving the Acura, 20 percent on the company, and 15 percent on the truck driver.[1]

$21.9 million is the headline, and it is not the check. Under California comparative fault, the defendants pay their share: 35 percent of the award, roughly $7.7 million. That breaks down to about $5.25 million of the $15 million awarded to Sebastien Gallegos' parents and about $2.4 million of the $6.9 million awarded to Angel Sanchez, the survivor. A verdict is not a payment, and appeals and post-trial motions can still move the number. But the number the jury wrote down is the one the industry will remember.[1]

Eight days before the verdict was announced, NHTSA recalled MAC liquid-tank trailers for shipping with no rear impact guards at all. Campaign 26V723 covers 2025-2027 trailers that fail FMVSS 223 and 224, the same standards the Gallegos jury just enforced. NHTSA strengthened those standards in 2022. Four years later, trailers are still leaving the factory without the bar, and a hauler just argued in court that the rule is too old to follow. Same story, told twice: once by a regulator, once by a jury.[3][4]

None of this makes the guard the cause of the crash. Jurors themselves said the Acura driver was mostly at fault, 65 percent. A guard is a second-chance device, one that matters only after somebody has already made a mistake, and here the mistake was the teenagers'. Buried in the losing argument was a real point: a standard written for steel dashboards and no airbags is a thin shield for a modern sedan. Their answer: a thin shield is still the law. Both things are true, and the verdict only prices one of them.[1]

You cannot inspect a truck's ICC bar from your lane, but you can see a bent or missing one at a light, and a missing guard on a commercial truck is a reportable defect. File it with the carrier and with FMCSA's National Consumer Complaint Database. If you run a fleet, audit your guards against FMVSS 223 and 224 as written, not as you wish they were written. That defense has now been tried in front of a jury, where it cost $7.7 million and lost.[4]
