## Key data (verified)
- IIHS front crash prevention ratings, n=524 vehicles (all have AEB fitted):
  - Good: 224 (42.7%)
  - Acceptable: 183 (34.9%)
  - Poor: 69 (13.2%)
  - Marginal: 48 (9.2%)
  - **Poor + Marginal = 117/524 = 22.3%** — more than one in five.
- Brand failure counts (rated / Poor-or-Marginal):
  - Jeep 23 / 14 = **60.9%** (2.7x the fleet rate)
  - Chevrolet 28 / 14 = 50.0%
  - Ford 34 / 17 = 50.0%
  - Audi 37 / 13 = 35.1% (premium ≠ protection)
  - Nissan 27 / 9 = 33.3%
  - Volkswagen 19 / 7 = 36.8%
- Model-year trend (share Poor/Marginal): 2024: 41/160 = 25.6%; 2025: 39/182 = 21.4%; 2026: 32/170 = 18.8%. Improving, slowly.
- Hardware driver: single forward camera struggles in low light; camera+radar combos do better. Two model years of the same car can differ more than two brands.
- Old test context (site's May piece): 85% of 2022 MY earned IIHS "Superior"; that test maxed at 25 mph; only 3% of police-reported rear-end crashes happen at those speeds. The current test adds pedestrian targets, darkness, and speed.

## Original contribution (novel analysis)
1. **Mandate-performance gap:** FMVSS 127 requires pedestrian detection "in both daylight and darkness" and avoidance up to 62 mph by Sept 2029. Today, 22% of rated systems cannot pass IIHS's day+night pedestrian evaluation. The federal standard assumes performance the market has not yet delivered.
2. **Jeep outlier:** 60.9% of rated Jeeps fail — 2.7x the fleet failure rate. An entire brand's lineup is functionally blind to pedestrians at night in the majority of its models.
3. **The price irrelevance finding:** Audi (premium) fails at 35%, above the fleet average. What predicts performance is hardware generation (camera-only vs camera+radar), not sticker price.

## Limitations (honest)
- IIHS ratings are controlled lab tests on one test vehicle per model/year; they do not measure real-world crash outcomes. A Poor rating does not mean the system causes crashes.
- ForCar's census is a third-party aggregation of IIHS's public ratings; brand counts depend on which trims/model years IIHS chose to test.
- Poor/Marginal systems still brake for stationary cars in daylight — they fail the *harder* scenarios (pedestrian, darkness, speed), not all braking.
- No FARS linkage: we cannot say how many deaths are attributable to weak AEB vs. absent AEB.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Front crash prevention is one of the most proven safety technologies in history: IIHS/HLDI research shows it substantially cuts rear-end crashes, and even the weakest rated system is a backstop that beats human reaction time in the scenarios it does handle. The new test is *deliberately* harder than most real-world scenarios — it is a stretch goal, not a minimum. The fleet is improving (26%→19% fail rate in three model years), and FMVSS 127's performance requirements will force the laggards to upgrade hardware by 2029 regardless. Rating-shaming risks the perverse outcome: a buyer who delays replacing a 2012 car with no AEB at all because a 2024 model's system is "only" Marginal is measurably worse off.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- Shopping used: ignore the feature list — look up the IIHS front crash prevention rating for the *exact model year*, not the model. Two model years of the same car can differ more than two brands.
- The rating lives at iihs.org/ratings; filter to front crash prevention.
- Treat the system as a backstop for attention, not a substitute: Poor-rated cars still stop for a car ahead in daylight, they just may not see a person at night.
- If buying new: 2026 models fail at 19% vs 26% for 2024s — newer hardware generations (camera+radar) are the thing to ask about, not the trim price.
