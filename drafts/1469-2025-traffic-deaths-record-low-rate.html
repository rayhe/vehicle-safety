By The Numbers

The Headline Says Record Lows. The Morgue Says 36,640.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 11, 2026

NHTSA has good news, and the good news has a body count attached. In April 2026, the agency announced an estimated 36,640 people died in traffic crashes in 2025, a 6.7 percent drop from 2024, with a fatality rate of 1.10 deaths per 100 million miles driven, the second-lowest rate in recorded history. Miles driven went up. Deaths went down. Deaths fell in 39 states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico.[1]

Now the other number. 36,640 works out to about 100 people a day, and it is still above the 2019 total of 36,096, the last normal year before the pandemic sent the death count to 42,939 in 2021. From that peak to the 2025 estimate is a 14.7 percent decline, 6,299 fewer deaths a year, more than half the spike unwound.[2][3] A rate can set records while the count stays above the last normal year, because Americans keep driving more miles. Both numbers are true. Only one of them makes a good headline.

36,640
Estimated traffic deaths in 2025, down 6.7% and still above 2019

Inside the 2024 final file, the one NHTSA has actually counted rather than estimated, the improvement is broad enough to be real. Passenger car occupant deaths fell 1,153, down 9.7 percent. Unrestrained occupant deaths fell 7.4 percent. Ejected occupants fell 11 percent. Speeding-related deaths fell 5.2 percent. Alcohol-impaired deaths fell 3.9 percent, to 11,904. Pedestrian deaths fell 3.9 percent and motorcyclist deaths 2.2 percent.[2]

Two categories went the other way, and they are the only two. Fatalities involving drivers 65 and older rose 1.4 percent. SUV occupant deaths rose 1.6 percent. An aging driving population and a fleet that keeps getting taller and heavier are structural trends, not cyclical ones. They do not reverse on their own.

And there is a stranger split hiding in the same file. Pedestrian deaths fell while pedestrian injuries rose 5.0 percent. Motorcyclist deaths fell while motorcyclist injuries rose 5.2 percent. Fewer people died; more people got hurt and survived. Better trauma care, slower crashes, denser traffic, the data does not pick an explanation, and neither will this article.[2]

The counterargument, at full strength: three straight years of decline, deaths down in 39 states, nearly every category improving, and the second-lowest rate ever while Americans drove more miles is a genuine public-health improvement, not a rounding error. Passenger car occupant deaths fell nearly 10 percent in one year. And the 2025 number is an early estimate, which means it will move; the 2023 total was revised upward when finalized, but it could just as easily revise down and land below 2019. Roads are getting safer and are still dangerous. Both clauses matter.

What this does not prove: the 2025 figure is an estimate, not a count, and it will be revised when the 2025 FARS file lands in spring 2027. The 2024 figure is the Annual Report File, also subject to revision when finalized. Injury numbers come from a sample, not a census. And 2019 was not some golden age, 36,096 deaths was a bad year by historical standards too.

What This Means for You

Nothing about this changes what you do on your next drive, which is the point. Categories that fell hardest are the ones with levers attached: buckle up, since unrestrained deaths fell 7.4 percent as more people did; slow down; put the phone away. NHTSA's own math on distracted driving: 18 people injured every half hour, one killed about every 2.5 hours.[1] If you are shopping for a used car, the IIHS death-rate tables remain the sharpest tool for picking a model. If you drive an SUV, remember yours is the only occupant category whose deaths rose in 2024. And if you want the number that matters most: 36,640 is an estimate. The final count arrives in spring 2027, and it will move.
