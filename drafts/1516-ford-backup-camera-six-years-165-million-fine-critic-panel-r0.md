## 1. General Editor — 8.5
Lede earns its keep: a federal query into a fix that may not have fixed anything is the right opening, and the headline delivers on it. Structure is clean, campaign-by-campaign chronology with the fine as the pivot and the aftermath as the kicker. The dock is length. At 1,521 words this is the longest article in recent memory against a 300-500 word guide and a recent range of 844-1,227. Six campaigns need room, and none of the paragraphs is filler, but the middle campaign paragraph is a data-dense run that asks a lot of a skimming reader. A table would have carried the same numbers in half the space. Still, the piece never sags, and the length is justified by the material rather than indulgence.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0
Rex is present and distinct: deadpan, noir, allergic to press-release language, and the jokes land ("Six campaigns, one component, six years" / "That is the price of moving slowly, per truck"). Rhythm metrics pass with margin and the sentence-length spread is genuinely human. Zero banned phrases, zero em dashes, The-starters at 1.4%. Minor tell: "accepted an independent monitor for three years, agreed to quarterly meetings" paraphrases the NHTSA release a little too faithfully in one sentence. One sentence does not break a voice. Ship it.

## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0
Backover deaths are handled with the actual victims named (children under five, 31 percent of the dead) without gore, without a named child used as a prop, and the Cameron Gulbransen reference is tied to the statute that bears his name, which is attribution, not exploitation. The hero image's bicycle on the driveway is evocative but not graphic. Blame lands on the manufacturer and on regulatory patience, never on owners, and the call to file a VOQ is legitimate civic action with a real mechanism behind it. No self-congratulation, no moralizing. The "yours could be the one that closes it" line flirts with recruitment language but stays on the right side: five complaints genuinely opened this query.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.5
The headline is built to travel, and the pull stat (3,368,065) is the number people will quote. "$266 per truck" is the second quotable line and the one most likely to survive a screenshot. The piece loses half a point because the shareable payload is front-loaded: after the pull stat, the article becomes a chronology that rewards reading rather than skimming, and there is no single-sentence pull quote in the back half as strong as the headline. Fine for the site's audience; slightly less so for a drive-by share.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0
Every checkable claim verified against primary documents. Campaign numbers, dates, and populations all match NHTSA filings: 20V575 (620,246), 24V684 (144,516), 25V270 (160,729), 25V315 (1,075,299), 25V572 (1,456,417), 26V489 (47,587). The consent-order breakdown ($65M / $55M / $45M, three-year term, independent monitor, quarterly meetings, three-year recall review) matches the NHTSA release verbatim in substance. RQ26004 is correctly framed as a query, not a finding, and the five-complaint trigger is accurate. FMVSS 111 cited correctly. One nit: reference [4] links to the NHTSA recall lookup URL rather than the Part 573 PDF; the lookup is a live page and the citation is still verifiable, but the PDF would be stronger. Arithmetic verified: tally sums to 3,368,065; $165M / 620,246 = $266.03.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0
Original contribution is real and stated: the 3.37M campaign-population tally with explicit expansion-overlap accounting, the $266-per-vehicle fine arithmetic, and the consent-order irony (ordered to review all recalls, then filed three more camera campaigns plus a 12.5M-vehicle service program). Limitations are explicit and honest: populations overlap, 2.7% defect rate means most recalled vehicles were fine, RQ26004 proves nothing yet, and nothing here shows a consent-order violation. The counterargument is genuinely steelmanned rather than strawmanned ("finding defects is the system working"). Methodology caveat is in the disclaimer as well as the body. Eight references, five of them primary NHTSA documents.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0
The pull stat is the thesis number and it is correct. Campaign data is presented chronologically in prose with each population attached to its defect mode, which reads better than a table for this story, though a table would have helped the length problem the General Editor flagged. All numbers are consistent between body, pull stat, and disclaimer, and the overlap caveat appears in both the pull-label and the disclaimer. The 25N17 program is correctly distinguished from a safety recall. No chart needed: the story is a count, and the count is the chart.
