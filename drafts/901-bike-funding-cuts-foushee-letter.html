America’s Roads Just Logged Their Safest Start in a Decade. Cyclists Just Logged Their Deadliest Year in 40.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 14, 2026

Celebration was in order this summer. NHTSA announced that 7,770 people died in traffic crashes in the first quarter of 2026, down 4.3 percent from a year earlier, with a fatality rate of 0.99 per 100 million miles — the lowest first-quarter figure since 2014.[1] Full-year 2025 deaths hit their lowest level since 2019. Buried underneath the good news: bicyclist fatalities rose 4 percent in 2025 to 1,148 deaths, near the highest level in more than four decades.[2] Inside cars, the numbers smirk. On two wheels, they snarl.

87%
increase in US bicyclist fatalities since 2010, per a Sep 10, 2026 congressional letter

Reps. Valerie Foushee and Mike Thompson put that number in a letter on September 10 to Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and FHWA Administrator Sean McMaster, and it was not the scariest figure in it. More than 36,000 people were killed on American streets in 2025, the letter says, and one in five traffic deaths was a person walking or biking.[3] Then came the reason for the letter: USDOT has been removing bike-safety resources from the FHWA website, canceling grants that included bicycling facilities, and signaling that bike lanes will lose funding eligibility in the BUILD grant program. Whatever you think of Washington’s spending habits, timing the cuts to the group setting 40-year death records is a bold choice.

Run the mechanism and the policy starts to look less like bookkeeping and more like a causal chain. The League of American Bicyclists, working from NHTSA data, found that 62 percent of bicyclist deaths with a reported speed limit happened on roads posted at 40 mph or higher, and the single most common speed limit for the striking driver was 45 mph.[4] Nobody builds a protected bike lane on a quiet residential street for fun; the lanes that save cyclists go on exactly the high-speed arterials where the federal grant money is now being restricted. Meanwhile NHTSA’s long-awaited pedestrian crashworthiness proposal would let automakers self-assess, would not touch the 5-star rating, and leaves bicyclists out entirely, despite direct urging from the cycling lobby.[5] Your next 5-star SUV will be graded on how it treats a pedestrian-shaped dummy and told nothing about how it treats an actual cyclist.

So what do you do with a split like this? If you ride, treat every 45-mph arterial without a protected lane as the statistical murder scene it is, because the data says that is precisely where cyclists die. Your city council’s bike plan now matters more than anything coming out of Washington; local funding is the whole ballgame. If you drive, understand that a 5-star rating currently promises a cyclist nothing, and that “safest year in a decade” is a statistic about people inside cars. And everyone with a VIN should run it through NHTSA’s recall database before the next oil change — the one safety system nobody is defunding is the one you trigger yourself.[6]

Fine print, because the paradox has cracks. NHTSA’s 2026 early estimate has no breakdown by road-user type yet, so nobody can prove cyclists aren’t sharing in this year’s decline. The letter’s “more than 36,000” is a preliminary count, subject to revision. And the funding fight is exactly that: two members of Congress opposing a stated intent, not a final rule, so the BUILD grant restrictions may yet be walked back or die in the comment period.

Now the strongest case against everything above, at full volume: maybe the 40-year high is a participation story, not a safety failure. American cycling ridership has grown for two decades, e-bikes have put millions of new riders on fast roads, and NHTSA does not track bicycle miles traveled anywhere. Deaths per mile could be flat or even falling while raw deaths set records, and the League itself argues that more riders eventually make riding safer for everyone. If that reading is right, the headline is “more Americans discovered bikes,” and the real question is whether infrastructure is keeping pace. Which, it turns out, is exactly the question the funding cuts answer: no.
