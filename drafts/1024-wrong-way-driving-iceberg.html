500 People Die Every Year Driving the Wrong Way. Most Wrong-Way Drivers Just Turn Around.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

Tonight, somewhere on an American highway, a driver will enter an off-ramp going the wrong direction, and usually nothing happens: the driver catches it or a passenger yells. But about 500 times a year it ends in a head-on collision at highway speed, and the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety says that toll climbed 34% between the early and late 2010s.[1]

87%
of wrong-way drivers were alone in the car, with no passenger to yell stop

Six in ten wrong-way crashes involve an alcohol-impaired driver; drivers over 70 are measurably overrepresented. Nearly nine in ten were driving alone, which makes the passenger the cheapest countermeasure ever studied: a second pair of eyes that spots the red reflectors facing the wrong way.[1] California runs the same pattern at smaller scale: 37 wrong-way deaths a year on its freeways.[2]

Massachusetts just decided to stop guessing. In July, Governor Maura Healey signed a law requiring a statewide, data-driven wrong-way strategy covering infrastructure, technology, education, and annual public reporting on high-risk locations.[3] Hardware is already moving: solar detection trailers with radar, cameras, and LED-enhanced signage are going up at five locations a week, and the network has logged 1,000+ wrong-way events in two years. MassDOT's safety chief offers the reframe: most of those drivers self-corrected. The flashing sign is the least interesting part of the trailer; the counter is what matters.[3]

Nobody else has run the iceberg math: a thousand-plus wrong-way entries in two years in one state, against a national toll of roughly 500 deaths a year across all fifty. Fatal crashes are not the phenomenon; they are the conversion rate at the bottom of a much larger funnel of near-misses, and until the detectors started counting, the funnel was invisible. Arizona saw it early, spending $4 million on a wrong-way alert system along 15 miles of I-17 in 2018, which won a Federal Highway Administration award the next year.[4] Massachusetts is the first state to write the counting into law.

There is a fourth wrong-way driver now, and it does not drink. In October 2025, NHTSA opened Preliminary Evaluation PE25012 into roughly 2.88 million Teslas, investigating reports that Full Self-Driving runs red lights and drives against traffic. At opening: 58 incidents, 14 crashes, 23 injuries, and some cars reportedly ran the light after stopping correctly first, a software decision rather than a sensor failure.[5] The drunk, the disoriented, and the alone have been joined by the algorithm, and no bigger sign fixes that one.

Steel-manning the other side: wrong-way deaths are barely one percent of the 39,000 Americans killed on roads each year. Every detection-trailer dollar is a dollar not spent on interlocks or crossings that would save more lives. Caveats: AAA's figures cover divided highways only and date to 2015–2018, the decade toll of 5,730 deaths comes from a detection-equipment vendor's press release rather than a peer-reviewed study, and “most drivers self-correct” is one agency's read of its own detector logs, not a national statistic.[1][3]

What to actually do: if you are the wrong-way driver, get to the shoulder, stop, hazards on, call 911; no hero U-turns across live lanes. If headlights come at you the wrong way: slow down, move right, call 911 with the location. And passengers: speaking up is not backseat driving; it is a safety device with a better record than most of the electronics in the car.
