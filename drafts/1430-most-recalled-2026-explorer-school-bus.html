The Most-Recalled 2026 Vehicle Is a Ford Explorer. The Runner-Up Carries Your Kids to School.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 8, 2026

I asked NHTSA's database a simple question. Which 2026 vehicle has been recalled the most? Back came a number, not a name: 14 separate safety campaigns on one model year of one vehicle, the Ford Explorer. Runner-up is not a car but a school bus. International has recalled its IC Bus CESB 13 times, twice for the parking brake and once for the service brakes, and the bus in question carries children to school every morning.[1]

14
Safety recall campaigns on the 2026 Ford Explorer, the most of any 2026 passenger vehicle in NHTSA's database

Count the Explorer's fourteen and a pattern shows up fast. Five involve the engine, spread across five separate campaigns, which means five separate moments when somebody at Ford found something else wrong under the hood, while two involve the windshield and two more involve the seats, one for the mid and rear assemblies and one for the front power adjust. Add headlights, seat belts, a defroster fan, a trailer hitch, and a frontal air bag, and you have a vehicle recalled in nearly every system a car has. Not all fourteen are 2026 build problems, since one seat belt campaign reaches back to model year 2020 and another seat campaign covers 2020 through 2027. Legacy defects inflate the count, and I will come back to that, because it matters.[1]

Now look at the runner-up, because it is worse in the way that counts. A school bus with 13 recalls is a different kind of number than an SUV with 14. Three of the thirteen touch the brakes: two parking brake campaigns plus a service brake hose and line campaign. Wheelchair lifts and wheelchair restraints account for two more, which means the equipment that secures the most vulnerable passengers has itself needed fixing twice. Headlights, wipers, transmission solenoids, seat belt fasteners, and body structure fill out the rest. One campaign covers a label; the other eleven do not. When the vehicle in question hauls forty children down a hill, a parking brake recall is not paperwork.[1]

Third place belongs to the Ram 1500, with nine. Its list reads like a modern truck's greatest hits: two instrument cluster campaigns, two backup camera campaigns, brake lights, headlights, rear seat belt anchorage, side air bags, and tire pressure monitoring. Ram owners should note the camera pair in particular, because a backup camera that fails to display is a failed back-over prevention system, not a dead infotainment screen. For scale, NHTSA's database held 26,804 recalls and 1,599,358 owner complaints as of November 7, 2026, and Ford was already running at a record recall pace this year after setting the all-time mark with 153 campaigns in 2025.[1][3][5]

Here is the strongest case against my own leaderboard, stated at full strength. A recall count is not a danger ranking. One campaign can cover 200,000 vehicles or six. A high count partly measures scrutiny, not quality: an automaker that files paperwork looks worse on this chart than one that buries defects, and every campaign on this list comes with a free fix. Fourteen small, fixed recalls may leave you safer than zero recalls on a car nobody inspected. All true. It is also true that five separate engine campaigns on one model year is not a paperwork artifact, and that a school bus needing two parking brake recalls is a finding, not a filing quirk. Both things can be true at once, which is usually how it works with counting.

Limitations, stated plainly: these counts are campaigns naming the model year, not vehicles affected, and not confirmed defects on any individual vehicle. Multi-year campaigns inflate the 2026 totals with older defects. NHTSA's API has make and model spelling quirks, which is why one aggregator's leaderboard-topper, a Volvo semi truck, is excluded here: I could not verify its count in the agency's own API, so it does not make the list. Recall counts also say nothing about completion rates, and most recalled vehicles never get fixed at all. What to do: Explorer owners from model years 2020 through 2026 should check their VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today, because fourteen campaigns means even a brand-new truck can carry multiple open items, including engine and air bag ones. School districts running IC Bus CESB fleets should confirm the parking brake and service brake campaigns are complete before the buses roll. Ram owners should check the two camera campaigns first. And everyone else should remember the only number that matters is the one attached to your own VIN.[2]
