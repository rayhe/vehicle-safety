GM Says Your Brakes Only Fail After You Stop. Your Brakes Say Otherwise.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 2, 2026

On August 21, 2026, federal regulators escalated their investigation of GM's eBoost brake system to a formal Engineering Analysis, covering an estimated 1,164,820 vehicles. The escalation happened for a reason you should find deeply unsettling: GM's account of how these brakes fail and drivers' accounts of how these brakes fail do not agree.[1]

Reported eBoost incidents that ended in a crash or fire (22 of 745)

GM's version goes like this. An internal spindle can fracture inside the eBoost unit. When that happens, anti-lock brakes, stability control, and traction control keep working until the car comes to a complete stop. Only then do the assists drop out, with warnings, lit lamps, and a 43 mph speed cap to get you home. Graceful degradation, nothing to see here. The company told NHTSA exactly that.[2]

Drivers tell a different story. NHTSA's file says it kept receiving reports of an immediate loss of brake assist during a braking event, not after a full stop, which is a completely different failure. If your assist vanishes mid-stop in an emergency, your stopping distance grows and nobody cares what the lamps did afterward. The agency opened this Engineering Analysis because enough drivers described the scary version that it stopped believing the tidy one.[3]

The numbers so far: 745 unique incidents logged (227 owner complaints to NHTSA, 531 reports from GM, duplicates removed), 22 crashes or fires, five injury incidents, six injuries, zero fatalities.[1] Do the arithmetic the way an actuary would: 0.064% of the investigated population filed an incident, and roughly 3% of those incidents ended in a crash or fire. Rare. Not nothing. And the spread matters more than the rate. Fourteen nameplates share this hardware: Cadillac Lyriq, Celestiq, and Optiq; Buick Enclave and Envision; Chevrolet Colorado, Blazer EV, Equinox EV, and Traverse; GMC Canyon and Acadia; the driverless Cruise Origin shuttle; and, the part Honda would prefer you skip, the 2024–2026 Honda Prologue and Acura ZDX, which were built by GM through the GM-Honda joint venture and brake on GM's eBoost system.[4] You bought Honda engineering. Your brakes are a GM part with an open federal file.

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this part. If you drive one of those 14 models, the eBoost signature is a hard brake pedal paired with a "Brake System Failure" message, at startup or while driving. If it happens at speed: keep pushing the pedal, because a mechanical fallback exists even when the assist does not, and get the car somewhere safe instead of restarting it and forgetting. Then check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because being "under investigation" is precisely the stage where an automaker knows something and the public does not.[5]

Now the honest accounting. Brake-by-wire systems retain mechanical fallback, so a hard pedal is not the same as no brakes, and panicked drivers misremember pedal feel. 0.064% is a tiny incident rate across 1.16 million vehicles, there are zero deaths, and GM's warning cascade may describe most cases accurately. NHTSA may close this file with no recall, and "under investigation" is not "defective." But that is exactly why the disagreement matters. The entire escalation turns on whether the mid-brake-loss reports are real, and the agency just spent an upgrade on a federal docket to say it does not trust GM's answer yet.[1]
