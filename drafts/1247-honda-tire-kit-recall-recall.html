The Recall That Recalled Its Own Fix

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 22, 2026

Honda has recalled the remedy. On October 8 the company filed NHTSA campaign 26V663, covering 4,804 Accord Hybrids, CR-V Hybrids and one CR-V Fuel Cell whose tire repair kits were already “fixed” once under an earlier recall, because the replacement nozzles shipped as the fix were in some cases the identical defective nozzles they were supposed to replace.[1] Honda estimates only 1% of the 4,804 vehicles actually carry the bad part, and it cannot say which ones.

100-to-1
Vehicles recalled per actually-defective nozzle — the price of a supplier that could not tell its own parts apart

Start with the first 150 milliseconds of a flat tire, which is where I live. Every kit nozzle carries a one-way valve, and running the kit with the nozzle improperly seated on the tire valve lets pressure build inside the sealant bottle. If the relief valve fails to vent that pressure, the cap detaches, and you are holding a pressurized bottle of sealant aimed at your own face. No injuries have been reported, which is the only reason this story is funny instead of grim. Honda caught it before the body count started.[1]

Now the part that should make every quality engineer flinch. The supplier, TEK Corporation of Troy, Michigan, was supposed to scrap the pre-fix nozzles and ship only corrected ones, but it implemented no change-point management and no disposal controls for the old parts, so old nozzles and new nozzles sat mixed together in one warehouse with no system to tell them apart.[1] A dealership flagged incorrect parts on July 3. Honda and TEK spent July and August auditing inventory and shipments, analyzed the affected population on September 2, and on October 1 Honda decided a safety defect existed. Three months from a dealer’s quiet “these look wrong” to a federal filing, all because nobody labeled two bins.

My favorite detail sits in the remedy description, quoted verbatim: the replacement nozzle does not have a one-way valve.[2] Read that again, because Honda’s fix for a defective one-way valve is a nozzle with no one-way valve at all. The safety feature was the hazard, so they deleted it. TEK, meanwhile, promises it will now implement, and I quote, “system-controlled processes to prevent parts with different configuration or disposition statuses from being mixed.” Translation: they bought labels.

Do the arithmetic, because it is the whole story. Four thousand eight hundred four vehicles recalled to find roughly forty-eight bad nozzles, a 100-to-1 recall ratio that exists for exactly one reason: TEK could not account for its own bins. The skeptic’s case writes itself, and it deserves a hearing. The system worked, a dealer caught the bad parts, nobody got hurt, and a wide recall is simply the price of traceability. Fair enough, except traceability is precisely what TEK lacked, which means the recall is enormous not because the system worked but because the supplier’s inventory control was a mixed bin. That is the system discovering, three months late, that it never knew what it shipped.

What this means for you

If you drive a 2023–2026 Accord Hybrid, a 2023–2026 CR-V Hybrid, or a 2025 CR-V Fuel Cell, and you already had the tire kit serviced under the first recall, 26V366, you are not done. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls or recalls.honda.com, because owner letters do not go out until November 16.[3] If your car is on the list, the dealer inspects the nozzle and replaces it free of charge. One more thing worth knowing: the defect only triggers when the kit runs with the nozzle improperly connected to the tire valve, so seat the nozzle on the valve first and then start the pump. Your owner’s manual buries this step. It does not.
