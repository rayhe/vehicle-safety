## Key verified facts (from DOT HS 813 822)

- 1,103 pedalcyclists killed in 2024, down 6% from 1,173 in 2023. 2.8% of all traffic fatalities.
- An estimated 52,887 pedalcyclists injured in 2024, UP 6% from 49,989 in 2023. Deaths fell, injuries rose.
- 2023's 1,173 was the most cyclist deaths since federal recordkeeping began in 1975 (GHSA). IIHS counts 1,075 for 2024 (third-highest ever) — the 28-death gap is a boundary difference: NHTSA counts motorized bicycles as pedalcyclists since 2022; IIHS does not.
- **The dark:** more pedalcyclist fatalities occurred in the dark (52%) than in daylight (44%), dusk 3%, dawn 1%. Cyclists are the road-user group killed more often at night than by day. The 6-8:59 p.m. window is the deadliest in every season (19-27% of seasonal deaths).
- **The vehicle:** of 1,035 cyclists killed in single-vehicle crashes where the first harmful event was the collision, light trucks struck 552 (53%) vs passenger cars 282 (27%). SUVs 292, pickups 220, vans 40. 84% were struck by the front of the vehicle (869 of 1,035). 95% of all cyclist deaths were single-vehicle crashes.
- **Who dies:** 87% male (959 of 1,103); male fatality rate 0.57 per 100,000 vs female 0.08 — more than 7x. Average age of a killed cyclist: 48. Largest death counts in ages 60-64 and 55-59; ages 65+ accounted for 237 deaths (21.5%). Children 14 and younger: 5% of deaths, 12% of injuries.
- **Where:** 81% urban, 60% not at intersections, 30% at intersections. 62% weekday, 38% weekend.
- **States:** Florida 208 deaths (most), California 163, Texas 79. Highest rate: Louisiana 0.96 per 100,000 (3x the national 0.32), then Florida 0.89. Lowest: Maine 0.07.
- **Cities:** Tucson, AZ highest big-city rate (1.44 per 100,000, 4.5x national), then Sacramento 1.12. Seven major cities reported ZERO cyclist deaths in 2024: Dallas, Fort Worth, Seattle, Denver, El Paso, Memphis, Milwaukee. Memphis had the highest total traffic fatality rate of any big city (30.77 per 100,000) and still killed zero cyclists.
- **Alcohol:** 33% of fatal cyclist crashes involved alcohol (driver and/or cyclist). 17% of crashes had a cyclist at BAC .08+; 14% had a driver at BAC .08+; 3% both. 21% of killed cyclists had BAC .01+, down from 27% in 2015.
- **Helmets (IIHS):** 68% of bicyclists killed in 2024 were not wearing helmets; helmet use unknown for 19%.
- **E-bike wrinkle:** CPSC counted 97 e-bike deaths in 2024, up from 6 in 2018. Since 2022 FARS counts motorized-bicycle riders as pedalcyclists, which lifts the NHTSA total vs IIHS.

## Original computations (not in the source)

- Light trucks vs cars: 552 / 282 = 1.96x — light trucks killed nearly twice as many cyclists as passenger cars. Light-truck share of single-vehicle cyclist deaths: 552 / 1,035 = 53.3%; cars 27.2%.
- Dark vs daylight: 52% of 1,103 = ~574 deaths in the dark vs ~485 in daylight. The dark kills about 89 more cyclists a year than daylight does.
- Injuries per death: 2024: 52,887 / 1,103 = 47.9; 2023: 49,989 / 1,173 = 42.6. Injuries-per-death rose 12.5% while deaths fell 6% — crashes got less lethal but more frequent, or reporting shifted. Either way the "good news" headline (deaths down 6%) is half the story.
- Florida + California + Texas: 208 + 163 + 79 = 450 = 40.8% of all US cyclist deaths in three states.
- Decade trend (NHTSA count): 621 deaths in 2010 (IIHS low) to 1,103 in 2024 = +77.6%. IIHS reports +73% on its own 1,075 count. Either way, cyclist deaths are roughly three-quarters higher than the 2010 low while total traffic deaths are roughly flat vs 2010 (32,999 in 2010 vs 39,254 in 2024, +19%).
- The 2024 total (1,103) is the third-highest in the fact sheet's 2015-2024 table (2023: 1,173; 2022: 1,117; 2024: 1,103) — a "decline" from the worst year on record to the third-worst year on record.
- Exposure gap (CDC): bike trips are 1% of all trips; cyclists are 2.8% of traffic deaths — 2.8x overrepresentation per trip.

## Novel angle (kill test: PASS)

Every cyclist-safety conversation is about the rider: wear a helmet, wear hi-vis, don't drink. The 2024 data says the killing is structural, not behavioral: more cyclists die in the dark than in daylight, light trucks (SUVs and pickups) now strike twice as many riders as cars, 84% are hit by the front of the vehicle, and 60% die away from intersections — mid-block, where there is no crosswalk, no signal, and no bike lane to hide in. The rider did not run a red light. A tall, heavy, blind-spotted front end met an unlit rider at 7 p.m. on a stroad. Site coverage check: zero queue hits for a Bicyclists 2024 fact-sheet piece; #1299 mentioned 2025 cyclists only as a one-line "exception"; #998 covered e-bikes (CPSC); #1020 covered an NTSB bike-route case; #1157 covered DST pedestrian deaths. Nobody on this site has ever written the 2024 cyclist story. The vehicle-design hook is Mia's beat: the light-truck front end is the weapon, and the dark is the accomplice.

## Strongest counterargument (must state)

The 6% decline in deaths is real and the 2023 record may have been a post-pandemic spike unwinding, not a trend reversal — cyclist deaths fell while injuries rose, which could mean crashes are getting less lethal (better trauma care, slower speeds) rather than rarer. Exposure is also unmeasured: nobody knows how many miles Americans cycled in 2024, so the death rate per mile is unknowable from this fact sheet, and a 6% death decline against unknown mileage proves nothing about per-mile risk. The light-truck finding is partly a fleet-composition artifact: light trucks are now the majority of vehicles sold, so they strike more of everything, not just cyclists. And the darkness finding is confounded by volume: evening is when the most people ride after work. The article must say all of this, then show why the pattern still matters: even after those discounts, a 2:1 light-truck-to-car kill ratio and a dark-over-daylight majority do not appear in pedestrian data the same way, and the 73-78% rise since 2010 dwarfs the 19% rise in total traffic deaths over the same span.

## Limitations (must state)

- 2024 figures are from the FARS Annual Report File, subject to revision in the Final File (2023's cyclist count was revised 1,166 to 1,173).
- Injury figures are CRSS estimates, not counts, with sampling error.
- The fact sheet only includes pedalcyclist crashes involving motor vehicles in transport; single-bicycle crashes and off-road deaths are excluded.
- No exposure denominator: no national count of bicycle miles traveled, so per-mile risk cannot be computed.
- The NHTSA/IIHS counts differ by 28 deaths because of the motorized-bicycle boundary; both are cited with the reason stated.
- Helmet-use data is incomplete (unknown for 19% of killed cyclists in IIHS data).
