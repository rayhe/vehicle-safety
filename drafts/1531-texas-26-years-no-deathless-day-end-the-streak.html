Texas Has Not Had a Single Deathless Day in 26 Years

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 16, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Texas has not had a single day without a traffic death since November 7, 2000. That was 9,505 days ago. More than 95,000 people have died on Texas roads since, which works out to ten a day, every day, for twenty-six years.[1]

9,505
Days since Texas last had a day with zero traffic deaths, November 7, 2000

TxDOT marked the anniversary on November 5 with a campaign called #EndTheStreakTX and a number that sounds like good news. Texas recorded 3,763 traffic deaths in 2025, down nearly ten percent from 4,162 in 2024. Best year of the streak, and still ten people a day. A 9.6 percent decline from a horrifying baseline is still a horrifying baseline, and the campaign knows it. Troopers are handing out safe-driving tips at traffic stops for two weeks, which is the state asking nicely while it spends $16 billion on cable barriers, rumble strips, and roundabouts over the next decade.[1]

NHTSA's new first-half 2026 estimate, published November 10, already took the good news back. Texas is projected at 1,851 deaths for January through June 2026, against 1,856 in the same period last year, flat, down three-tenths of one percent. Nationally the same report shows the first quarter falling five percent and the second quarter falling two-tenths of one percent, which means every bit of the 2026 improvement happened before April and none of it happened after. Texas's best year and its flat half-year are not a contradiction. They are a before and an after.[2][3]

Count is the wrong metric for a state this size, so run the rate. Texas kills 1.19 people per hundred million miles driven. America kills 1.02. That gap is 17 percent, and it is not a rounding error or a denominator trick. If Texas drove at the national rate for the first half of 2026, roughly 265 fewer Texans would have died. Assume the whole gap is closable and the arithmetic is still honest about one thing: the problem is not that Texas is big. The problem is that every mile in Texas is more dangerous than the average American mile.[3]

Harris County owns the biggest number and the wrong reputation. It killed 517 people in 2025, more than any other Texas county, and it also leads the state in DWI fatalities. But Harris holds a fifth of the state's crashes and only an eighth of its deaths. Rural Texas holds 22 percent of the crashes and 45 percent of the deaths. Fifteen of 254 counties account for half of all Texas deaths, and 27 counties recorded none at all. A crash on a 75-mph rural road kills 21.5 people per thousand crashes. A crash on a 30-mph street kills 3.2. The streak lives on the two-lane highway, not the Houston freeway.[4][5]

Here is the part the campaign cannot say out loud. A deathless day requires a day with zero deaths, and Texas averages ten. If daily deaths followed a Poisson distribution, the odds of a zero day would be about one in twenty-two thousand. That is not a goal; that is a lottery ticket. Two weeks of tip cards will not move it. Sixteen billion dollars of cable barriers and roundabouts might, eventually, if the daily mean ever falls far enough for zero to become plausible. TxDOT's own page says roundabouts can cut intersection fatalities by 90 percent. Intersections are not the whole streak, but they are a start.[1]

What to do: buckle up, and if you are drinking in Houston, get a sober ride, because Harris County leads the state in DWI deaths and the county that crashes the most is not the county that kills the most per crash. On rural Texas roads, slow down. The speed ladder is the whole story: 75-mph roads kill nearly seven times as often per crash as 30-mph streets. Before you buy a car in Texas, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because a crash here is more likely to kill than the same crash in a city, and an open recall is a risk you can check for free in sixty seconds.[4]
