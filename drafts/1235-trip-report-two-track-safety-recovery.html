By The Numbers

America's Roads Got Safer Four Years Running. Unless You Weren't in a Car.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 21, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, and they didn't get better for everyone. On September 25, the nonprofit research group TRIP released its annual accounting of America's crash toll, and the headline is genuinely good: traffic deaths fell in 2025 for the fourth straight year, unwinding the pandemic-era spike of 2020 and 2021.[1] Four years of fewer deaths is real progress.

$1.75 trillion
Estimated societal harm from 2025's fatal and serious crashes: $435 billion economic, $1.32 trillion in lost quality of life

Here is the other number, the one that made me re-run the spreadsheet: pedestrians and bicyclists accounted for 21% of all U.S. traffic deaths in 2025.[1] Their combined toll rose 8% between 2020 and 2025, pedestrians from 6,236 to 6,529 and bicyclists from 891 to 1,148, a 29% jump.[1] Do the subtraction and the story writes itself. The national death toll fell four years running while the people outside cars died more often, which means every bit of the improvement came from the people inside them.

21%
Of all 2025 U.S. traffic deaths were pedestrians and bicyclists, a share that grew while the total shrank

Now the money, because I will fit a cost function to anything. TRIP puts the societal harm of 2025's fatal and serious crashes at $1.75 trillion: $435 billion in economic costs plus $1.32 trillion in quality-of-life losses, using NHTSA's 2023 crash-cost methodology.[1] That is roughly $4.79 billion of harm per day. And the Governors Highway Safety Association reports that the 3,024 pedestrians killed in just the first half of 2025 cost society more than $40 billion,[3] which works out to about $13.2 million per death.

Fairness requires the other dataset. GHSA's preliminary state data puts 2025 pedestrian deaths at 6,732, down 7%: the third straight annual decline, 505 fewer than the year before.[2] That figure is higher than TRIP's pedestrian number (6,529), because preliminary state reports and finalized national estimates are different vintages of the same tragedy. Both can be true: the five-year arc is worse, and the recent trend is bending down. Meanwhile GHSA's autopsy of how pedestrians die reads like an indictment of the fleet: SUVs and pickups account for more than half of pedestrian deaths, nearly one in four is a hit-and-run, more than three-quarters happen at night, and three in five occur where there is no sidewalk.[2]

The caveats, since I read the footnotes: 2025 final FARS numbers are not out, so both reports lean on estimates. TRIP's cost math applies NHTSA's 2023 methodology, built on 2019 unit costs, to 2025 dollars, and medical inflation alone makes that fuzzy. And 2020 is a strange baseline; pandemic traffic patterns flatter the occupant story and steepen the vulnerable-user one. What survives the caveats: speed kills people on foot (71% of Massachusetts' 2025 pedestrian deaths happened on roads posted 25 to 35 mph, posted limit rather than actual speed);[4] SUVs and pickups dominate the killer's side of the ledger; and if your car has an open recall, the VIN lookup takes ninety seconds at nhtsa.gov/recalls.[5] The cheapest safety device in 2025 remains a slower car and a sidewalk.
