Ford's F-150 Got Recalled Three Times in One Week – for the Parts That Hold It Together

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 18, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. In the week of September 14, Ford filed four safety recalls covering 225,000-plus vehicles, and three of them were the F-150. The big one: 223,472 trucks built between January 2023 and August 2026 whose front fuel tank straps may not have been assembled properly, meaning the tank can leak fuel or detach from the truck while you're driving.[1]

223,526
F-150s recalled across three campaigns in a single week

A fuel tank falling off a moving truck sounds like a cartoon gag, and Ford's paperwork is less funny. The straps that anchor the tank weren't always slotted into the frame correctly, and Ford traced the failure to periods when its assembly-line cameras weren't monitoring the strap installation at all.[2] So far: 16 warranty claims worldwide, zero reported crashes. That doesn't mean zero tanks moved, it means nobody's filing paperwork for the ones that shifted and leaked quietly.

Then came the other two, and this is where it stops looking like bad luck. Thirty-seven 2026 F-150s rolled off the line with the wrong rear axle tail bearings: 8.8-inch units where the Max Tow package demands 9.75-inch ones. Wrong part, wrong bin, installed on two days in May before the Sterling Axle Plant caught it. The failure mode isn't a rattle; it's rear wheel lock-up, driveshaft separation, or the truck rolling away if the parking brake isn't set.[3] And 17 F-150 Raptors got Panhard rod fasteners that may never have been torqued, because the torque-checking device was sitting in bypass mode. A dealer found that one on a customer's truck, after two warranty claims. The quality gate was a guy who heard a rattle.

Notice the pattern: none of these are design flaws. no airbag geometry, no crash structure, no sensor blind spot. All three are assembly failures: cameras not watching, torque tools in bypass, parts from the wrong bin. The F-150 sells on toughness, and toughness is meaningless if the bolts were never tightened. This is the highest-volume vehicle in America, and Ford's own records describe its assembly line running for days with the error-proofing switched off.

Now the uncomfortable data, because I ran it: over the decade FARS tracks, the F-150 accumulated 9,194 deaths, the second-highest raw body count of any nameplate in America, behind only the Chevrolet Silverado's 9,591.[4] On fatality rate the truck sits mid-pack: 1.04 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles, against 1.25 for the Silverado, 1.01 for the GMC Sierra, and 0.80 for the Toyota Tacoma. In other words, the most popular truck is not the safest truck, and it never was. Volume explains the body count, but it doesn't excuse it. Tacoma proves you can sell a pile of trucks at a materially lower death rate.

Counterargument, stated fairly: at 700,000-plus F-150s a year, three recalls totaling 223,526 units is a fraction of one production year's output, and Ford caught two of them internally before anyone got hurt. Scale cuts both ways, though: when the best-selling vehicle in the country has an assembly-line quality lapse, hundreds of thousands of drivers inherit it. A boutique defect is a recall; a volume-vehicle defect is a public works project.

FARS only covers 2014 through 2023, so it barely overlaps these model years. I'm not claiming any of those 9,194 deaths came from a loose fuel tank, and you shouldn't either. What the data does show is that popularity is not a safety proxy. And what this week's paperwork shows is that the truck's weakest link isn't the steel, it's the process that puts the steel together.

What to do: if you own a 2023–2027 F-150, enter your 17-character VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now. Do not wait for the letter, which Ford starts mailing September 21. If you smell fuel, see pooling under the truck, or hear a new rattle from the rear axle, treat it as urgent. Every one of these repairs is free by federal law.[5]
