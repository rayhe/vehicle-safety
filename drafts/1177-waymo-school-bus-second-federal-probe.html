Waymo Can't Stop for School Buses. NHTSA Is Asking Again.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 15, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: somewhere in Atlanta earlier this month, a car with no driver in it looked at a stopped school bus, red lights flashing, stop arm extended, children unloading, and drove around it.[1] Now the federal government is investigating Waymo's robotaxis over school buses for the second time in a year. Austin's school district alone has issued about 20 citations for Waymo vehicles illegally passing stopped school buses since the school year began.[1] Twenty. Since August.

20
School-bus passing citations against Waymo vehicles from one Texas school district this school year

Here is the cosmic punchline: this already happened. In October 2025, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Preliminary Evaluation PE25-013 after a Waymo drove around a stopped Atlanta school bus, passing the extended crossing arm near disembarking students and the extended stop arm on the bus's left side, with no safety operator aboard.[2] Its resume includes a sentence that has aged like an open wound: based on its engagement with Waymo so far, "the likelihood of other prior similar incidents is high."[2] Then came the December expansion, after Austin police documented 19 incidents including five that occurred after Waymo's November 17 software fix.[4] Police Chief Wayne Sneed's verdict was blunt: the programming changes "did not resolve the issue or our concerns."[4] Waymo filed a voluntary software recall covering more than 3,000 vehicles.[5] And here we are, ten months later, with NHTSA reviewing fresh footage and Waymo telling regulators a new voluntary software recall is coming.[1]

Now the part that bends the mind. One of the documented violations wasn't a pure autonomy failure at all. Per the NTSB's preliminary report on a January 12, 2026 incident in Austin, the Waymo had initially stopped, queried a remote agent at the operations center in Michigan, and received the wrong answer: the human, 1,300 miles from the intersection, identified the situation as not involving a school bus with active signals and approved the pass.[6] So the driverless car had a driver, one working from home 1,300 miles from the intersection. Somewhere between the lidar, the neural nets, and the Michigan call center, the cheapest insurance policy on earth, a red octagon on a stick, went unread by every system involved, machine and human alike.

Austin's school district asked Waymo to pause operations during student pickup and drop-off hours. Waymo declined, and that deserves a moment: a school district asked the company to simply not drive past its buses while children are boarding, and the $126 billion company, fresh off a $16 billion raise, 500,000 rides a week, 14 cities and counting, said no.[5][1] For context, Austin ISD says it prosecutes more than 10,000 human violations around its buses per year, which Waymo's chief safety officer was happy to quote while declining to pause.[3] The math is technically on his side. A school bus stop arm flashing in your face is not great optics.

This is not Waymo's only federal file involving children this year. In January, a driverless Waymo Jaguar I-Pace struck a 9-year-old near an elementary school in Santa Monica during drop-off, braking from 17 mph to under 6 mph before contact, and NHTSA opened PE26001 to ask whether the car exercised appropriate caution near the school. When TechCrunch inquired, the agency posted Waymo's responses to twelve questions about how the car behaves around schools, every one of them redacted as confidential business information.[1] Two probes, two incidents, two different failure modes, one calendar year. One company insists its driver is ten times safer than a human while declining to show its work on the exact question the public is asking.

Waymo's strongest case deserves a full hearing, because it is real. No collision has occurred in any of the school-bus passing incidents, and the Santa Monica child's injuries were minor. Measured in peer-reviewed studies and federal crash reports, the company's aggregate safety record genuinely beats the human baseline. Twenty robotaxi violations against 10,000-plus human violations in one district is 0.2 percent of the problem. A human who blows past a school bus gets a ticket and a scolding; a robotaxi gets a federal investigation precisely because the standard is higher, which is arguably the system working as intended. And on the Santa Monica crash, Waymo's own model puts a fully attentive human's impact speed at 14 mph against the car's under-6, and every one of those facts is true. They do not change the other fact, the one NHTSA keeps returning to: a robot cannot reliably perform the single most legible maneuver in American driving, stopping for the red lights of a school bus, and the company refused the district's simplest ask.

What this analysis did not prove, stated plainly. That "fresh probe this week" framing comes from the October 15 report; I could not independently verify a new ODI action number, and the article attributes the timing claim to that reporting. Citation counts vary by outlet and date, 19 versus 20 versus 24-plus, so treat them as ranges. Industry coverage of the preliminary report, not the report itself, is the source for the NTSB remote-agent finding. Waymo has not been found to have a safety defect, and the planned voluntary recall may fully resolve the behavior. FARS tracks fatal crashes, not school-bus passings, so the embedded data cannot quantify the real-world risk. And note the parallel file sitting on the same desk: October 2026 also has Tesla's DP26010 school-zone speeding petition open, covered elsewhere on this site. This is not one company's bug; it is the robotaxi industry's school problem.

If your district uses stop-arm cameras, know that the enforcement math against robotaxis is still being written, because the vehicle owner is a corporation with a legal department. If you ride robotaxis, treat stopped school buses exactly as you would with a human driver behind you, which is to say, stop, because the car ahead of you might not. And remember the general principle this saga keeps demonstrating: a completed recall is a press release, not a verified fix. What matters is whether the violations continued after the update, and in Austin, they did, twice. Ask it about every software recall, every time. Robots are learning and buses are still stopping, but only one of those facts is comforting.
