The 2009 F-150 Was America's Safest Truck. It Was Also the One Ford Sold the Fewest Of.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 19, 2026

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. In January 2009, Ford announced that its redesigned F-150 was the safest full-size pickup ever built: an IIHS Top Safety Pick, five-star NHTSA ratings front and side, and standard AdvanceTrac with Roll Stability Control, a first for the nameplate.[4] The federal death records appear to agree. Across the 2009 redesign, the F-150's normalized fatal-crash deaths fell 45.8 percent, the steepest improvement for any generation change in our dataset.[1]

−45.8%
F-150 fatal crashes per model year, normalized by years on road: 2005–2008 vs. 2009–2012

So I ran the same calculation on the Silverado. It dropped 45.5 percent across the same window. Curious thing about that: the Silverado was not redesigned in 2009. Its GMT900 generation dated to 2007, and nobody held a press conference about it that year.

Then I ran it on everything else with a bed. Ram fell 32.4 percent, the Sierra 43.0, the Tundra 53.8. Even the Nissan Titan, which sells in numbers best described as artisanal, fell 56.6 percent, though its counts are small enough to wobble. Every major pickup in America shows the same 2009-to-2011 trough in per-model-year deaths, then rebounds as if nothing happened. Redesigns had nothing to do with it. Something else was killing fewer people, and it wasn't a high-strength chassis: it was the absence of trucks.

Ford F-Series sales collapsed from 690,589 in 2007 to 413,625 in 2009, the worst year of the Great Recession, before recovering to 763,402 by 2013.[5] The trough in deaths lines up almost exactly with the trough in production. Fewer trucks built means fewer trucks on the road, which means fewer fatal crashes involving those model years, a relationship so obvious it barely deserves the word analysis. And here is the part that should embarrass everyone who cited the redesign: the 2013 and 2014 F-150s, still the celebrated 12th generation, rebounded to roughly 26 to 30 normalized deaths per year on road, right back at pre-redesign levels. The safety gains evaporated at precisely the moment the assembly lines sped back up.

Now, the strongest case for the other side deserves its hearing, because it is genuinely strong, and it starts with a simple fact: electronic stability control works. IIHS research found it cuts fatal single-vehicle crash risk roughly in half, and the 2009 F-150 really was the first in its class to standardize AdvanceTrac with roll stability control across every trim.[2] Ford earned those five stars in actual crash tests, not in a spreadsheet. The honest problem is that the data cannot separate the two effects. The redesign and the recession arrived in the same model year, wearing the same coat, and no FARS-only analysis can tell you how much of the trough belongs to each. Some of that 45.8 percent is real engineering. Most of it, given the Silverado control moved in lockstep without any redesign at all, is arithmetic.

What this does not prove matters as much as what it does. FARS captures only fatal crashes from 2014 to 2023, so a redesign that traded deaths for injuries would be invisible here. The normalization divides by years on road and assumes each model year sold at a steady clip, which is exactly the assumption the recession destroyed; calendar-year sales are a proxy for model-year production, and a rough one. Older trucks also leave the fleet through scrappage, which biases the pre-redesign years downward, meaning the real volume-adjusted drop is probably smaller than the headline number, not larger.

Here is what to do with this. If you are shopping for a used truck, do not pay a "safer generation" premium based on raw model-year death counts; a 2010 F-150 is not meaningfully safer than a 2008, there were simply fewer of them built. What actually predicts used-truck safety is boring and checkable: electronic stability control, which became mandatory on all light vehicles in September 2011, so anything from the 2012 model year on carries it by law.[3] Crash-test ratings for that specific generation, not the marketing around it. And open recalls, which you can check by VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands. A 2009 or newer truck gets you standard stability control, and that part of the story was true. The rest of the halo was the economy.
