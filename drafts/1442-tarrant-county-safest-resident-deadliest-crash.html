The Gap

Tarrant County Is the Second-Safest Big County in Texas to Live In. It Is the Second-Deadliest to Crash In.

Axle McScatter • Data Visualization Editor
November 9, 2026

Tarrant County, Texas killed 190 people on the road in 2025.[1] That is the third-largest body count of any county in the state, tied with Bexar County at 190, and the most boring ranking in the dataset: divide by population and Tarrant drops to fifth of the six biggest counties, at 8.56 deaths per 100,000 residents, the second-safest rate of the Big Six, ahead of everyone except El Paso.[3] Run the second denominator and the county flips. TxDOT’s county table counts 186 fatal crashes out of 30,433 total crashes, a rate of 6.11 per 1,000, second-deadliest of the Big Six behind only Travis County at 8.53.[1]

6.11
Fatal crashes per 1,000 total crashes in Tarrant County, 2025, vs 4.44 in Harris County and 3.89 in Bexar County (TxDOT county tables)

No other big county in Texas splits like this. Travis County ranks first on both denominators, the most deaths per resident and the most per crash, and El Paso ranks sixth on both, the safest on both.[3] Dallas, Harris, and Bexar each move one place at most between the two rankings. Tarrant moves four: fifth per capita, second per crash. A crash in Tarrant County was 1.38 times as likely to be fatal as one in Harris County and 1.57 times as likely as one in Bexar County. Fewer people die per resident here than almost anywhere big in Texas, and when the crash happens, it kills more often than almost anywhere big in Texas. Both statements are true at the same time, which is what makes the county interesting and what makes the per-capita ranking a trap.

Fort Worth is not diluting the county. It is the county. TxDOT’s Cities and Towns report counts 109 fatal crashes and 112 deaths inside Fort Worth city limits in 2025, out of 15,680 total crashes, a fatal-crash rate of 6.95 per 1,000, the second-highest of any major Texas city after Austin at 8.52.[2] Fort Worth holds 58.6 percent of the county’s fatal crashes and 58.9 percent of its deaths, which means the lethality is not a fringe artifact, it is downtown and the interstates feeding it. Arlington, the county’s second city, sits almost exactly on the county average: 31 fatal crashes and 31 deaths out of 4,985 total crashes, 6.22 per 1,000.[2] Arlington is not the problem and not the solution. It is the county average with a stadium district attached.

Now the twist, and it is the freshest number in this story. In the first nine months of 2026, Tarrant County recorded 24,868 crashes, up 0.8 percent on the same period in 2025, and 12,495 injured people, up 3.7 percent, but 142 deaths against 151, down 6.0 percent.[4] Crashes up, injuries up, deaths down: the three series are moving in different directions for the first time in the county’s recent record, and the first half tells the same story, 94 deaths against 104, down 9.6 percent, while crashes rose 1.9 percent.[5] One split inside the improvement deserves its own line. Commercial-vehicle crashes were flat, 1,770 against 1,774, but commercial-vehicle deaths rose from 13 to 18 in the nine-month window, even as overall deaths fell.[4] Passenger vehicles are getting safer in Tarrant County. Trucks are not.

Zoom out a decade and the per-capita safety stops looking like a fluke. NHTSA’s FARS county series puts Tarrant at 9.0 road deaths per 100,000 residents per year from 2015 through 2024, below the Texas rate of 13.6, and 21st of the 46 American counties averaging a million or more residents.[6] In 2024 alone the county recorded 205 deaths and 193 fatal crashes, 9.2 per 100,000 against 13.3 statewide. Tarrant has been an average-to-safe county by population for ten years. Per-crash lethality is the new finding, not per-capita safety, and it has a face: pedestrians. Inside Fort Worth city limits from 2021 through 2025, pedestrians were 2 percent of crashes and 26 percent of the deaths, 1,426 pedestrian crashes and 157 deaths.[7] At intersections the toll was 506 crashes and 31 deaths. One corner, Henderson Street at Belknap and Weatherford on the downtown edge, logged 197 crashes in five years and 51 in 2025 alone, the most at any Fort Worth intersection in a single year.[7]

Give the ranking its proper discount before believing it. Tarrant logged only 30,433 total crashes against Harris County’s 107,472, so its rate is more sensitive to a bad year, and under-reported minor crashes inflate any county’s fatal-per-1,000 rate. But the gap is large, 6.11 against 4.44, a 38 percent lead, and the per-capita ranking uses a completely different denominator, population rather than crashes, so a paperwork quirk would have to move both the same way to manufacture the split. Two independent counts of the county’s deaths also agree within one, TxDOT’s April county table at 190 and the Reyes CRIS export at 191.[1][3] And the 2026 improvement deserves the same skepticism in reverse: nine deaths is a small absolute number, and one bad quarter can erase it. Tarrant’s 2026 improvement is real in the public record but unproven as a trend. Next two quarters decide whether it is a turnaround or a lucky draw.

What to do with this depends on where you drive. If you live in Tarrant County, your odds of dying on the road in a given year are lower than in any other big Texas county except El Paso, and your odds of dying if you do crash are the second-highest, so buckle up like the crash is the dangerous part, because here it is. If you drive I-35W through Fort Worth, know it logged 1,787 crashes in nine months of 2026, the most of any roadway label in the county.[4] On foot in Fort Worth, cross at intersections and assume you are invisible after dark: the five-year data says the risk is not evenly spread, it is concentrated on people walking. And if you drive a truck or work around them in Tarrant County, the improvement everyone else is enjoying has not reached you yet.
