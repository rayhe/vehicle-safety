# Research — #1564: The $21.9 Million Verdict on a Garbage Truck's Underride Guard

**Kicker:** Investigation
**Date researched:** November 22, 2026

## Angle
A Los Angeles jury just put a $21.9 million price on a rear underride guard that wasn't up to the law. In November 2019, an Acura carrying three 18-year-olds hit the back of a Consolidated Disposal garbage truck in the north San Fernando Valley. Two of them died. The survivor has a permanent brain injury. After a two-month trial, the jury found the truck's rear underride guard — the ICC bar meant to stop a car sliding under a truck — was not compliant with existing law. The defense's argument was remarkable: the company said it wasn't required to have compliant guards because the standards are out of date and can't protect modern sedans anyway. The jury disagreed. And the timing is the story's second punch: the same month this verdict landed, NHTSA recalled MAC liquid-tank trailers for shipping with no rear impact guards at all (26V723, Nov 11, 2026). Seven years after the crash, the industry is still shipping trucks without the bar, and still arguing the rule is too old to follow.

## Verified facts (3+ primary sources)

1. **The verdict (PR Newswire, Nov 19, 2026, plaintiff's counsel release; corroborated by Santa Clarita Valley Signal, Nov 14, 2026).** A Los Angeles County jury awarded $21.9 million after a two-month trial in Chatsworth. Case: *Gallegos v. Consolidated Disposal Service LLC, dba Republic Services of Southern California*, Los Angeles County Superior Court, Consolidated Case No. 21STCV42588. Crash: November 2019, north San Fernando Valley. An Acura carrying three 18-year-olds collided with a Consolidated Disposal garbage truck. Sebastien "Bash" Gallegos, 18, passenger, killed. Eugne Oh, 18, driver, killed. Angel Sanchez, 18, passenger, survived with a permanent traumatic brain injury. Fault: 65% to Oh (the Acura driver), 20% to Consolidated Disposal, 15% to the Consolidated truck driver. Awards: $15 million to Gallegos' parents Kimberly and Alex Gallegos ($7.5M past suffering + $7.5M future suffering); $6.9 million to Sanchez ($500K future lost earning capacity, $400K medical expenses, $1M past pain and suffering, $5M future pain and suffering). Total $21.9M.
2. **The guard was the central issue (PR Newswire, Nov 19, 2026).** "A central issue at trial was the truck's rear underride guard, also known as an ICC bar." The guards are "required by State and Federal law to be properly in place and substantially constructed." The plaintiffs' evidence showed "the guards being used by Consolidated/Republic were not compliant with existing law." The defense "repeatedly argued that they were not required to have underride guards and that their alleged failure to follow State and Federal law was excused because the laws were out of date and no longer capable of protecting modern passenger sedans." The jury rejected that: the law applied, and the guards failed it.
3. **The standard itself (NHTSA FMVSS 223/224; 2022 final rule, Federal Register 2022-14330 — via #1559 research).** Federal rear underride protection requirements date to 1953 (per co-counsel Maxey Scherr in the release). NHTSA's 2022 final rule strengthened FMVSS 223/224, requiring guards that withstand higher forces. The defense's "outdated law" claim cuts against a rule NHTSA updated four years ago — and the plaintiffs' evidence was that the guards failed even the law as written.
4. **Same-month parallel: trailers shipping with no guard at all (NHTSA recall 26V723, Part 573 report Nov 11, 2026 — via #1559).** MAC Trailer recalled certain 2025–2027 liquid tank trailers because they may be missing rear impact guards, failing FMVSS 223 and 224. Dealers install under-ride bumpers free; owner letters expected Dec 29, 2026. The verdict and the recall landed eight days apart.
5. **The undercount backdrop (GAO-19-264; IIHS Brumbelow 2023; Hein 2023 — via site underride research).** GAO found underride fatalities "likely underreported" in FARS. Hein's audit of 40 verified fatal side underride crashes found 43% inaccurately coded or missing in FARS. IIHS estimated side guards could prevent 159–217 fatalities/year vs. NHTSA's 17.2. The database that measures the problem misses nearly half of it — which is why a jury verdict, not a federal statistic, is doing the counting here.

## Original analysis (for the article)
- **Apportionment math:** The $21.9M is the jury's total award, but the defendants were found 35% at fault (20% company + 15% driver). Under California comparative fault, the defendants' share of the award is roughly 35% of $21.9M, about $7.7M — $5.25M of the Gallegos award and about $2.4M of the Sanchez award. The headline number is the harm; the payable number is a third of it. State both, don't conflate them.
- **Timeline math:** Crash November 2019 → verdict November 2026. Seven years from crash to verdict; case filed 2021 (case number 21STCV42588); two-month trial. The guard that failed in 2019 was judged against a standard NHTSA strengthened in 2022 — the defense argued the old law was the excuse, but the trial evidence was non-compliance with the law as it stood.
- **The defense's concession:** "The laws are out of date" concedes the guard would not protect a modern sedan. The jury's answer was that an outdated floor is still a floor. That is the article's thesis in one line.

## Limitations
- The PR Newswire release is the plaintiffs' counsel's account; Republic Services/Consolidated Disposal has not been quoted on the verdict in the sources reviewed. The article must say so.
- The release does not detail the specific guard defect (dimensions, strength, mounting) — only that the guards "were not compliant with existing law." Do not invent a specific failure mode.
- The $21.9M is the jury award; post-trial motions, appeal, or settlement could change the final payment. Note that the number is the verdict, not a check.
- FARS underride figures are for side underride primarily; this crash was a rear underride. Do not import side-underride counts as if they describe rear impacts.

## Strongest counterargument
The driver of the Acura was found 65% responsible — the jury itself said the crash was mostly the teenagers' fault. A guard is a second-chance device: it only matters after someone has already made a mistake. The defense's underlying point has force: a 1953-era standard, even strengthened in 2022, was written for cars that no longer exist, and holding a hauler to it after a crash its driver mostly caused looks like deep-pocket justice. The article must carry this at full strength: the verdict prices a failure to mitigate, not a failure that caused the crash.

## Actionable takeaways
- If you drive a car, you cannot inspect a truck's ICC bar from your lane — but you can refuse to tailgate a truck with a visibly damaged, bent, or missing rear guard, and you can report it: a missing or damaged rear guard on a commercial truck is a reportable defect to the carrier and to FMCSA's National Consumer Complaint Database.
- If you run a fleet, this verdict is the cost of the "the standard is outdated" defense: $7.7M in apportioned liability, seven years of litigation, and a jury that heard the excuse and rejected it. Audit your guards against FMVSS 223/224 as written, not as you wish it were written.
- Check NHTSA's recall lookup for trailers: MAC 26V723 (2025–2027 liquid tank trailers missing rear guards) is open now, remedy free.
