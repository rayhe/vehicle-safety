A Software Bug Can Switch Off 37,901 Hyundais' Airbags. The Settlement Over the Last Airbag Computer Is Still Open.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 5, 2026

The airbag control unit is a small box with one job, and it has about 150 milliseconds to do it: decide whether the airbags fire. Hyundai just told federal regulators that a software error in that box can make the decision for it, before any crash happens, by switching the whole system off. Airbags. Seat belt pretensioners, both deactivated, on 37,901 cars. The only warning is a light on the dashboard.

37,901
Veloster N and Kona Electric cars whose airbags and pretensioners may not deploy

NHTSA campaign 26V728 covers certain 2019-2022 Veloster N and 2019-2023 Kona Electric cars, and Hyundai's internal number is 311.[1] A dealer will update the airbag control unit software free of charge. No over-the-air fix exists, so every car needs a service visit, and owner letters do not go out until January 1, 2027. The fastest way to know is a VIN lookup at nhtsa.gov/recalls starting November 6. No crashes or injuries tied to the defect appear in the public record. Read that as an absence of published data, not as proof of zero incidents.

Meanwhile, a federal judge approved a $62.1 million settlement last October over a different airbag control unit defect in Hyundais and Kias, and claims are still open until April 8, 2027.[2] That case alleged electrical overstress: a surge during a crash knocking the unit out at the exact moment the airbag should fire. Hyundai, Kia, and their supplier Mobis deny it. Owners split into two lists: recalled cars can claim expenses, a residual payment capped at $350, and a ten-year parts warranty, while unrecalled cars cap at $150, with no expenses and no warranty, and the unrecalled list is the longer one. It includes 2018-2023 Konas and 2019-2021 Velosters.

Now lay the two lists side by side. Three of the four Veloster model years in the new recall sit on the settlement's unrecalled list, and all five Kona Electric model years do, with one caveat: the settlement list says Kona, not Kona Electric, and eligibility is decided by VIN, not by model year, so treat the overlap as approximate and check your own car. Settlement approval came 391 days before this recall report, and the recall letters land 97 days before the claims deadline closes, which puts the same owners in the position of filing a claim about one airbag computer defect while opening a letter about another.

Fair objection, and it deserves its full weight: these are two different defects from two different eras, the old one is alleged and denied, the new one is a software bug with no reported injuries and a free fix that takes one service visit, and connecting them is guilt by component association. A reasonable person could call the overlap a coincidence of model names. That person would not be wrong about the engineering. They would be wrong about what it feels like to own the car, because the same dashboard light that warns you about the new defect is the light drivers have been trained for decades to ignore until the next oil change, and the same owners are now holding a settlement claim form in one hand and a recall letter in the other, for the same small box.

So check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls starting November 6, and do not wait for the January letter. If your airbag light is on, book the dealer now; the update is free, and a lit airbag light means the system may already be off. And if you owned or leased a Hyundai or Kia on April 14, 2025, run your VIN through acusettlement.com before April 8, 2027. Unrecalled-list owners can still claim up to $150 with nothing but a claim form, and the VIN lookup, not the model year, decides which list you are on.

Limitations: This is a defect story, not a fatality-rate story; no crash, injury, or fatality count is published for 26V728, and none is claimed here. The settlement's unrecalled list is defined by VIN, not model year, and several model years sit on both lists, so the model-year overlap is approximate. The ACU supplier for 26V728 is not named in the public record, and the article does not assert it is ZF-TRW. The software error's trigger conditions are not published: nobody knows what driving conditions deactivate the system, or how long a car can drive deactivated before the light comes on. The 391-day and 97-day figures are calendar arithmetic from published dates, not findings of causation.
