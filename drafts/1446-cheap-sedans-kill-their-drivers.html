When a Cavalier Crashes, Somebody Inside Dies. When a Ram 2500 Crashes, Somebody Else Does.

Vin Wreckage • Existential Dread Columnist
November 9, 2026

Every safety ranking you have ever read asks the wrong question. It asks how often a car gets into a fatal crash, measured per mile, and then it crowns the winner. But there is a second question hiding inside the same data, and it is the one that decides whose funeral you attend: when this car is in a fatal crash, who dies? Rank all 337 models in NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System by occupant deaths per fatal crash, and the American fleet splits cleanly in two. A Chevrolet Cavalier kills somebody inside it 0.857 times per fatal crash. A Ram 2500 does it 0.205 times. Same dataset, same decade, same roads. One car kills its driver. One car kills the other guy.[1]

Start at the top of the list, because it reads like a junkyard inventory: the Cavalier at 0.857, the Dodge Neon at 0.856, the Buick LeSabre at 0.823, the Chevrolet Cobalt at 0.808, and the Lincoln Town Car at 0.805. Twelve of the fifteen most lethal-per-crash models are sedans, and every single one of them is discontinued. Production ended between 2004 and 2011 for most of the list, with the Chevy Spark (0.744) as the newest corpse, dead since 2022. Nobody is buying these cars new. Millions of Americans are still driving them, because a running Cavalier costs about three thousand dollars, and three thousand dollars is what a first car costs when you are nineteen and broke.[1]

Now flip the list over: the Ram 2500 at 0.205, the Ford Transit van at 0.308, the Ram 1500 at 0.341, the GMC Acadia at 0.343, the Toyota Land Cruiser at 0.347, and the Ford F-250 at 0.364. Read those numbers the way the data means them: in roughly four out of five fatal crashes involving a Ram 2500, nobody inside the Ram dies. Somebody dies, or the crash would not be in this dataset at all. That somebody is in the other car, or on foot, or on a bicycle. A heavy pickup is a fortress with a battering ram attached, and the lethality ranking measures the fortress half. IIHS measured the other half four months ago, in its July 2026 driver death rate report, and found the Ram 3500 crew cab has the highest other-driver death rate of any current model: 204 deaths per million registered vehicle years, against a fleet average of 53.[2][3]

Two cars on this list deserve a closer look, because they prove the two metrics answer different questions. Pontiac's Grand Am has one of the lowest death rates per mile in the entire dataset, 1.18 per 100 million vehicle miles, a fact this site already celebrated. It also has the ninth-highest lethality per crash, 0.774. It almost never gets into a fatal crash, and when it does, somebody inside dies. Its mirror image is the Land Cruiser: a death rate of 6.27 per 100 million miles, among the highest anywhere, paired with a lethality of 0.347, among the lowest. Land Cruisers get into fatal crashes constantly, and their occupants usually walk away. If you only read the per-mile ranking, you would call the Grand Am safe and the Land Cruiser a deathtrap. Both verdicts are wrong, and both are also right. Depends which funeral you are trying to avoid.[1]

None of this is a pure crashworthiness score, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Cheap old sedans are driven by younger, poorer, less-belted drivers on worse roads, and they are more often the struck vehicle when a truck hits them. IIHS says the same about its own numbers: driver death rates reflect far more than crashworthiness. Single-vehicle crashes, run-off-road into a tree at midnight, inflate lethality for small cars regardless of what else is on the road. But the confound does not rescue the Cavalier. It explains the number without changing it. When a nineteen-year-old's first car is in a fatal crash, the odds say somebody inside that car dies, and the odds are four times worse than in the truck that hit them.[2]

So here is the choice the used market actually offers, stripped of the marketing. Three to five thousand dollars buys a sedan whose fatal crashes kill the person inside, or an older SUV or pickup whose fatal crashes kill the person outside. There is no option on the lot that kills nobody. There is only a decision about whose risk you are buying, and most buyers never know they are making it. Before you sign for a first car, look up the model in IIHS's driver death rate tables, check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, and ask the only question that matters: when this car crashes, who dies?
