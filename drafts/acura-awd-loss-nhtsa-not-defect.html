137,000 Acuras Lost All-Wheel Drive. NHTSA Says That's Not a Safety Problem.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 3, 2026

Christopher Mammarella filed a defect petition in January 2026 covering 137,329 Acura TLX sedans and MDX crossovers whose all-wheel-drive systems permanently fail.[1] Forty-eight owners reported the same failure: the interfacing splines between the transmission and the rear-drive power take-off unit shear apart, converting their SH-AWD vehicles into front-wheel-drive ones without a warning light, without gradual degradation, without any indication at all until the driver discovers their rear axle has been permanently disconnected from the powertrain.

44%
Reduction in SUV fatality rate from AWD, per IIHS 2024 data

On August 6, NHTSA denied the petition. The agency concluded that the reported failures "are not likely to result in a finding that a defect related to motor vehicle safety exists."[1] No crashes had been reported, no injuries, no fatalities, and so the petition was denied outright.

Except for one problem that the denial itself created.

IIHS published fatality data in 2024 showing that all-wheel-drive SUVs die at a rate of 22 per million registered vehicles versus 39 for their front-wheel-drive counterparts, a 44% reduction attributable to a single drivetrain variable.[2] NHTSA knows this. NHTSA funded the crash database that makes this calculation possible. FARS data from 2014 through 2023 places the Acura MDX at a fatality rate of 0.30 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, well below the SUV median of 0.43 and less than half the SUV mean of 0.79.[3] The TLX sits at 0.11 per 100 million VMT, second-safest sedan in the entire dataset behind only the Tesla Model 3. These are not average cars; they are statistically exceptional ones, and SH-AWD is standard equipment on every unit affected by this petition.

So the agency that co-publishes the data proving AWD reduces fatalities by nearly half has officially ruled that losing AWD does not constitute a safety problem. No one has to die for a feature to be safety-relevant; they just have to die without it at higher rates, which they already do. NHTSA has quantified the body count difference and then declined to act on it, which is not a bureaucratic oversight but a philosophical position: the absence of reported casualties from this specific defect overrides the statistical certainty that the lost capability prevents casualties in general.

Worth noting: the Acura TLX was discontinued in July 2025 because nobody was buying it.[4] Some of these 137,329 vehicles are now orphans, built by a manufacturer that no longer makes the model and cleared by a regulator that does not consider their drivetrain loss dangerous. If your MDX or TLX loses its SH-AWD, you have a front-wheel-drive vehicle that was never designed, tested, or marketed as one, operating with a drivetrain configuration that IIHS data associates with 44% more deaths in its vehicle class.

What you should do: If you own a 2021 through 2023 Acura TLX or a 2022 through 2023 MDX, check whether your AWD system is functioning: unusual vibrations, handling asymmetry on wet roads, or a complete loss of rear-wheel engagement are symptoms documented in the 48 complaints. File your own complaint at nhtsa.gov, because the agency dismissed 48 reports and forty-eight hundred would be considerably harder to ignore. Keep records for warranty claims; Honda's drivetrain warranty covers 60 months or 60,000 miles, and some owners have reported out-of-pocket repair costs exceeding $5,000.

Limitations: The 44% fatality reduction for AWD SUVs from IIHS data is a population-level statistic that does not isolate drivetrain from correlated factors including vehicle weight, price bracket, and driver demographics. Higher-income buyers who select AWD may also purchase vehicles with more advanced safety equipment and may engage in less risky driving behavior. The Acura MDX and TLX FARS rates reflect overall model performance across all drivetrain configurations, not a controlled comparison of AWD versus FWD within the same model. No crashes or injuries have been attributed to the SH-AWD spline failure itself, which limits any causal claim about imminent danger from this specific defect.

Strongest counterargument: Front-wheel drive is the default drivetrain configuration for the majority of passenger vehicles on American roads. Every Honda Civic, Toyota Corolla, and Hyundai Elantra operates as FWD without AWD, and NHTSA does not consider any of them defective for lacking it. An Acura that loses its SH-AWD reverts to the same drivetrain layout as the best-selling cars in the country. The owner paid for AWD and did not receive it, which is a consumer warranty issue, not a motor vehicle safety defect under 49 U.S.C. § 30101. The IIHS data showing 44% lower fatality rates for AWD SUVs correlates with factors beyond drivetrain alone, and treating correlation as causation would make every FWD vehicle on the road a de facto safety defect by comparison.
