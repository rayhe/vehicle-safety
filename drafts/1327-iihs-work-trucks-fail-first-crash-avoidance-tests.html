America's Work Trucks Failed Their First Crash-Avoidance Test. Not One Can See a Motorcycle.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 30, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better. On October 8, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety published its first-ever front crash prevention ratings for commercial vehicles, and not one of the four Class 3 heavy-duty pickups tested earned a Good rating in either the vehicle-to-vehicle or the pedestrian test.[1] These are the trucks that tow your boat, deliver your packages, and share every highway interchange with you at 70 mph. Their collision-avoidance systems cannot reliably see motorcycles, cannot reliably see children in daylight, and in at least one test, a 7,000-pound Ford plowed into a simulated passenger car at 43 mph without slowing down at all.

Some context before the scoreboard. IIHS launched its commercial vehicle assessment program in June 2026 and just applied the same protocols and the same good-acceptable-marginal-poor scale it uses on family cars.[2] Class 3 means a gross vehicle weight rating of 10,001 to 14,000 pounds, which covers the heavy-duty pickups contractors and fleets buy by the thousand. Here is how the pickups scored:

Now the part that should make every motorcyclist read this twice. IIHS evaluated forward collision warning and automatic braking against passenger-car and motorcycle targets at multiple speeds, plus warnings against a semitrailer.[1] Motorcycle detection was the single toughest challenge in the whole program. Several systems failed to issue warnings or slow down in most motorcycle trials. Not "warned late": the systems neither warned at all nor slowed at all. A motorcycle is a narrow, low-profile target, and the sensor stacks on these trucks appear tuned for the silhouette of a Camry, which is great news unless you ride something with two wheels.

Speed exposed the gap even more brutally: the F-350 could avoid a collision with a passenger-car target only at 31 mph. In four tests above that speed it failed, and in one 43-mph test it failed to reduce speed at all, which means a truck that size carrying a truck's momentum arrived at the impact point with essentially its full energy.[3] The Silverado 3500HD unsuccessfully avoided the passenger car in all six of its tests. All six. I checked three times because the first two times felt like a typo.

The Sprinter is the only vehicle in the entire program to earn a Good in the vehicle-to-vehicle test. The two pedestrian-test leaders are electric delivery vans, the BrightDrop 400 and the Rivian RCV-Delivery 500, which is exactly what you would predict: newer platforms, newer sensor suites, fewer excuses. At the bottom, the Express and Savana scored Poor in both tests because they do not have automatic emergency braking at all; buyers can spec an optional forward collision warning, which is roughly like offering an optional parachute.[2] And here is the data point that made me put my head in my hands: per reporting on the ratings, Chevrolet ended BrightDrop 400 production in October 2025, meaning one of the two vehicles to earn a Good pedestrian rating is discontinued, which means the good truck is dead and the bad trucks are in showrooms.[4] The good truck is dead. The bad trucks are in showrooms.

One more result deserves attention because it is genuinely strange. Several trucks had more difficulty detecting and avoiding a child dummy in daylight than an adult dummy at night. The F-350 and Ram 3500 both struggled more with the child in the daytime runs.[1] IIHS suggests the problem may be the combination of a short dummy and a very tall truck front end: the child drops below the sensor's confident detection zone in bright conditions where you would expect the best performance. Counterintuitive, reproducible, and exactly the kind of edge case that kills kids near schools and work zones, which is where these trucks spend their days.

of all 2024 US road deaths involved medium/heavy-duty vehicles or light vans: 6,215 people

Now the regulatory punchline. Per IIHS, citing NHTSA data, 6,215 people died in 2024 in crashes involving medium- or heavy-duty vehicles or light vans, about 16 percent of all US roadway fatalities.[5] The automatic emergency braking requirements that apply to new passenger cars do not apply to these commercial trucks and vans, so read that again slowly: the vehicles in one-sixth of fatal crashes are exempt from the braking rule written for the other five-sixths, and as of mid-October no manufacturer had announced a fix for the tested vehicles.[6] IIHS President David Harkey called the results "a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers." The data suggests the call has been ringing for a while and nobody picked up.

What happens next is at least structured: IIHS will keep rating Class 3 vehicles through this year and next, adding headlight and rear automatic braking ratings, and will phase in testing of larger Class 4 through 6 trucks later.[2] More tests, more tables, more numbers that will not get better on their own.

What this analysis does not prove: IIHS measured behavior under its own controlled protocol, not crash outcomes on public roads. There is no exposure denominator here, no crashes-per-mile for these specific vehicles, so a Poor rating is not a fatality forecast. The 6,215-death figure is fleet-wide context for all medium and heavy-duty vehicles plus light vans, not the eleven tested vehicles. The Express and Savana's failures are mostly the absence of a feature, not a broken one, and those vans are ancient fleet specials, not the trucks most of you drive. Test vehicles are model years 2025 through 2027, and manufacturers may have already changed sensors or software since.

The strongest counterargument: a test protocol is not the real world, and commercial buyers know it. Fleet operators care about uptime, payload, and price; nobody has shown that these IIHS ratings predict actual crash rates for these vehicles, and until someone runs that correlation, the ratings are a laboratory ranking, not an actuarial one. Manufacturers could also reasonably argue that a 14,000-pound truck stopping for a motorcycle target at 43 mph is a much harder physics problem than a 3,500-pound sedan doing the same, and the rating scale does not grade on a mass curve. Fair. But "harder physics" is an explanation, not an exoneration, and the motorcycle riders behind these trucks do not get a grading curve either.

What to do with this: If you spec fleet trucks, pull the IIHS commercial ratings before you sign. The Sprinter 2500 is currently the only Good vehicle-to-vehicle performer; the BrightDrop and Rivian lead on pedestrians. If you ride a motorcycle, ride like the truck behind you has no idea you exist, because the test data says some of them genuinely do not. If you manage a school zone or work zone with Class 3 traffic, know that child-size detection in daylight is a documented weak point, so separation beats signaling. And if you drive one of these trucks yourself, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, confirm whether your truck even has automatic emergency braking, and do not assume the federal mandate covered you, because it probably did not.
