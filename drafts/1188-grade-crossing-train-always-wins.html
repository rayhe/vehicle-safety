The Gap

286 Dead at Railroad Crossings Last Year. The Fix Is a Gate, Not a Slogan.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

Let's talk about the mile before the crossing, because that is where the crash actually happens. In 2025, trains and highway vehicles collided approximately 2,273 times at American grade crossings, killing 286 people and injuring 766, according to the Federal Railroad Administration.[1] A train doing 55 miles per hour needs about a mile, seventeen football fields, to stop after the engineer dumps the emergency brakes.[2] You cannot out-brake it, outrun it, or argue with it. Every one of those 2,273 collisions was a negotiation the driver was guaranteed to lose.

Your eyes are part of the problem. A locomotive's big flat front triggers a size-speed illusion: it looks slower and farther away than it is. Drivers pull onto the tracks imagining they have plenty of time to clear them. They do not. Add quiet zones, communities that legally silenced the horn, and at a passive crossing you get a crossbuck, a yield sign, and your own lying visual cortex standing between you and 200 tons of steel.

Passive versus active is the whole story. Colorado counts 960 passive crossings against 784 with lights or gates, and its own utility commission says the money to signalize crossings is limited.[2] A passive crossing is a design decision to let the driver's judgment do the work of a gate. In April, a 67-year-old Sumter County, Florida school bus driver allegedly drove onto the tracks against active warning signals with 29 students aboard, and the train clipped the bus. She was arrested on 29 counts of child neglect.[3] If a professional driver legally required to stop at every crossing can get this wrong, your judgment is not the safety system. The gate is.

September brought the 10th annual See Tracks? Think Train Week, with hundreds of agencies running Operation Clear Track enforcement at crossings nationwide.[1] Lovely. The collision count has sat at two thousand plus a year for as long as anyone has counted. A decade of education has not moved the number, because the number was never about awareness. It is about hardware. Every passive crossing converted to gates is a crossing where the illusion stops mattering.

The counterargument, because engineering deserves honesty: 286 deaths is under one percent of annual traffic fatalities, and most public crossings already have active warnings. Much of the residual toll is deliberate gate-running, which concrete and steel only partly deter. Signalizing every rural crossing would cost billions to protect intersections that see a dozen cars a day. Fair. But the crossings that kill people are disproportionately the ones with nothing but a crossbuck, and limited funding is a choice, not a law of physics.

If you drive: never race a gate and never stop on the tracks. If your car stalls on the rails, get out immediately; if a train is coming, run at a 45-degree angle away from the tracks, because debris travels along the rail line. At passive crossings: treat the crossbuck as a stop sign. Stop, look both ways, listen. If you live near a passive crossing with a history of near-misses, petition for active warnings. That documented history is what moves a crossing up the funding list.
