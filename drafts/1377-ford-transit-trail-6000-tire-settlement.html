Ford Shrunk the Transit Trail's Tires. Now It Owes Owners $6,000 a Van.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 3, 2026

Before you paid $65,975 for Ford's adventure van, the brochure made one promise above all others: big tires. Ford launched the Transit Trail in November 2022 as an extended high-roof Transit with a 3.5-inch lift, a wider track, skid plates, tow hooks, and 30.5-inch Goodyear all-terrain tires built for van life off pavement.[1] Then the tires turned out not to fit. Ford recalled all 1,902 Transit Trails in March 2024, and its free fix was to take the big tires away.[4]

$6,000
Per eligible van in the Transit Trail class-action settlement; claims due January 15, 2027

Under load, with the wheels turned and the brakes on, the 30.5-inch tires could contact the front wheel-arch liners and the body flange, wearing the tire toward rapid air loss or tread-belt separation.[4] Ford's remedy swapped the Goodyear Wrangler Workhorse AT tires, LT245/75R16E at 30.47 inches, for 235/65R16C tires at 28.03 inches, plus new placards, labels, and a recalibration.[5] Read that swap plainly: 10-ply E-rated rubber built to air down on a trail became 6-ply C-rated rubber, the same size a standard Transit ships with, and the van lost about 1.2 inches of tire-radius ground clearance in the exchange. Ford blamed a contractor that had not fully accounted for the front tire envelope.[5]

Now the downgrade has a price tag. In Provo v. Ford, a Delaware federal class action, Ford agreed to pay $6,000 per eligible vehicle to original owners and lessees who bought or leased a 2023 or 2024 Transit Trail before June 1, 2024 for personal, family, or household use and who received no prior compensation.[2] A judge gave preliminary approval on June 17, 2026, claims are open at FordTransitTrailSettlement.com, and the claim deadline is January 15, 2027, with a final approval hearing set for March 16, 2027.[2][3] Attorneys' fees and service awards come out of Ford's pocket separately, not out of the $6,000.[2]

Notice how much wider the money runs than the repair did. Ford expected only about 500 of the 1,902 vans to actually need the tire swap, and at recall time it knew of seven tire-rub reports with zero tire damage.[5] Yet every eligible original owner can claim, because the alleged harm was buying the promise, not just driving the rub. Run the totals: $6,000 is 9.1% of the van's $65,975 base price for its defining feature, and if every van claimed, the payout would reach $11.4 million before fees. For two years owners carried the smaller tires while Ford carried the contractor excuse. Only the settlement made owners whole.

What to do depends on which side of the deal you sit on. Eligible original owners should file at FordTransitTrailSettlement.com by January 15, 2027 with proof of ownership or lease, and should not wait for final approval, since the claim deadline arrives two months before the hearing.[2] Current owners of any 2023-2024 Trail should check the VIN for recall 24V226 completion: a van still on 30.5-inch tires is unremedied and still carries the rub risk, while a van on 28-inch tires is safe but shorter than advertised. Used buyers should ask which tires are fitted and whether the prior owner already claimed, since later buyers are not in the settlement class.[3]

What this analysis does not prove. Settlement approval is preliminary, not final, and no payment date had been announced as of October 24, 2026, so $6,000 is a promised payment on a valid timely claim, not cash in hand.[3] Safety risk here rested on potential, not observed failure: seven rub reports, no known tire damage at recall time. FARS data does not break out the Transit Trail trim, so this article claims no fatality rate for the van. Tire diameter conversions come from trade reporting of the tire designations, not from Ford's filing text reviewed firsthand.

Strongest counterargument. Six thousand dollars for a free tire swap is generous, not grudging. Smaller tires eliminated the rub, which was the actual safety hazard, and the van kept its lift, skid plates, tow hooks, and wider track, so calling it a base Transit overstates the loss by a wide margin. Most owners never loaded to the weight rating at 60% steering lock while braking, which means most never met the rub condition at all. On that view the settlement pays for a marketing disappointment rather than a safety injury, and the honest headline is that Ford bought peace at $6,000 a van for a defect that never hurt a tire.
