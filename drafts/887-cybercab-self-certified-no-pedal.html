Tesla Certified a Car With No Brake Pedal as Compliant. NHTSA Asked to See the Homework.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 11, 2026

On September 3, Tesla began charging Austin riders for trips in the Cybercab, a gold two-seater with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no mirrors. The company told NHTSA it had certified the car compliant with all applicable federal safety standards. That same day, NHTSA opened Audit Query AQ26002, covering an estimated 1,000 Cybercab vehicles, to examine the technical basis of that certification.[1][3]

1,000
Estimated Cybercab vehicles covered by NHTSA Audit Query AQ26002

American cars are self-certified. Manufacturers declare their own compliance with the Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards; NHTSA audits the paperwork when something looks odd.[2] Tesla's declaration rests on a legal argument the audit filing names explicitly: that certain FMVSS are simply inapplicable to a vehicle with no human controls. Which standards, and how a car without a brake pedal satisfies a brake-pedal standard, is the homework NHTSA now wants to see.[1]

Zoox already took this exam. In 2022 it self-certified its own control-less robotaxi, NHTSA issued a special order, then opened a formal audit query in 2023.[2] Four years later, in July 2026, Zoox won a Part 555 exemption, the first of its kind, which caps deployment at 2,500 vehicles per manufacturer per year.[6][3] That cap is the whole story. A taxi business cannot scale at 2,500 cars a year, which makes self-certification the only legal route that does not put a ceiling on the fleet. Tesla did not apply for an exemption. It certified, launched, and let NHTSA come to it.

NHTSA, meanwhile, is rewriting the rules it is enforcing. In June the agency proposed dropping the manual brake pedal requirement for self-driving vehicles, then added the fine print: existing standards remain in force until the new ones are finalized.[4] Tesla launched into that gap, between a rule that exists and a rule that is being erased. One more number for the ledger: Texas records show 45 Cybercabs registered in the state as of the probe's announcement, against a federal audit population estimated at 1,000.[3] The audit is not a recall, not a finding of non-compliance, and no injury has been publicly tied to the Cybercab.[5] It is an argument about paperwork that will decide whether the Cybercab business is legal at scale.

If you take a Cybercab in Austin, the ride is legal today and its compliance basis is unresolved, worth knowing rather than worth panicking over. Watch whether NHTSA routes Tesla through Part 555; that path caps the fleet at 2,500 a year, which is where the expansion math dies. And track the June rulemaking on manual controls: it is the long game, and it decides whether any pedalless car is legal in America, not just this one.
