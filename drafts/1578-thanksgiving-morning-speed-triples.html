Thanksgiving Morning Is the Fastest Three Hours on American Roads

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 25, 2026

Somewhere around 7 AM tomorrow, while the turkey is still in the fridge and the parade is still warming up, the fastest three hours on American roads will begin. Allstate counted more than 44 million Drivewise trips from last Thanksgiving and found the share of miles driven at 80 mph or faster nearly tripled between 6 and 9 AM on Thanksgiving morning, not doubled but tripled.[1] And the roads were empty.

Roughly how much the share of miles at 80 mph or faster rose on Thanksgiving morning, 6–9 AM

Between 6 and 9 AM, high-speed driving rose nearly 200 percent against a typical Thursday morning, then stayed more than 100 percent above normal straight through lunch. Trips of 100 miles or more doubled on Wednesday and again on Thanksgiving Day itself. One trip in five on Thanksgiving Day stacked multiple risky behaviors, and more than 90 percent of those paired speeding, defined as 15 mph over the limit, with phone handling.[1] Do the multiplication. Roughly 18 percent of all Thanksgiving Day trips in the dataset combined a speeding car with a driver looking at a phone. Wednesday night set the table: trips rose more than 40 percent between 9 PM and 3 AM, and the Monday after the holiday was Allstate Roadside's second-busiest day of 2025, with service calls 52 percent above average.[1]

Empty roads are the whole trick, and the trick works on arithmetic. Allstate's headline number is a share of miles, not a headcount of speeders, so a highway with almost nobody on it will show a soaring high-speed share even if the absolute number of fast cars barely moves. Fewer cars means fewer reasons to slow down. So the advice everyone gives, leave early and beat the traffic, drops you into the fastest window of the year. South Carolina led the state table at plus 151 percent, with New York at 149, Texas at 139, North Carolina at 129, and Florida at 123, a ranking that tracks the states where the interstates run long and straight and the holiday traffic thins out fastest, which is another way of saying the speed map is really an emptiness map.[1] Black Friday kept the pattern going, with high-speed driving up more than 80 percent between 6 and 9 AM on about half the usual number of trips.[1]

None of this makes Thanksgiving deadlier per day than an ordinary week, which is the part the reputation gets wrong. Work backward from NHTSA's holiday tables. The five Thanksgivings from 2020 to 2024 produced about 500 deaths apiece, roughly 110 a day across the four-and-a-half-day window, against 107 a day for all of 2024.[2] What changes is the mix: in a NHTSA tabulation prepared for MADD, half of the people who died on Thanksgiving Day 2024, 65 of 131, were killed in crashes involving a drunk driver, against a 30 percent national baseline for the year.[2] Of the 286 passenger-vehicle occupants killed over Thanksgiving weekend 2024, 45 percent were unbelted, a share that barely differs from the full-year figure, which is the quiet scandal inside the holiday numbers: the belt problem is not a Thanksgiving problem, it is an every-day problem that the holiday merely concentrates into four and a half days.[2]

Before anyone prints this on a bumper sticker, the case against the panic deserves its full hearing. Allstate measured behavior, not crashes, and its sample is customers who opted into being tracked, a younger, phone-carrying, discount-chasing slice of drivers, not America. Part of the tripling is mechanical: an empty interstate at dawn will always show a high speed share, and plenty of those 80-mph miles happened on wide, dry, straight highways where the crash risk per mile is low. Thanksgiving morning might be mostly harmless, a fast drive on an empty road, the same thing that happens every Sunday at dawn, and the report never ties a single one of those fast miles to a single crash, which means the scariest number in this piece is a behavior count dressed up as a body count, and the honest headline would be that people drive fast when the road is empty, a fact any commuter could have told you for free. Fair enough, and that caveat stays attached to every number in this piece. The National Safety Council estimates 428 people will die on the roads this Thanksgiving holiday period, with a 90 percent confidence interval of 372 to 487, and about 160 lives saved because occupants wore belts.[3]

Still, the overlap is the thing to fear, because speed and phone use did not show up separately. Set the cruise control before the highway empties out. Put the phone out of reach before the car moves. Treat Wednesday night like the drinking holiday it is: half of Thanksgiving Day's dead in 2024 involved alcohol, and the night before is when the bars fill. Buckle up in the back seat too, check tire pressure cold before you load the car, and run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before a 100-mile drive in a car you have not checked in a year.[4]
