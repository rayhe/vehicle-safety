Your Harley Says Neutral. It Is Lying.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

Green light on the dash, bike in gear, and those two facts are not supposed to coexist, yet on 21,643 brand-new 2026 Harleys they can.[1] Harley-Davidson told NHTSA on November 3 that the engine control module on nine touring models may report neutral when the transmission is actually in gear, and as of October 27 the company was already aware of two injuries potentially related to the defect.[2]

Picture the scene, because it is painfully ordinary. You thumb the starter in your garage, the dash says N, and you let the clutch out expecting nothing to happen, while an 838-pound motorcycle with a 105-horsepower Milwaukee-Eight 117 lurches forward into a wall, a gas pump, a curb, or you.[3] Nobody died. That is luck, not design.

21,643
2026 Harley-Davidson touring motorcycles recalled under NHTSA campaign 26V-730, all nine models from the Street Glide to the police Road Glide.

Break the failure down and it gets worse, because there is no backup anywhere in the chain. A gear position sensor talks to the engine control module, the module talks to the dash, and you trust the dash. Nobody in that chain checks anything against the actual gearbox, which means the green N on your instrument cluster is not a measurement. It is a rumor passed down a wire, and on the affected bikes the rumor is wrong.

Somebody noticed. Not an engineer, not a regulator, a magazine reviewer on a routine road test. CycleTrader published its 2026 Road Glide review on July 30, and right there in the complaints section sits this sentence: the screen "displays the bike being in neutral at start-up regardless of whether it is in gear or not," and starting the bike in gear "could make for an exciting and expensive situation."[4] Three months before the recall filing, in print, in a buyer's guide, a defect a reviewer can see by riding the bike and looking at the dash took until November to become a federal recall, which raises a question Harley has not answered: who else saw it, and when?

Now run Harley's own numbers, because they do not sit comfortably together. Harley estimates that less than 1 percent of the 21,643 bikes are actually defective, which works out to fewer than 216 motorcycles.[2] Two injuries are already potentially related. If the estimate is right, that is roughly one injury for every hundred defective bikes, a strikingly high hit rate for a failure that only bites when a rider starts the bike in gear with the clutch out. Either the defect estimate is optimistic, or this particular failure is unusually likely to hurt someone when it happens, and both readings are bad.

One more detail from the population list deserves a pause. The FLTRXP is in there, 329 bikes, and that is the police-package Road Glide, the motorcycle ridden by motor officers, the riders who train hardest on low-speed control and clutch discipline.[2] Training does not fix a sensor. Neither does experience, which is exactly why a lying neutral light is different from an ordinary defect: it defeats the habits that are supposed to protect you.

Zoom out and a pattern forms: this is the fourth distinct recall wave on the same 2026 touring line in a single year. Recall 0191 covered a Sportster S triple clamp in February. Recall 0192 covered a Softail rear brake line in April, 16,994 bikes. Recall 0193 covered an airbox breather port that could eject hot oil, 88,039 bikes, also April. Now 0194, the gear sensor, in November.[5] Four systems, four failure modes, one flagship model line, one launch year. That is not a story about Harley building bad bikes. It is a story about a flagship line that had four separate things go wrong in its first year on the road, and the fourth one was visible in a magazine in July.

The counterargument, at full strength: less than 1 percent of these bikes are actually defective, the fix is a free sensor swap at any dealer, and nobody has died. A neutral indicator was never a safety interlock. Experienced riders start a bike with the clutch in and a brake covered, and a rider who does that is protected whether the light lies or not. By that account this is a precautionary recall handled the way recalls are supposed to be handled, and the two injuries are "potentially related," not proven. That reading has real weight, especially on the remedy: a dealer visit, a new sensor, done. But it does not answer the July review, and it does not change the fact that the one instrument a new rider trusts most is the one that lies.

What this does not prove: the two injuries are "potentially related" per Harley, not confirmed as caused by the defect, and no crash count was published, so do not write this up as two confirmed defect injuries. Harley's sub-1-percent estimate is Harley's own number, unverified by NHTSA. One reviewer on one press bike establishes that the defect was observable, not that every bike in the population shows it at startup. And there is no FARS model-level data for 2026 Harleys, so this is a recall story, not a fatality-rate story; motorcycle deaths overall actually fell 2.2 percent in 2024.[6]

What This Means for You

If you own a 2026 Street Glide, Road Glide, Street Glide Limited, Road Glide Limited, or any of the nine listed models, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls or call Harley at 1-800-258-2464 and reference recall 0194. Do not wait for the owner letter, which is not expected until November 16. Until the sensor is replaced, start the bike with the clutch pulled in and a brake covered every single time, no matter what the dash says. Treat the green N as advisory, not fact. Buying a used 2026 Harley touring bike? Confirm recalls 0194 and 0193, the airbox recall, were both completed, and ask for the service record instead of taking the seller's word. And the broader lesson applies to every bike you will ever ride: a neutral indicator is a convenience display, not a safety system. The clutch lever is the interlock.
