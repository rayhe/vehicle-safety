# Research: Continental's $931 Million Summer at the Test Track

Slug: `continental-931m-test-track-verdicts`
Journalist: Vin Wreckage (Existential Dread / Investigation)
Number: 1132

## The story

In July 2023, a group of kids from a Detroit-area STEM summer camp (DAPCEP — Detroit Area Pre-College Engineering Program) visited a test track in Auburn Hills, Michigan, owned by Continental Automotive Systems Inc. Continental's test driver, Linus Gugino, was demonstrating a Ford Mustang Dark Horse prototype on loan from Ford. Plaintiffs allege the driver deliberately spun the car out of control — drifted it — to wow the children standing trackside. The car snapped sideways, slammed into the guardrail where the kids were standing, bounced back onto the track, and landed on its roof.

Injured: Elijah Gibson (8 — leg fractures, deep foot laceration, brain injury) and Lavell McGee (10 — leg and head injuries), both with ongoing memory problems, per the 2024 lawsuit filing. A third child from the Walker family was also injured. Vincent Gibson, the father, filed a bystander claim.

Defendants in the original suit: Continental, Ford, DAPCEP, and the driver. The summer-2026 trials proceeded against Continental, which admitted liability in each case — so all three were damages-only trials before Judge David Cohen in Oakland County Circuit Court.

## The verdicts

- July 27, 2026: $896,467,970 for the brain-injured child. Believed to be the largest single-plaintiff verdict in Michigan history. Continental's own trial team had suggested $10.5 million.
- August 4, 2026: $2,066,000 for Vincent Gibson's bystander claim. Believed to be the largest bystander-claim verdict in Michigan history.
- September 11, 2026: ~$32.7 million for the third child. Case captioned Walker v. Continental Automotive Systems Inc., Case No. 2024-209675-NO, Oakland County Circuit Court.
- Total: $931,233,970 — roughly $931 million. All three trials were recorded gavel-to-gavel by Courtroom View Network.

Continental made zero settlement offers at any point, according to plaintiff attorney Jon Marko (Marko Law PLLC). Marko's explanation, told to CVN: a disconnect between Continental's trial team and its German insurers — $450 million in available coverage with Allianz as primary carrier — plus the assistant general counsel who handled the case leaving the company. Judge Cohen sanctioned Continental $1 million for lying about insurance limits. Marko noted the sanction already changed behavior in other Michigan cases ("defense suddenly 'found' more insurance policies").

## Novel contribution (original analysis)

The verdict-to-defense-offer ratio. Continental's trial team suggested $10.5 million as the right number for a child with a catastrophic brain injury. The jury returned $896.5 million — 85 times the company's own suggestion. Across all three trials the combined awards total $931.2 million, roughly double the $450 million in insurance coverage Marko says Continental carried. Admitting liability and then fighting only on damages, with zero settlement offers, turned a catastrophic-injury case into the largest single-plaintiff verdict in Michigan history. The $1 million sanction for hiding insurance limits is the footnote that should scare every corporate defendant more than the verdicts.

The deeper irony for a vehicle-safety site: Continental is a brake-and-chassis supplier. The failure mode here wasn't a brake. It was crowd management at the company's own test facility — a 500-horsepower prototype intentionally slid next to schoolchildren standing behind a guardrail. The safety company forgot the most basic safety rule in its own house: setback distance.

## Limitations

- Verdict amounts come from the plaintiff's press release and CVN's reporting; rounded to the dollar in the press release ($896,467,970, $2,066,000, ~$32.7M). No independent audit of the injury or damages claims.
- Marko himself concedes the "sticker shock" element could get the $896M award trimmed on appeal. These numbers are verdicts, not payments.
- Plaintiff-framed sourcing dominates: Continental declined to comment to CVN. The deliberate-drift allegation is the plaintiffs' characterization; the driver and Continental did not contest liability, so the mechanics were never relitigated.
- "Largest verdict in Michigan history" is Marko's belief, not a court finding.

## Strongest counterargument

These were damages-only trials: Continental admitted liability, so no jury ever ruled that the Mustang was defective, that Continental's brakes failed, or that any vehicle was unsafe. The $931M figure measures the lifetime cost of a child's catastrophic brain injury plus a jury's anger at a defendant that offered nothing — it is not a finding about cars. Ford, which loaned the prototype, was a co-defendant in the original suit but the trials that produced these numbers were against Continental alone. A reader could fairly say: this is a story about litigation strategy and insurance communication, not vehicle safety. The rebuttal: a test-track operator choosing to drift a prototype next to schoolchildren IS a vehicle-safety decision, and the sanction for hiding insurance limits is a data point about how the industry handles accountability.

## Actionable takeaways

- If you organize or attend vehicle demonstrations: crowd setback distance is the single cheapest safety device in motorsport, and it costs nothing. The allegation here is that excitement was the point. Excitement is the hazard.
- Parents: field trips to automotive facilities have no federal crowd-safety standard. Ask where the kids stand.
- For the industry: admitting liability and refusing to make any settlement offer, while your insurers aren't in the loop, is how $10.5M becomes $896.5M.
