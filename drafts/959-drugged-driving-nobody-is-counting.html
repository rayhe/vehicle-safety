43% of Impaired Drivers in Fatal Crashes Are on Drugs. Nobody Is Counting Them.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 21, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, 43.2% of impaired drivers in fatal crashes between 2014 and 2023 tested positive for drugs. Not alcohol plus drugs. Drugs. I ran the FARS toxicology tables myself: 490,736 drivers in fatal crashes, 98,348 with any impairment, and 42,496 of them drug-positive. That is 86.6 drug-positive drivers for every 1,000 fatal crashes, and it is almost certainly an undercount, because drug testing is the least reliable measurement in the entire federal crash database.[1]

43.2%
Share of impaired drivers in FARS fatal crashes (2014-2023) who tested positive for drugs: 42,496 of 98,348

The alcohol numbers get a breathalyzer, a courtroom, and a Mothers Against Drunk Driving chapter in every county. Drugs get nothing. An NTSB analysis found that only four states and the District of Columbia drug-tested more than 60% of fatally injured drivers in 2020. Everybody else is flying blind, which is how you end up with a country where the drugged-driving death count is whatever the coroner felt like measuring.[5]

NHTSA tried to measure it properly, and to its credit it recruited seven Level 1 trauma centers and medical examiner offices to blood-test 7,279 seriously or fatally injured road users for alcohol plus legal, prescription, and illegal drugs. What came back: 55.8% tested positive for something potentially impairing, with cannabinoids leading at 25.1% with alcohol just behind at 23.1%, stimulants at 10.8%, opioids at 9.3%. Among drivers who were killed and tested by medical examiners, the numbers were uglier still: 67.7% positive for any drug, alcohol at 35.8%, cannabinoids at 28%, opioids at 15.3%. And 32% of the fatalities had two or more drugs in their system, which is the toxicology equivalent of a cocktail.[2][3][6]

18%
Injured drivers in a UC Davis/NHTSA surveillance study with sedating medications in their blood, vs 10% with alcohol and 9% with cannabis

Now the UC Davis twist, because this is where it stops being a story about illicit drugs and starts being a story about your medicine cabinet. Researchers at UC Davis Medical Center tested 490 drivers injured in Sacramento-area crashes as part of a California surveillance system funded by the state Office of Traffic Safety through NHTSA. Forty percent had a potentially impairing substance in their blood. Alcohol: 10%. Cannabis: 9%. Sedating medications, the prescription and over-the-counter kind, the sleep aids and the cold medicine and the stuff with the tiny warning label: 18%. Your NyQuil is beating the breathalyzer crowd by nearly two to one.[4] These are legal substances taken in ordinary doses, showing up in crash victims at higher rates than the two drugs the entire impaired-driving apparatus is built to catch.

And the apparatus, such as it is, is being dismantled. Since 2025, NHTSA's impaired-driving division has gone from seven staff to two. Its behavioral research team, the one that studied impaired driving, went from 16 people to three or four. In 2021, Congress set aside $750 million in the infrastructure law to modernize crash-data programs, and as of this year more than $475 million of it sat unused and expired in September. Back in 2022 the NTSB recommended a comprehensive national drugged-driving dataset, then followed it with a recommendation for an interim trauma-center surveillance system while the states got their act together. NHTSA reported some headway in 2023. Then it never followed up.[5]

This is the part where someone says drug presence is not drug impairment, and they are right, and it is also the point. THC metabolites linger for weeks. Prescription opioids show up after legitimate medical use. There is no 0.08 equivalent for cannabis, let alone for diphenhydramine. That counterargument is not wrong; it happens to be the strongest argument for the research that is being defunded. If presence is a bad proxy for impairment, the fix is better impairment science, not no science. What we are doing instead is measuring alcohol with exquisite precision and treating everything else as a rounding error, in a dataset where the rounding error is nearly half of all impaired drivers.

Consider Mesa County, Colorado, one of the rare places where a coroner actually tracks this stuff. From 2017 through 2024, a third of traffic deaths involved alcohol alone. Factor in drugs and combinations, and nearly half involved an impaired driver. Those two fractions, and the gap between them, are the national blind spot, measured in one county that bothered to look.[5]

The sports-stats version: alcohol is the veteran franchise player with a guaranteed contract, and drugs are the free agent class nobody is scouting. In the four geographical samples the NTSB examined, about half of drivers arrested for impaired driving and more than a quarter of drivers killed in crashes tested positive for multiple substances. Cocaine, sedatives, antidepressants, plus the legal stuff. Every year we publish a leaderboard with the drunk-driving fatality count. It is a partial scorecard printed by a league that refuses to hire statisticians.[5]

Strongest case against this article: the drug numbers are softer than the article's tone implies, and softness runs in the direction of overstatement. A positive THC screen three weeks after use says nothing about impairment at crash time; lumping it into the same column as a 0.15 BAC is category fraud. Prescription-drug positives include patients taking legal doses for real conditions, and it is entirely possible that some share of the 18% sedating-medication positives at UC Davis were incidental, people who took a cold medicine that morning and crashed for unrelated reasons. My 43.2% FARS figure mixes jurisdictions with wildly different testing regimes, and since fatal-crash drug panels are often only ordered when police already suspect impairment, the denominator is biased toward finding drugs. A fairer statement of the evidence is that drugs are common in crash victims and badly measured, not that drugs cause 43% of impaired-driving deaths.

What this analysis cannot prove: FARS drug testing is incomplete and non-uniform across states, so the 43.2% figure is a floor computed over a biased sample, not a population rate. That trauma-center study covered seven sites and is not nationally representative; medical-examiner cases skew toward deaths, which skews positivity upward. The UC Davis data came from a single Sacramento-area center, n=490 injured drivers, one year of collection. Positive for a drug does not equal impaired by it, and no per-se impairment threshold exists for most drug categories, so drug and alcohol positives cannot be equated. Those staffing and funding figures come from reporting on NHTSA's internal state; the agency's public position is that it has the resources it needs.

What you can actually do: open your medicine cabinet and read the tiny labels. Opioids, benzodiazepines, sleep aids, and sedating antihistamines all carry "do not drive or operate machinery" warnings for a reason, and unlike alcohol there is no legal per-se limit for any of them, which means impairment is judged by an officer's field assessment, not a number. If you take prescription sedatives, ask your pharmacist specifically about driving; do not assume the label is boilerplate. If you are driving after any impairing substance, alcohol included, the roadside test is the least of your worries: it is the crash that does the testing. And the next time you see a drunk-driving fatality statistic, remember that it is a subset of a larger number nobody is paid to compute.
