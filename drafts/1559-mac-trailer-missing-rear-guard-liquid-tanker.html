The Guard Was Never There: 60 Liquid Tankers Shipped Without Rear Underride Protection

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

Most recalls start with a part that broke. A weld cracked, a sensor lied, a battery caught fire. This one starts with a part that was never there at all. On October 30, 2026, MAC Trailer told NHTSA that 60 of its 2025-2027 liquid tank trailers may be missing their rear impact guards, the steel bar that keeps a passenger car from sliding underneath the trailer in a rear-end crash.[1] Not damaged. Not defective. Missing. Federal law has required that bar since 1998, and a 2022 upgrade made the requirement stronger. Somebody built 60 tankers anyway, and shipped them.

Speed at which a missing rear underride guard protects a car. The upgraded federal standard requires protection at 35 mph

Run the dates and the story gets worse. Production ran from June 26, 2024 to August 4, 2026, a span of 769 days, which works out to roughly one guard-less tanker leaving the factory every 13 days for more than two years.[1] NHTSA’s upgraded standard for rear impact guards, FMVSS 223 and 224, took effect for trailers built on or after July 15, 2024.[2] Nearly every trailer in this recall was built after that compliance date. In other words, these tankers rolled off the line after the government raised the bar, and they cleared a bar of zero, because there was nothing to test. A guard that exists and fails is a defect. A guard that was never installed is a different animal. It means nobody checked, or nobody noticed, for 25 months.

What the bar is supposed to do is brutally simple. A trailer floor sits high enough that a car’s bumper, crumple zone, airbags, and seat-belt tensioners all pass underneath it. Without a guard, the trailer floor meets the windshield header and the occupants’ heads. IIHS proved the stakes in 2011 by crashing a midsize sedan into the rear of parked trailers at 35 mph: a guard built only to the old US standard failed catastrophically at its attachment points, let the car slide under, and put the trailer in contact with the dummy’s head.[3] That was a guard that existed and still failed. MAC’s 60 trailers skipped the failing step entirely. NHTSA has estimated that about 423 people in passenger vehicles die each year when their vehicles strike the backs of large trucks, and IIHS’s analysis of federal crash data found underride in nearly 80 percent of the 115 rear-strike crashes it examined, with severe or catastrophic underride damage in 23 of the 28 fatal cases.[3] Apply the 80 percent share to the 423 annual deaths and roughly 340 people a year die in crashes where a car slides under the back of a truck. That is the crash this guard exists to prevent.

Credit where it is due: MAC reported the problem, filed the recall, and is installing the missing bumpers free of charge. Owner letters go out December 29, 2026.[1] Sixty trailers is also small. America builds roughly 260,000 new trailers a year, so 60 is a rounding error, and a reader could fairly call this a story about a recall system working as designed. Fair, and also beside the point. Recall systems catch failures after the fact. Quality control is supposed to make this particular failure impossible, because the guard is not a component with a failure rate. It is a bar bolted to the back of a trailer, visible to anyone standing behind the vehicle. A missing guard is the kind of defect a walk-around inspection catches in ten seconds, which raises the only question that matters: how does a federally mandated, plainly visible safety device go missing from 60 trailers over two years without anyone on the line, at the dealer, or at the fleet noticing? NHTSA’s public summary does not say. MAC’s Part 573 report, filed November 11, 2026, does not explain the mechanism in the public record either. Production omission, supplier shortfall, paperwork error: pick a theory, because the record offers none of them.

Limitations, stated plainly. No crashes, injuries, or deaths have been tied to these 60 trailers in the public record, so the risk is prospective, not realized. Sixty is small, and nothing here supports an industry-wide claim. These are liquid tank trailers, which haul liquid cargo, but the recall record says nothing about what any specific trailer carried, so do not picture 60 fuel tankers in particular. Picture 60 trailers with an open gap where the guard belongs. Finally, the death math above is an estimate built on estimates: the 423 figure is NHTSA’s, the 80 percent share comes from a 115-crash sample in the Large Truck Crash Causation Study, and the one-every-13-days rate assumes even production across the window. All three are illustrative. None of them is measured.

What to do with all of this. If you run a fleet or own a 2025-2027 MAC liquid tank trailer, check the VIN now at nhtsa.gov/recalls against campaign 26V723000, and do not wait for the December 29 letter.[1] If you drive a car, the lesson is broader and it costs nothing: a trailer’s rear guard is the only thing between your windshield header and a steel floor in a rear-end crash, so if you cannot see a guard bar hanging below a trailer’s rear, do not tailgate it. That advice holds for every trailer on the road, not just these 60. Rear guards are the one direction of underride that federal law actually covers. Side guards still have no federal standard at all. Rear was supposed to be the solved problem, and 60 tankers just proved it is not solved. It is only regulated, which turns out to be a different thing.
