Toyota Recalled the Prius Twice for Rear Doors That Open While You Drive. The First Fix Wasn’t One.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 1, 2026

The fifth-generation Prius is the prettiest one Toyota has ever built, and every reviewer said so. Sleek. Low. Finally interesting. It also shipped with flush electronic rear door handles that rain can short-circuit, causing an unlocked rear door to open while the car is moving. Toyota has now recalled these cars for this defect twice.[1] And if your Prius was fixed under the first recall, brace yourself: the second recall replaces the first, and you have to go back.

55,700 → 141,286
Vehicles in the defective population: the 2024 recall vs. the 2026 recall that replaced it. The population more than doubled while the first “fix” was in the field.

Start with the timeline. April 2024: Toyota files recall 24V274 on 55,700 Priuses and Prius Primes after water got into the electronic rear door opener switches and shorted them. The dealer notice was blunt: if the doors are not locked, they could open while the vehicle is moving or in a crash.[2] The remedy was to replace both rear door opener switches with improved ones, and Toyota stop-sold about 1,800 cars sitting on dealer lots. Twenty-one months later, Toyota files recall 26V049: 141,286 vehicles, 2023 through 2026 model years, expanding and replacing 24V274.[1] Every vehicle repaired under the first recall has to have the new remedy performed. Read that again. The repair you sat in a waiting room for is being recalled.

The paperwork hides the interesting part in plain sight: the remedy changed type. In 2024, Toyota replaced the switch, a parts-level fix aimed at the seal. In 2026, dealers modify the rear door switch circuits so a short cannot command an opening, a logic-level fix aimed at the failure mode.[1] The trigger was a February 2025 field report from Japan of a rear door opening to half-latch while driving, on a car inside the original recall population.[5] So the first fix sealed the water path. The second admits the real defect was never the water. It was a latch that interprets a short circuit as an instruction.

This is the structural point, and it goes beyond Toyota. A mechanical latch has exactly one way to open: somebody pulls the handle. An electronic latch puts three things between your back-seat passengers and the road: a switch, a circuit, and firmware that treats a short as an open command. When the fifth-gen Prius launched, those flush handles were a design scorecard win. Nobody at the design review asked what happens when the switch gets wet and the firmware can’t tell a short from a finger. A door should never be openable by weather. Full stop.

So here is the consumer part, and it is worth more than the indignation. If you own a 2023–2026 Prius, Prius Prime, or Prius Plug-In Hybrid: punch your VIN into nhtsa.gov/recalls and look for 26V049, Toyota internal number 26TB03. If your car was remedied under the 2024 campaign, you are not done, and a Carfax line that says “recall completed” from 2024 means the interim fix. Verify 26V049 specifically. Until the dealer modifies your circuits, turn on automatic door locking in the vehicle settings; a locked door cannot be shorted open, and Toyota says so itself.[3] Shopping used? Any 2023–2026 Prius with only the 2024 remedy on record is still driving with the old circuit.

In fairness, and the fairness matters: Toyota’s own estimate is that about 1 percent of the recalled vehicles actually have the defect, and the filings I reviewed document no injuries and no crashes, only the risk of injury.[5] The company recalled voluntarily, twice, and electronic latches are on millions of vehicles across the industry with failure rates this small. I also don’t have Toyota’s internal count of post-2024-remedy failures; the replacement of 24V274 is strong circumstantial evidence the first fix was insufficient, not a published number. But weigh it honestly. A door is not a taillight. Toyota paid to fix 55,700 cars, then paid again on 141,286, because its first remedy addressed the seal and the defect lived in the circuit. That is not the system working. That is the system taking two tries to find the part it broke.
