Congress Ordered Every New Car to Be a Breathalyzer by 2024. The Breathalyzer Doesn’t Exist.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 19, 2026

According to the federal statute books, your next new car was supposed to refuse to start if you were drunk. Section 24220 of the 2021 infrastructure law told NHTSA to require passive impaired-driving prevention technology in every new passenger vehicle, with a final rule due November 15, 2024.[1] That deadline came and went like a barfly’s promise. As of June 2026, NHTSA has published no proposed rule and no final rule, and its own report to Congress admits the sensor this law requires does not exist at anything close to the accuracy a mandate would demand.[2][3]

219,000,000
wrongful immobilizations per year if the mandated sensor ran at 99.9% accuracy, the threshold NHTSA itself calls unacceptable

NHTSA put the problem in writing to Congress, and the sentence is worth reading twice. Even a 99.9 percent detection accuracy, the agency wrote, "could result in millions to tens of millions of instances each year where the technology would incorrectly prevent or limit drivers from operating their vehicles."[2] I ran their threshold through public inputs so you can see the shape of it. America has roughly 240 million licensed drivers, each starting a car about 2.5 times a day, which comes to around 219 billion ignition events a year. At 99.9 percent accuracy, one in a thousand of those goes wrong: about 219 million wrongful lockouts annually. Push the sensor to 99.99 percent, an accuracy no commercial device claims, and you still get roughly 22 million sober drivers stranded per year.[2] Nobody in the privacy debate talks about this math, which is odd, because it is the math that makes the rule technically unwriteable.

Meanwhile the body count the law was written for keeps growing. In 2024, 11,904 people died in crashes where at least one driver was alcohol-impaired, thirty percent of all traffic fatalities, or one death every 44 minutes.[4] Fatal collisions involving an alcohol-impaired driver run at triple the rate after dark, and the heaviest overrepresentation sits with drivers aged 21 to 24.[4][5] The Driver Alcohol Detection System for Safety program has been chasing a passive sensor since 2008, eighteen years, pursuing a breath sensor in the steering column and an infrared touch sensor that reads blood alcohol through skin, and the current status of both is "still in review."[6] Eighteen years. The iPhone was three generations old when they started.

Politics, because no safety law is complete without it: Rep. Thomas Massie’s amendment to defund the mandate failed 164 to 268 in January, so the law stands.[7] Massie warned the dashboard becomes "your judge, your jury, and your executioner."[8] MADD, which helped draft the law, insists the only purpose is preventing deaths and opposes any system that collects, stores, or sells driver data.[8] Robert Strassburger of the automaker consortium building the tech says the data would "never leave the vehicle" and calls "kill switch" hyperbolic.[8] Everyone is arguing about surveillance, and the surveillance machine cannot yet tell a sober Tuesday commute from a drunk Saturday night. The privacy debate is a sequel to a movie that has not been filmed.

Three things you can do while Congress waits on physics. First, stop waiting on the mandate: the technology that actually works today is the unglamorous ignition interlock, which AAA estimates can cut fatal impaired-driving crashes by up to 26 percent among offenders, so if someone you care about has a DUI, push for the interlock instead of a newer model year.[9] Second, look at your state legislature instead of Washington: dropping the per se limit from 0.08 to 0.05 BAC is estimated to cut impaired-driving deaths by roughly 11 percent, Utah already did it, and no sensor needs inventing.[9] Third, respect the night math: with alcohol-impaired fatal crashes running triple the daytime rate, book the rideshare for the late hours, not the early ones.[5]

What this article did not prove: the 219 million figure is an order-of-magnitude illustration from stated assumptions (240M licensed drivers, 2.5 starts per day), not a prediction of any real device. Real systems may escalate (warning, then speed limiting, then immobilization) rather than binary-lock, and drivers would adapt their behavior, both of which change the real false-positive cost. FARS 2024 alcohol counts include BAC values estimated by multiple imputation where tests were missing, so 11,904 is partly modeled. DADSS lab accuracy figures are not public; "nothing close to 99.9%" is NHTSA’s characterization, not an independently verifiable benchmark. This article does not model false negatives, impaired drivers the system misses, which is the other half of the tradeoff, and the AAA effectiveness figures for interlocks and checkpoints apply to offender and enforcement populations, not to a fleet-wide passive system.

Strongest case against this article: the threshold math cuts both ways, and MADD’s side of the ledger is 11,904 corpses a year. A mandate-grade sensor does not need to be a conviction machine; an escalating system that warns, then limits speed, then immobilizes would make false positives annoying rather than catastrophic, and 219 million stranded commuters is a scarier number than the lived reality of a dashboard that yells at you. DADSS has improved for eighteen years, and the history of safety mandates (airbags, ESC, backup cameras) is full of industry claims that the technology was not ready right up until it was. The privacy debate is not premature either: once the sensor exists, the data-exfiltration question becomes the whole ballgame, and Massie lost 164 to 268. Treating the rule as technically dead because NHTSA is late has a poor track record; NHTSA is always late, and the mandates still arrive.
