The Gap

43% of Highway Deaths Were Alive When Help Arrived. The Help Didn't Bring Blood.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 26, 2027

Sixteen straight quarters of falling traffic deaths through the start of 2026. Enforcement is back, cars are safer, the victory lap is deserved. But NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison said something at the Governors Highway Safety Association meeting that reframes the whole celebration: between 2019 and 2023, 43% of the people killed on American highways were alive when first responders arrived at the scene.[1]

Read that again. Nearly half the dead were not dead yet.

15,755
Estimated 2025 highway deaths who were still alive when first responders arrived (43% of NHTSA's 36,640 estimate)

The killer in those minutes has a name, and it is not the guardrail. Catastrophic blood loss is the leading cause of death from injury, full stop.[1] The fix has a name too: whole blood, transfused at the scene instead of an hour later at the hospital. NHTSA's own tally says 32,992 crash patients met the clinical criteria for a prehospital transfusion in 2025.[1] For severely bleeding trauma patients, getting blood early cuts the risk of dying by 37%.[2] Every minute of delay raises the death risk another 11%.[2]

Now the gap. Fewer than 3% of America's roughly 15,000 EMS agencies carry blood.[1] Four hundred thirty programs against 32,992 qualifying patients in a single year. A federal interagency committee on EMS has set a goal of 2,028 agencies with blood programs by the end of 2028, and says faster post-crash care could prevent up to 10,000 deaths a year.[3] Do the division. Even if Washington hits its own target, about 86% of ambulance services still will not have blood on board, and 10,000 preventable deaths would be more than a quarter of the entire annual toll.

Technology is not the problem, which is the part that should make you angry. In 2026, Hillsborough County, Florida, launched the nation's first EMS drone program for whole blood delivery with Tampa General Hospital: two drones covering 70 square miles, blood lowered on a tether line in under three minutes on average, FAA beyond-visual-line-of-sight waiver in hand.[4] A cooler, a protocol, and a medical director's signature. That is the entire bill of materials for the device that decides whether the 43% live.

Give the skeptics their full hearing, because they are not wrong about the logistics. Whole blood expires in about five weeks and demands an unbroken cold chain, so stocking thousands of agencies means staggering waste. That 37% survival benefit comes from mature trauma systems, not the average county service. Rural agencies, where 39% of fatal crash victims do not reach a hospital for one to two hours compared with 10% in cities,[2] are the ones least able to afford the program. And the death count fell for four straight years without a drop of blood in an ambulance, which is a fair argument that the money belongs in prevention.

Here is what this story does not prove. That 43% includes unsurvivable injuries where no transfusion would have mattered; the per-minute figure describes early-window risk and cannot be compounded across an hour; and the 10,000 preventable deaths is an agency aspiration from a request for information, not a measured outcome. My derived numbers, the 15,755 and the 27% and the 2.9%, are arithmetic on cited inputs, rounded, not new data.

What you can actually do: learn to stop bleeding. Direct pressure and a tourniquet are the civilian version of this entire story, the training is free, and unlike the ambulance inventory, it is already in your hands. Ask your county EMS agency whether its ambulances carry whole blood. If you live far from a trauma center, learn the distance, because the golden hour is a myth when the blood is forty miles away.
