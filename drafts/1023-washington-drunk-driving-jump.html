Washington's Drunk-Driving Deaths Jumped 46%. The State Left 16,700 Blood Tests in the Fridge.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and in Washington there are always more reports than results, the state's alcohol-impaired road death rate climbed from 2.06 to 3.01 deaths per 100,000 residents between 2015 and 2024. That is a 46.28% increase, the fourth-steepest rise in the country, behind only the District of Columbia, Vermont, and Iowa. NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System supplies the numbers, filtered through a new state-by-state study, and they arrive at the same moment Washington's own enforcement machinery is visibly seizing up.[1]

16,700
DUI blood-test cases sitting in Washington's toxicology backlog at the end of 2025

Start with the evidence pipeline. Washington's forensic toxicology backlog reached 16,700 unprocessed DUI blood cases at the end of 2025, a pileup severe enough that lawmakers voted to let private labs handle the overflow. Cases stall while samples wait. Prosecutors triage. Some charges quietly expire before a scientist ever opens the vial. If you are scoring the state's drunk-driving strategy at home, this is the part where you note the scoreboard is blank because nobody has processed the scoreboard.[1]

Then run the interlock math, because I did. A January 2026 audit of the state's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Committee examined 66,000 drivers ordered to install ignition interlocks between 2018 and mid-2025. Only 41% complied. Meanwhile the other 59%, roughly 38,940 people, were told by a court to put a breathalyzer on their starter and simply declined. Washington spent $14.5 million on the program and, until lawmakers commissioned the audit, had assigned nobody to track compliance at all. Put it plainly, as the committee's analyst did: there is no way to know whether these drivers kept driving unless they get pulled over.[4]

This is where the math turns cruel. Washington's own traffic safety commission evaluated interlocks years ago and found that repeat offenders with the device reoffended at dramatically lower rates: 26% lower for second-time DUI offenders, 28% lower for third-timers and beyond. The devices work. They also catch the behavior in real time, with start-failure readings averaging a blood alcohol concentration of 0.09, above the legal limit, which means these machines are regularly stopping actively drunk people from turning a key. Washington knows the fix, bought the fix, funded the fix, and then never checked whether anyone installed the fix.[5]

Olympia's big legislative answer this year was Senate Bill 5067, which would have lowered the legal limit from 0.08 to 0.05. It passed the Senate 26 to 23 on January 28, then died in the House Community Safety Committee in February after the chair could not round up enough Democrats. Sponsor Sen. John Lovick blamed the hospitality industry. Senate transportation leadership offered the counterpoint: Washington ranks last in the nation in police staffing per capita, which makes writing more tickets with fewer troopers an arithmetic problem rather than a policy problem.[2][3]

Fairness requires the caveats, and they are substantial. Caveat one: the 46.28% figure rests on NHTSA data where known BAC results range from 8% of dead drivers in Mississippi to 86% in Montana, with the gaps filled by statistical imputation, and NHTSA itself urges great caution when comparing states across this patchwork. Caveat two: the study behind the ranking was published by Lantana Recovery, a for-profit addiction treatment company with an obvious interest in the headline. And the newest preliminary numbers actually cut the other way: Washington's total traffic deaths fell to 659 in 2025 from 809 in 2023, and deaths involving impaired drivers dropped from 370 to 297, though dozens of lab results are still pending and preliminary counts have a habit of revising upward. Utah's experience also suggests the 0.05 debate may be the wrong fight entirely. After the state lowered its limit in 2018, alcohol sales, tourism, and tax revenue all kept climbing, and the population it targets, drivers between 0.05 and 0.08, is a different crowd from the chronic offenders clogging the untested-blood queue.[1]

So here is what to actually do with this. If you live in Washington: the interlock data is unambiguous for repeat offenders, and skipping the device is now the majority behavior, so if someone you know is driving on an order they ignored, that is not a loophole, it is the norm, and it is worth saying something. If your legislator talks about 2027, ask what they did about the 38,940 missing interlocks first, because a lower legal limit aimed at 0.06 drivers does nothing for the offender who never installed the court-ordered device in the first place. And if you are anywhere else: your state's drunk-driving death count is built on the same imputed scaffolding, so treat any ranking, including this one, as directionally useful rather than precise. The one number that needs no imputation is 16,700. That is a count of actual vials, in actual refrigerators, holding actual evidence, waiting.
