# Research: Same Platform, Three Different Ways to Die (Panther Triplets)

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
Ford's Panther platform underpinned three sedans that were mechanically near-identical,
yet FARS 2014-2023 shows a 2.7x spread in fatality rates between them, and the car with
the LOWEST rate has the HIGHEST share of fatal crashes that kill its own occupant.
Same chassis. Different funerals.

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? Yes: no Panther-platform comparison exists anywhere on the site
  (checked stories/ + queue: no "panther", "town-car", "crown" comparison; the Crown Vic
  ghost-fleet piece covers one model only, not the triplet gap).
- Novel angle? Yes: the rate ranking and the own-death-share ranking are INVERTED.
  The safest-looking Panther by rate is the deadliest to its own occupant when it crashes.
- Not a data dump? The inversion is the story; the numbers are the evidence.

## FARS numbers (2014-2023, from fars_output.js)

| Model | Deaths | Rate* | Fatal involvements | Own-death share | Impairment (n) | Median MY |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mercury Grand Marquis | 1,153 | 2.29 | 1,516 | 76.1% | 18.5% (1,330) | 2002 |
| Ford Crown Victoria | 881 | 1.46 | 1,349 | 65.3% | 22.3% (936) | 2004 |
| Lincoln Town Car | 260 | 0.86 | 323 | 80.5% | 20.4% (754) | 2003 |

*Rate = deaths per 100M VMT (estimated), per site dataset methodology.
Combined: 2,294 deaths, ~229 per year across the three nameplates.

## Calculations (show the math)
- Rate spread: 2.29 / 0.86 = 2.66x (Grand Marquis vs Town Car).
- Grand Marquis vs Crown Vic: 2.29 / 1.46 = 1.57x. Crown Vic vs Town Car: 1.46 / 0.86 = 1.70x.
- Own-death share = deaths / fatal-crash involvements ("crashes" in the dataset =
  vehicles involved in fatal crashes, per fars_process.py vehicle_count):
  Town Car 260/323 = 80.5%; Grand Marquis 1,153/1,516 = 76.1%;
  Crown Victoria 881/1,349 = 65.3%.
- Impairment: alcohol- or drug-positive drivers in fatal crashes / drivers tested:
  Grand Marquis 246/1,330 = 18.5%; Crown Vic 209/936 = 22.3%; Town Car 154/754 = 20.4%.
  Impairment does NOT explain the rate gap: the highest-rate car has the LOWEST
  impairment share, and the spread is only 3.8 points.
- Model-year concentration: Grand Marquis top years 2000 and 2003 (113 deaths each);
  Crown Vic top year 2008 (78); Town Car top year 2005 (34). All three nameplates
  ended production in 2011; every death here is in a car at least 3 years old at the
  start of the window, most far older.

## Platform facts (verified)
- Panther platform from 1979; body-on-frame, rear-wheel drive, V8; from 1997 to 2011
  the three Panthers were the only full-size body-on-frame sedans built in North America.
- Later cars share the 4.6L modular V8; 2003+ cars got a stiffer hydroformed frame and
  rack-and-pinion steering. Town Car rides a longer wheelbase with plusher trim;
  Grand Marquis and Crown Vic are near-identical siblings apart from trim and styling.
- Duty cycles differed: Crown Vic was the police/taxi workhorse; Town Car was the
  livery/airport car; Grand Marquis was the retail car, bought heavily by older drivers.

## Original contribution
The inversion itself: ranking the triplets by fatality rate (Grand Marquis worst,
Town Car best) gives the opposite order of ranking them by own-occupant death share
in a fatal crash (Town Car worst, Crown Vic best). The site's rate tables and its
lethality tables have never been crossed on a single platform before, and the
crossover kills the naive reading of either table alone.

## Strongest counterargument (stated at full strength)
Same platform does not mean same car in use, and the gap may be entirely about who
drove them, not what they were. Crown Vics spent their lives as police cars and cabs,
driven hard at all hours; Town Cars were chauffeured gently and garaged; Grand
Marquis buyers skewed old, and older bodies break easier in any crash. The Town Car's
low rate may measure low mileage and gentle duty, not superior safety, and its high
own-death share may measure frail occupants, not a weak structure. If you controlled
for driver age, mileage, and crash context, the platform gap might shrink to nothing.
The data cannot do that controlling, so the gap is descriptive, not causal.

## Limitations (honest accounting)
- FARS captures fatal crashes only. A car can look safe here and still injure plenty.
- Rates use estimated fleet and VMT, not odometer readings; for discontinued,
  low-volume nameplates the uncertainty is real (treat 2.29 vs 0.86 as a wide gap, not
  a precise ratio).
- "Own-death share" counts deaths of the model's occupants per fatal involvement; a
  fatal crash can kill occupants of both vehicles, and some involvements kill only the
  other party. It is not a crash-survival probability.
- Impairment is toxicology-positive in fatal crashes, not proof impairment caused them.
- Model-year medians describe the cars that died, not the cars still on the road.

## Actionable takeaways
- Shopping a used Panther? Do not let the Town Car's 0.86 rate sell you "safest":
  when a Town Car is in a fatal crash, its occupant dies 4 times out of 5. Buy on
  condition and crash-avoidance upkeep (tires, brakes, lights), not on the badge.
- Grand Marquis owners: your car has the highest rate of the three. The single
  biggest lever is not the car, it is the driving: the impairment gap is small, so
  this is not a drunk-driver story, it is an old-car, old-driver, high-mileage story.
- All three: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change, and
  remember none of these cars has modern side-curtain and stability-control coverage
  comparable to a current sedan. They are 2011 technology at best.
