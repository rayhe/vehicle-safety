The Most Dangerous Part of Your Car Is the Part That Leaves It

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 8, 2026

On August 3, at about 6:10 in the evening, the front left wheel came off a Chevrolet 3500 on Interstate 20 in Ward County, Texas. It rolled across the center median, entered the eastbound lanes, and hit the windshield and roof of a Toyota 4Runner. A 30-year-old woman from Odessa died in the 4Runner; the 3500's driver, presumably, had a story to tell the insurance adjuster.[1]

Other lane
Where the victim is in nearly every fatal wheel-off crash: oncoming traffic, not the vehicle that lost the wheel

That asymmetry is the whole story. When a wheel separates at highway speed, the vehicle's own driver usually just feels a thud and pulls onto the shoulder. Meanwhile the person in the oncoming lane gets a hundred-pound steel projectile arriving through the windshield at the sum of both vehicles' speeds. It is, in the strictest actuarial sense, a hazard you inflict on strangers.[2]

This is not some freak of physics nobody understood. Georgia's Department of Public Safety put out a fact sheet years ago stating plainly that wheel separations "most commonly result from improper tightening of wheel fasteners and wheel bearing failure," which is bureaucratic for: somebody didn't use a torque wrench. Their lead case study is from 1991, when a wheel detached from a Mack truck on Florida's State Road 836, bounced over a 32-inch concrete barrier, and entered a school bus. It killed two children and put a chaperone in the ground ten days later.[2] Thirty-five years of progress, and the wheel is still coming off.

There is a courtroom paper trail, too, and it reads like a maintenance log with a body count. In Boryszewski v DaimlerChrysler, a wheel came loose from a Jeep on I-280 in New Jersey, struck the roof of a Plymouth Grand Caravan, and killed a 38-year-old woman. Investigators traced it to a garage that had rotated the Jeep's tires and failed to secure the lug nuts.[5] Read enough of these and the pattern stops looking like mechanical failure.

Now for the part that should embarrass Washington: nobody knows how many people this kills, because nobody counts them. NHTSA's own crash data folds everything wheel-related into "the general category of tire failure," which means a loose-lug projectile death and a bald-tire blowout land in the same statistical bucket.[4] FARS, the fatality database this site lives on, has no wheel-off code. NHTSA's figures attribute roughly 700 deaths a year to tire failure broadly, but wheel separations specifically are statistically invisible, which means no trend line, no target, and no urgency.[6] You cannot fix a body count you refuse to tally.

Now the counterargument, at full strength: most wheel-off events hurt nobody, and a wheel bouncing into an empty median is a story nobody tells. Impairment and speeding kill orders of magnitude more people, and this failure mode at least has a known, cheap fix, which is more than you can say for a drunk at 2 a.m. But cheap fixes are exactly what makes this one infuriating. In nearly every case, the cause is maintenance a shop already had the car on a lift for.[2]

What to do about it: after any tire service, rotation, or brake work, have the lug nuts re-torqued at 50 to 100 miles. This is standard shop procedure for a reason. Watch for warning signs: steering vibration at speed, a rattling or clunking from one corner, a wheel that looks slightly crooked in the rearview of the car behind you. If you own a torque wrench, ten minutes after a rotation buys you out of the worst lottery in traffic. Re-torquing lug nuts is free, and a hundred-pound wheel bouncing down the interstate is the most expensive object on it.[2]
