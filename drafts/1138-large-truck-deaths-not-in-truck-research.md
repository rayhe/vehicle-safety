## Angle (1-2 sentences)
NHTSA's June 2026 federal report on 2024 large-truck crashes counts 5,340 dead, and 83% of them were not in the truck at all — 4,420 people killed by physics they didn't sign up for, roughly five other deaths for every truck occupant. California sharpens the picture: 17.4% of its large-truck deaths were pedestrians or bicyclists versus 12.5% nationally, and the truck drivers themselves were six times less likely to be drunk than the people in the cars around them.

## Original calculations (the novel contribution)
- **83% rule:** 4,420 other people killed / 5,340 total = 82.8%. Ratio to truck occupants: 4,420 : 920 = **4.8:1** — nearly five deaths outside the truck for every one inside it.
- **Three-way split, 2024:** other-vehicle occupants 3,753 (70.3%); truck occupants 920 (17.2%); nonoccupants (pedestrians/cyclists) 667 (12.5%). 667/5,340 = 12.49%, which independently confirms the law-firm release's "12.5% national baseline" — their numbers check out against the federal report.
- **CA anomaly verified:** 68/391 = 17.39% vs 12.5% national. Florida is worse on share: 69/353 = 19.5%, though CA leads on raw count of the two >60 states (with TX at 73 nonoccupants, 73/720 = 10.1%).
- **Impairment inversion:** 4% of large-truck drivers in fatal crashes were BAC≥.08 vs 25% of passenger-car drivers and 25% of motorcyclists. The driver behind the 80,000-lb vehicle is ~6x less likely to be impaired than the driver next to it.
- **Injury side matches:** of 161,201 injured, 119,748 (74%) were other people vs 41,454 (26%) truck occupants. The asymmetry survives injury data too (CRSS estimates).
- **Work-hours toll:** 76% of fatal large-truck crashes on weekdays; of those, 70% during daytime (6 a.m.–5:59 p.m.). Large trucks are 5% of registered vehicles but 10% of all VMT and 9% of vehicles in fatal crashes.

## Limitations (must state in article)
- 2024 figures come from FARS 2024 ARF (Annual Report File) — preliminary and subject to revision; 2015–2023 use Final Files. Injury counts are CRSS sample-based estimates, not census counts.
- The California "17.4% vs 12.5%" gap is a share comparison, not a rate: CA has more urban VMT and more pedestrians, which mechanically inflates the nonoccupant share. FARS provides no state-level truck VMT denominator, so per-mile comparisons are impossible.
- BAC figures mix tested and imputed values; testing coverage differs by driver type (commercial drivers are tested more consistently), which can bias the 4%-vs-25% comparison.
- "Large truck" = GVWR >10,000 lb, excluding buses and motorhomes, commercial and noncommercial. Light vans (≤10,000 lb) are not in these numbers.
- The 2020 vehicle-classification change (vPIC) breaks comparability with 2019 and earlier — trend claims must not cross that line.

## Strongest counterargument (must state at full strength)
This is physics and exposure, not villainy. Each truck logs enormous mileage (trucks are 5% of registered vehicles but 10% of all VMT), and every one of those miles happens next to other vehicles — so the "who dies" split largely reflects who is in the way, not who is at fault. Truck drivers are the least impaired drivers on the road (4% BAC vs 25% for car drivers) and less likely to carry license suspensions (7.2% vs 11.8%). In two-vehicle fatal crashes, both vehicles were simply going straight 42.4% of the time — ordinary traffic, ordinary people, an 80,000-lb mismatch. The 19.3% prior-crash figure for truck drivers reads the same way: drive enough miles and everyone collects incidents. The trucker is rarely the reckless one. The problem is mass times momentum.

## Actionable insights (hard gate)
- Give trucks room and never linger alongside them: if you cannot see the truck's mirrors, the driver cannot see you (the No-Zone). Pass quickly and completely on the left.
- Never cut in front of a large truck and brake — a loaded tractor-trailer needs vastly more stopping distance than your car, and in 6.3% of two-vehicle fatal truck crashes the truck was stopped in the road when the other vehicle hit it.
- Pedestrians and bicyclists: 667 of you were killed by large trucks in 2024, and in California nearly one in six large-truck deaths is a nonoccupant. Near turning trucks, assume you are invisible — turning maneuvers put peds in blind geometry.
- If you share roads with trucks daily, the weekday daytime window (76% of fatal crashes on weekdays, 70% of those daytime) is the exposure window, not the empty midnight highway.
