The Average Jeep Fails Three IIHS Safety Tests. The Average Mazda Fails None.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 19, 2026

I read safety ratings the way most people read them: one row at a time. Headlights: Acceptable. Side impact: Good. Moving on. This week ForCar read the same data the other way, column-wise, and asked the dumber question: out of six IIHS evaluations, how many does each vehicle fail? 145 of 305 vehicles fail nothing. 44 fail three or more. Three vehicles fail five out of six.[1] Counted this way, a single Marginal stops looking like a blemish and starts looking like a fingerprint.

38×
How many more IIHS evaluations the average Jeep fails than the average Mazda (3.07 vs. 0.08 per vehicle)

Failures cluster by brand, and the clustering is violent. Jeep averages 3.07 failed evaluations across 14 vehicles. Chevrolet sits at 1.83, Ford at 1.71. Then the chart falls off a cliff: Hyundai 0.44, Honda 0.41, and Mazda at 0.08, which is two failures total across 25 vehicles.[1] Divide 3.07 by 0.08 and a Jeep fails roughly 38 times as many evaluations as a Mazda. That is not a difference of degree. That is two different industries sharing a parking lot.

Price does not explain any of it. Audi, a luxury badge, averages 1.56 failures, worse than mainstream Ford and Kia and roughly three and a half times worse than Hyundai.[1] Fit a regression line to price versus failures and the slope is a shrug. What predicts the score is platform age. A vehicle designed before an evaluation existed fails it, and a long product cycle drags that failure across every sibling model at once. Take the Wrangler: Marginal in the small overlap test (it tipped onto its side in IIHS audit runs), Poor to Marginal headlights depending on trim, Poor pedestrian front braking, Marginal belt reminders. Four unrelated systems, four failures, one old platform.[6] Multiply that by a 14-vehicle lineup of long-lived platforms and you get 3.07.

What to do with this: when you shop, count the failures instead of reading the rows. One Marginal on an otherwise clean rating sheet is noise. Three failures across unrelated systems tells you the generation of the design. Then check the model year, because the gap between two years of the same nameplate can exceed the gap between two brands: most of these evaluations are recent, and cars redesigned after they appeared behave completely differently.[1] Verify at iihs.org/ratings before you sign anything, and run any used VIN through nhtsa.gov/recalls. The badge on the grille predicts the scores better than the price on the window. That is not supposed to be true, but I have 305 data points that say otherwise.
