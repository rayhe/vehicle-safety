Recalls Didn't Rise This Year: They Got Fat

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. Nearly 24 million vehicles were recalled in the United States in the first half of 2026, more than double the number a year earlier, according to Sedgwick's U.S. Product Safety and Recall Index.[1] That is not a full-year figure. That is six months, and it nearly matched all of 2025, a year that itself set an 11-year low with 27.4 million units across 928 campaigns.[2]

~29,500 → ~52,000
Mean vehicles per recall campaign, full-year 2025 vs. my estimate for H1 2026

Here is the part that made me sit up. Sedgwick recorded 1,634 recall events across all industries in H1 2026, essentially identical to the 1,636 in H1 2025. Events flat. Total product units recalled up 345.5 percent.[1] In automotive specifically, coverage reports that automakers issued fewer notices overall even as vehicle volume doubled.[3] So run the division yourself. 27.4 million vehicles divided by 928 campaigns in 2025 gives about 29,500 vehicles per recall. If H1 2026 pushed ~24 million units through a campaign count that stayed flat or fell, the mean recall roughly doubled in size, to the neighborhood of 52,000 vehicles, and the headline writes itself: recalls aren't rising; they're getting fat.

Why? Sedgwick's Chris Harvey says it plainly: "This does not necessarily indicate a decline in vehicle quality... Rather, it reflects the interconnected nature of modern vehicle design and manufacturing, where a single issue can have far-reaching impacts."[3] Shared platforms, shared electrical architecture, and shared software patched by one OTA update to a dozen nameplates. One bad solder joint or one bad bearing spec now travels on a conveyor belt that feeds half the industry.

The 2026 fattening follows a 2025 pattern worth flagging. Last year's single biggest unit cause was back-over prevention systems: 7.8 million vehicles, up 206 percent year over year, a ten-year high.[2] A mandated safety system, shared across dozens of nameplates, became the largest recall vector in a decade, and if you need a moment, chew on that one for a while. The hardware meant to stop cars from backing over children is now recalled in such volumes that it defines the industry's recall statistics. Nobody planned that; the spreadsheet just landed there.

Now for the punchline, and it is a grim one. As recalls get fatter, they get harder to fix. J.D. Power's analysis of 2013-2015 NHTSA data found that recalls exceeding one million vehicles achieved only a 49 percent completion rate, compared with 67 percent for recalls under 10,000 vehicles.[4] So the industry's own history says the mega-campaigns most likely to touch your car are the least likely to be completed. Volume growth is exposure growth and repair-rate decline at the same time: a beautiful, terrible two-sided distribution.

Fairness requires the counterargument at full strength, because it is a good one. Sedgwick's expert says it himself: bigger recall volumes do not prove cars are getting worse. They can prove the surveillance system is getting better. NHTSA's reporting rules have tightened, over-the-air updates turn what would have been silent software patches into formal recalls, and a million-car recall over a low-severity electrical glitch is not a million cars in danger. Shared platforms are an engineering efficiency that lowers prices; the cost is recall concentration, and the data doesn't price the benefit. One car with a loose gas cap and one with a fire-prone battery both count once, which is to say: count is not consequence.

Also, honesty demands the caveats. Sedgwick counts vehicles per campaign, not unique vehicles, so cars appearing in two campaigns count twice. The ~24 million figure and the "fewer notices" detail come from automotive trade-press coverage of Sedgwick's gated H1 2026 index report; Sedgwick's own press release confirms the all-industry numbers (flat events, 941.2 million units, up 345.5 percent) but does not publish the auto-specific event count, which is why the ~52,000-per-campaign figure is labeled as my estimate conditioned on flat campaign counts.[1] The J.D. Power completion data is from 2013-2015, the best long-horizon analysis available but dated, and modern OTA recalls may behave differently. And "on pace for a five-year high" is pace-based; H2 2026 could still undershoot.

What to do with all this: if your model's sibling on the same platform gets recalled for a shared electrical or backup-camera defect, check your own VIN even when your nameplate wasn't named. Shared parts are the vector; the recall notice follows the part, not the badge. Don't wait for a second letter on a mega-recall: with completion rates under 50 percent for million-plus campaigns, the herd will not save you. Book the remedy the week the notice arrives. And if you're buying used, ask the dealer to prove "all recalls completed" against the actual VIN, because 2024-2026 model years sit squarely in the fattest recall half-year in recent history. Check at nhtsa.gov/recalls; it takes ninety seconds. The mega-recall era doesn't care about your schedule.
