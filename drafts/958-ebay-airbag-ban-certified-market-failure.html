eBay Spent Years Building a Safe Way to Buy Used Airbags, Then Banned the Whole Category.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 20, 2026

Before you buy that used car, ask a question nobody thinks to ask: who put the airbag in the steering wheel. If the answer is anything other than the factory, you have a problem that no VIN check can solve, and as of this week the largest online parts marketplace in America has decided the only safe answer is to stop selling them entirely. In April 2025, a 31-year-old Michigan man named Dinas Kamaitis was arraigned on nine felonies for selling counterfeit restraint systems through his eBay store, the first case filed by Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel's newly expanded Auto Fraud Task Force, with more than $600,000 seized from his bank accounts.[3][4] According to the AG's office, approximately 81,000 Michigan vehicles may be equipped with counterfeit restraint systems, and over 80 percent of counterfeit airbags fail to deploy properly in a crash.[3]

81,000
Michigan vehicles that may be equipped with counterfeit restraint systems, per the Michigan Attorney General

Effective September 24, 2026, airbags, airbag inflators, and steering wheel airbag covers are no longer permitted to be listed or sold on eBay by any seller, in any country, under any listing trick. Sellers got 30 days to pull their listings; eBay will remove whatever remains. Control units, crash sensors, diagnostic modules, and passenger sensors survive, but inside the United States only for pre-approved sellers, because anything containing a pyrotechnic charge is now treated the way the platform treats everything else that can kill someone.[1] Note what is not in that announcement: no scandal, no named villain. The company says it acted after "a comprehensive review of the risks these products may pose to eBay users," which is corporate for: we looked at this market and concluded it cannot be made safe on our platform.[1]

That conclusion is the real story, because eBay's airbag category was not the Wild West. For years the company restricted airbag sales to a select group of certified professional automotive recyclers, built in partnership with the Automotive Recyclers Association, complete with safety training, certification, recall verification, compliance standards, and VIN tracking protocols designed to keep recalled components out of the retail stream.[2][6] This was the highest-friction, most-verified, most legitimate channel for buying a used airbag anywhere on the internet. eBay just killed it anyway. Read that as a confession: even with certification, training, recall cross-checks, and VIN tracking, nobody could reliably tell a genuine module from a fake at listing time. When the "safe" program dies, it dies because the program itself was the illusion.

The industry saw this coming for a decade. Representatives of the Automotive Anti-Counterfeiting Council spent nearly ten years lobbying digital marketplaces to enact total prohibitions, arguing that outright sales bans are the only verifiable mechanism against counterfeit safety parts.[6] Think about what that sentence admits: not that verification is hard, but that it is impossible. The most sophisticated compliance apparatus the recycling industry could build, running on the largest e-commerce platform on earth, could not keep fakes out of steering wheels, so the apparatus got scrapped.

Now do the Michigan arithmetic, because it explains why a private company moved before any regulator could. One prosecuted seller, one eBay store, six hundred thousand dollars seized, nine felonies. Nine felonies. And on the other side of the ledger, roughly 81,000 Michigan vehicles that may be carrying the things he and people like him sold, of which more than four out of five will not deploy when a crash asks them to.[3][4] Michigan built a dedicated Auto Fraud Task Force and aimed it at this problem, and its first case touched one man. Enforcement at that pace cannot scale to a denominator of 81,000 in a single state, which is precisely why the marketplace acted unilaterally: its enforcement tools, account restrictions and automated listing removal at internet scale, reach further than a task force that prosecutes cases one at a time.

Two different ways your airbag can betray you, and buyers keep confusing them. The DTN60DB inflators that NHTSA banned from sale and import on April 29, 2026 are the loud failure mode: they rupture and fire metal fragments into the cabin, and the agency has linked them to 11 deaths and 3 severe injuries across 14 crashes, with the most recent death on August 27, 2026, in Dallas, in a 2018 Chevrolet Equinox.[7] The generic counterfeits the Michigan AG describes are the quiet failure mode: over 80 percent simply do not deploy.[3] Nobody pictures that when they hear "counterfeit airbag." Everyone imagines shrapnel. The statistically likelier fake is a decorative steering wheel cover with a bomb-shaped paperweight behind it. Both arrive through the same uninspectable channel, and a VIN lookup at nhtsa.gov catches neither, because neither ever passed through a manufacturer's records.[7]

How big is the pool these parts swim in? CARFAX data from July 2024 counted nearly 2 million vehicles back on U.S. roads after an airbag deployment in just the prior two years, each one a vacancy where a replacement module had to go, while NHTSA itself said there is "no way to know for sure just how many counterfeit airbags are being installed in U.S. cars today," while the enforcement pipeline produces single-digit cases, and while the federal recall system only works through reachable manufacturers.[8] The arithmetic of that mismatch is the whole story of the counterfeit airbag market, and eBay's ban is the market's biggest participant doing the math in public.

Strongest case against this article: the ban may make used-car buyers less safe, not more. The certified-recycler program eBay just killed was the safest way to buy a legitimate used airbag at a non-dealer price, with VIN tracking and recall verification that no Facebook Marketplace listing will ever offer. The Automotive Recyclers Association said as much: it was disappointed to see the program end.[2] What remains is a two-tier market. Wealthy owners buy genuine modules from dealers at full price. Everyone else migrates to unregulated channels, Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, Car-Part.com, where there is no certification, no recall cross-check, and no one removing listings, and where the price gap between a genuine part and a fake only widens, strengthening exactly the economic motive that put Dinas Kamaitis in a courtroom. There is also the compliance-theater objection: the DTN60DB inflators that actually killed people appear to have been illegally imported and installed by repair shops, not sold through eBay's certified program, so banning eBay listings polices a storefront while the death vector keeps moving through channels nobody monitors. A2C2 calls a total ban "the only verifiable mechanism," which is true of eBay's storefront and says nothing about the parts that never touch it.

What this analysis cannot prove: the AG's 81,000 figure is an estimate of vehicles that may carry counterfeit restraints, not confirmed installations, and it rests on deployment-count data rather than forensic part inspections. CARFAX's 2 million is the opportunity pool, vehicles back on the road after a deployment, not the number with fakes installed. The true installed base of counterfeit airbags is unknowable, which NHTSA stated outright in 2024. No established link connects the Kamaitis prosecution to the DTN60DB inflators specifically; the 80 percent non-deployment figure and the DTN60DB rupture figures describe different products and must not be merged. And eBay's ban governs eBay only: non-pyrotechnic parts remain listable by any seller outside the US, and nothing in the announcement stops sales migrating to other platforms.

What you can actually do: if you are buying used, pull a vehicle history report and look specifically for airbag deployment records, then demand the repair order and invoice that names the supplier and part number of any replacement module. A seller telling you the SRS warning light is off is not evidence of anything.[7] If your car was repaired after a deployment, insist on genuine OE part numbers on the invoice, and if the paperwork is unclear, pay a brand dealer for an SRS inspection; this is one of the few inspections worth buying blind. Watch the airbag warning light for unusual behavior, which the National Insurance Crime Bureau flags as a possible hint of a tampered module.[3] Never inspect an airbag module yourself, never buy one from a marketplace listing, and understand that a clean VIN search at nhtsa.gov/recalls is not an all-clear: it catches manufacturer recalls, and this problem has never touched a manufacturer.[7]
