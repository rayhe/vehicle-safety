## Verified facts (all from NTSB HIR-26-07 unless noted)

### The crash
- August 31, 2024, ~12:33 a.m. CDT, I-20 westbound near mile marker 9.5, Vicksburg, Mississippi.
- 2018 Volvo 9800 Grand L motorcoach (built April 2017, Mexican market), operated by Transportes Regiomontanos, on a regularly scheduled route from Reading, Pennsylvania, to Monterrey, Mexico.
- 44 people aboard: 54-year-old driver, co-driver, 42 passengers.
- Left front steer-axle tire suffered sudden catastrophic deflation ("blowout") at 71 mph on a straight 1.2-mile section, 1.6% downhill grade. Motorcoach departed the roadway 3.1 seconds later, crossed the left lane and shoulder, entered a 275-foot-wide grassy/wooded median, tipped onto its left (driver) side, slid down the slope, came to rest ~26 feet south of the westbound roadway edge.
- 7 passengers died, 8 seriously injured, 13 minor injuries, 14 uninjured. Driver minor injuries; co-driver serious. All 7 fatally injured passengers were ejected, as were 3 of the seriously injured.
- 11 occupants extricated from under/between seats; 3 survivors found under or partially under the motorcoach.
- 911 at 12:36 a.m.; first deputy 12:50 a.m.; westbound I-20 reopened 6:07 a.m.
- Report published August 11, 2026 — 710 days (23 months) after the crash.

### The tire (probable cause)
- NTSB lab: tread/belt separation; progressive tearing between belt plies initiated at belt edges; radial ply exposed over ~25% of circumference; numerous broken radial steel cords with necking (tensile overstress fracture); inner liner splits, loosened from radial ply.
- Most likely cause: previous impact damage to the tire's internal structure (broken cords, tears across inner liner/radial ply/shoulder/sidewall near separation origin). NTSB could NOT determine when or how the impact damage occurred; carrier records contain no documentation of it.
- Internal damage would have been difficult to detect in routine visual inspection — making pressure maintenance critical.
- Both steer tires showed overdeflection indicators (underinflation and/or excessive load): chafing at bead seat interface, compression groove at rim flange, partially worn radial vents, shoulder step/chamfer wear around entire circumference of both shoulders. Overdeflection causes heat buildup, deteriorates rubber, accelerates preexisting damage.
- Steer tires replaced February 3, 2024 (~7 months before crash). All tires had >=8/32-inch tread (minimum 4/32 steer, 2/32 others).
- Carrier maintenance: 41 scheduled/unscheduled events in the year before the crash, last August 22, 2024 (9 days before). Records show tire checks were standard practice but record NO measured pressure values.
- CTIS (Vigia NM 253 central tire inflation system): designed to auto-inflate to preprogrammed pressure and alert driver. At postcrash inspection ALL axle buttons and the auditory alert button were in the deactivated position; deactivated axles do not resume inflating until manually reactivated, and settings do not reset with ignition. Supply line to left steer tire leaked ~4 psi/sec (could not determine preexisting vs crash-caused). Alert programmed to deactivate at 100-105 psi (steer), vs tire max 123 psi. CTIS displays equalized axle pressure, not individual tires, and stores no data — pressures at crash undeterminable.
- NTSB: "likely below the optimal operating pressure for tires rated up to 123 psi." If the coach ran near max GVWR (54,564 lbs), steer-axle load would likely approach or exceed rated capacity even at 123 psi. Loaded weight was NOT recorded.
- NTSB probable cause: "catastrophic failure of the left steer axle tire... due to previous impact damage to the tire's internal structure. Contributing to the crash was the inadequate vehicle inspection process of the motor carrier, which permitted the motorcoach to operate with damaged and overdeflected tires."

### The belts (contributing to ejections and fatalities)
- Passenger seats had lap-only belts. Only ONE of the 46 passenger lap belts was functional and accessible at the time of the crash. Nearly all others hung below the seat pan or were lodged between seat cushions and could not be extended to latch.
- None of the 6 passengers interviewed wore a belt or received a pretrip safety briefing. Two passengers asked the driver/co-driver about belts and were told the motorcoach had none.
- NHTSA estimates lap/shoulder belts 77% effective and lap-only belts 76% effective at preventing fatalities in rollover crashes (PRIA) — but only if maintained, accessible, and worn.
- Driver and co-driver had lap/shoulder belts, both operational; driver was belted (video-confirmed). Co-driver was in the sleeper berth (about 10 inches shorter than 49 CFR 393.76 requires).
- Contributing to ejections/fatalities per NTSB: "the motor carrier's use of a motorcoach that was not compliant with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards and failure to ensure that the motorcoach-equipped lap-only seat belts were operational, accessible, and properly used by passengers."

### The bus was never certified for the US
- Manufactured for the Mexican market, never formally imported: no USDOT certification label (49 CFR 567), absent from NHTSA's import-eligibility databases.
- Noncompliant with at least two FMVSSs for MY2018 motorcoaches: FMVSS 208 requires lap/shoulder belts in all passenger seating positions on over-the-road buses built after Nov 28, 2016 with GVWR >26,000 lbs (this bus: 54,564 lbs; it had lap-only). FMVSS 217 requires emergency-exit labels in English; roof exit hatch instructions were labeled only in Spanish.
- CBP inspects commercial vehicles entering the US for contraband and documents; FMVSS compliance is examined only on formal import. NHTSA oversees FMVSS compliance but the bus never entered that system.
- No valid Texas travel permit since October 2022 (~22 months before the crash); carrier obtained no permits for any other state on this trip. (Mexico does not participate in the International Registration Plan.)
- No ESC: motorcoach not equipped, nor required to be — FMVSS 571.136 requires ESC only for motorcoaches built after August 1, 2019; this one built April 2017.

### The carrier and the driver
- Transportes Regiomontanos, principal place of business Laredo, Texas; USDOT authorization April 2005; two fixed routes from Monterrey: Cleveland, Ohio, and Reading, Pennsylvania.
- No written safety plan; no dedicated policies for pretrip safety briefings or performance-based driver incentives.
- FMCSA Safety Measurement System: Crash Indicator BASIC in alert status since July 2024 (~8 weeks before the crash); 4 crashes in the 4 years before Vicksburg, 1 fatal (2023, motorcoach not at fault — struck in a queue).
- Postcrash FMCSA compliance review: 7 violations, none critical or acute; rating "satisfactory." Carrier then could not renew insurance or obtain a policy elsewhere and has ceased operations (as of report date).
- NTSB identified several carriers with common ownership/management/operational elements — possible "reincarnated carrier" (49 CFR 385.1003/385.1005) — and shared the information with FMCSA.
- Driver: Mexican Class A international CDL (valid in US under 1991 MOU); clean CDLIS record; FMCSA clearinghouse clean; drug test negative. Required postcrash alcohol test (within 8 hours, 49 CFR 382.303) was NOT performed; carrier submitted no documentation explaining why.
- Driver training: 4 days on hire (HOS, inclement weather, security). Training did NOT include response to sudden tire failure. He had never experienced a steer-tire failure. He braked lightly, believing harder braking could cause loss of control/overturn; speed fell ~1 mph in the first second.
- On duty ~3.5 hours after ~14 hours in the sleeper berth. Cell records show phone use while on duty but not at/around the crash; inward camera showed him alert, belted, left hand on wheel, looking ahead; grabbed wheel with both hands as it rotated rapidly left.
- NTSB: even a properly belted driver with both hands on the wheel would have found it "extremely challenging" to maintain control; the blowout's lateral force and unanticipated nature made departure likely regardless. Texas CMV handbook: hold wheel firmly, stay off brakes until slowed; training on steer-tire failure is not federally required and not commonly provided industry-wide.
- Tech exists: Kodiak Robotics demonstrated in 2022 that an ADS could handle a catastrophic steer-tire blowout, hold lane, and stop controlled. Volvo Dynamic Steering (trucks) detects sudden steering movement and limits excessive displacement; Volvo told NTSB in Feb 2026 that European drivers reported benefits in blowout situations. Neither system is installed on motorcoaches.

### The inspection gap (postcrash action)
- CVSA Level I inspection procedures for passenger-carrying CMVs check only the DRIVER's seat belt — not passenger belts, which are required equipment on motorcoaches built on/after Nov 28, 2016.
- After the crash, NTSB briefed CVSA (workshop April 19-23, 2026, Chicago). CVSA is developing updated procedures: motorcoaches built on/after Nov 28, 2016 with inoperable/inaccessible passenger belts would be cited under 49 CFR 571.208. Draft to Passenger Carrier Committee, annual conference September 2026, for a vote.
- Prior NTSB motorcoach belt-accessibility findings: Louisville, NY (2013 bus), San Jose, CA (2014 motorcoach), Biloxi, MS (2016 motorcoach, buckles non-functional). NTSB recommendations H-24-33/H-24-34, H-17-8/H-17-11.
- Prior steer-tire motorcoach crashes: Wawayanda, NY, Sept 21, 2023 (2 dead, 42 injured; driver unbelted, one hand on wheel) — NTSB HIR-25-04; plus 2010 and 2009 cases.
- NTSB 2009 tire-pressure recommendations: H-09-19 (FMCSA, Closed—Unacceptable Action), H-09-27 (AAMVA, Closed—Acceptable Action), H-09-22 (NHTSA, TPMS for motorcoaches, Open—Unacceptable Response). FMVSS 138 (TPMS) applies only to vehicles <=10,000 lbs GVWR.

## Original calculations (novel contribution)
1. **1 of 46 belts usable (2.2%).** 45 of 46 passenger lap belts were inaccessible or inoperable. The bus carried the legal minimum of nothing and delivered 2.2% of it.
2. **100% ejection among the dead.** All 7 fatalities were ejected; 10 of 44 occupants (23%) were ejected in total. NHTSA's own estimate says lap-only belts are 76% effective at preventing rollover fatalities — the gap between 76% and what happened here is entirely accessibility.
3. **CTIS setpoint gap.** System programmed to hold steer tires at 100-105 psi vs 123 psi max rating = 15-19% below max (100/123 = 81%, 105/123 = 85%). And the system was found switched off entirely. A machine built to prevent exactly this failure was both misconfigured and disabled.
4. **3.1 seconds.** Blowout at 71 mph to roadway departure in 3.1 seconds (~323 feet at 104 ft/s). The tire-friction mark ran 351 feet from its origin to the departure point — the numbers agree: there was no time to do anything but hold on.
5. **22 months without a permit; 8 weeks on alert.** No valid Texas trip permit since Oct 2022; FMCSA Crash Indicator BASIC in alert since July 2024. The crash came ~8 weeks into the alert.
6. **710 days.** Crash Aug 31, 2024 to report Aug 11, 2026. The bus was already out of business by the time the report landed — the carrier ceased operations after losing insurance.
7. **Rate check (context, not a claim):** 7 deaths / 44 occupants = 16% fatality rate aboard; 10 ejections / 44 = 23%.

## Strongest counterargument (must be stated at full strength)
The tire's fatal damage was internal and invisible. No inspection regime — not the carrier's 41 maintenance events, not a roadside Level I inspection, not the driver's pretrip walk-around — could have seen broken steel cords inside a tire with 8/32-inch tread. The carrier checked its tires, replaced the steers seven months earlier, and kept the bus maintained. The driver did nearly everything right: belted, alert, both hands on the wheel once it yanked, braking lightly exactly as the handbook's spirit suggests. The NTSB itself says even a perfect response probably would not have prevented the departure. If the blowout was bad luck on a tire no one could have condemned, then the story is not "negligent carrier kills seven" but "a random failure met a bus with no margin" — and the margin (belts, certification, pressure monitoring) is where the deaths actually happened. That distinction matters: the blowout was arguably unavoidable; the ejections were not.

Additional honest caveat: the "illegal bus" framing needs precision. The carrier held valid USDOT operating authority (since 2005) and the driver held a valid international CDL. The vehicle was never FMVSS-certified, but cross-border carriers operate Mexico-registered vehicles in the US under longstanding arrangements; the gap is that nobody checks FMVSS compliance at the border. The bus was noncompliant, not smuggled.

## Limitations (must appear in article)
- NTSB could not determine when or how the tire's impact damage occurred, nor the tire pressures at the time of the crash, nor whether the CTIS was deactivated before the crash or whether the 4 psi/sec supply-line leak predated the crash.
- The loaded weight of the motorcoach (passengers + luggage) was not recorded, so whether the steer tires were overloaded cannot be established — only that overdeflection indicators were present on both steer tires.
- The seating positions of passengers could not be determined; belt-use findings rest on postcrash inspection plus 6 passenger interviews.
- The report's 710-day lag means the carrier was already defunct when findings published; the CVSA inspection fix was still pending a September 2026 vote at report time.
- This is one crash, not a rate: 7 deaths do not establish a motorcoach tire-failure trend. The Wawayanda 2023 precedent (2 dead) is the closest comparator.

## Actionable insights (required)
- If you ride a motorcoach: before departure, physically pull your seat belt out of the seat and latch it. If it is buried, broken, or missing, say so to the driver and consider another bus — the Vicksburg belts were lap-only and 45 of 46 were unusable, and every person who died was ejected. Ask for a safety briefing; the Vicksburg passengers never got one, and two who asked were told there were no belts.
- If you charter a bus: ask the carrier for its FMCSA safety rating and whether its coaches are FMVSS-certified for US operation (USDOT label) — Mexico-market coaches can look identical and lack lap/shoulder belts and English exit labels.
- If you drive a vehicle with a tire-pressure system: check that it is actually switched on and set to the tire's rated pressure, not a lower convenience setting. The Vicksburg CTIS was off and, even on, would have held 100-105 psi against a 123 psi rating.
- Steer-tire blowout response (Texas CMV handbook / AAMVA / FMCSA guidance): both hands firm on the wheel, stay off the brakes until the vehicle slows, do not jerk the wheel. Training on this is not federally required — if you drive commercially, ask your carrier for it.
