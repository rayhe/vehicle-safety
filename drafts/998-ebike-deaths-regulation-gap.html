The Gap

310 People Died on E-Bikes. The Government Calls Them Bicycles.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds when a 28-mile-per-hour object meets the side of a pickup truck: nothing good. That object used to be a motorcycle, back when 28 mph with a motor meant licensing, lighting standards, a DOT helmet, and a crash-test regime. Now it's an e-bike, and the federal position is that it is a bicycle. The CPSC counted 310 e-bike deaths between 2017 and 2024, and the death count grew sixteenfold in five years.[1]

55%
of e-bike deaths were collisions with motor vehicles, per the CPSC's hazard decomposition

Of those 310 deaths, 170 came from collisions with moving or parked motor vehicles: 55 percent. Being hit by a car is the leading cause of death on an e-bike, the same leading cause that governs motorcycles and bicycles. But the e-bike occupies a regulatory void neither category tolerates. By the CPSC's own legal definition these machines are capped under 20 mph on a level surface, yet the agency's own 2024 injury study found 15 percent of injured riders were traveling 20 mph or faster when they crashed, and class-3 e-bikes, legal in most states, assist all the way to 28 mph. Nobody crash-tests them, nobody sets their lighting standard, and nobody requires a license; only 40 percent of injured riders were wearing a helmet.[1]

A motorcycle that travels 28 mph is regulated like a motorcycle, while an e-bike that travels 28 mph is regulated like a 1974 Schwinn. Engineers have a word for a design that behaves like one category while being certified as another: a loophole. E-bike deaths went from 6 in 2019 to 97 in 2024, and the recent plateau is not necessarily improvement: the CPSC warns its fatality data lags death certificates by up to two years, making the latest counts a floor rather than a final tally.[1]

And the demographics make the gap crueler. Eighty-two percent of the dead were men, and of 92 micromobility deaths among riders 65 and older, 75 involved e-bikes. E-bikes restore moped-class speed to the age cohort with the slowest reaction times and the most fracture-prone bones, then send them into traffic wearing a foam helmet, when they wear one at all; six in ten injured riders wore none. Meanwhile the battery under the frame kills on its own schedule: 19 e-bike deaths came from lithium-ion fires across 13 incidents, several tied to homemade packs and repair shops, and a BBC records request found 18 more fire deaths in the UK since 2019. This fire risk is not an American manufacturing quirk; it is the chemistry, which is the setup for the counterargument at full strength: e-bikes displace car trips, and cars kill roughly 40,000 Americans a year, so every e-bike mile is a car mile not driven; 310 deaths over eight years is a rounding error against motor-vehicle carnage, and clamping down could push riders back into cars and raise the total count. There is even a charitable read of the helmet number: 40 percent is roughly double the bicycle average, which suggests e-bike riders take safety more seriously than the cyclists they replaced. Both can be true while the regulatory gap stays indefensible, because displacement is an argument for managing the category well, not for pretending it is a bicycle.

Pennsylvania is learning this the hard way. PennDOT reported bicyclist deaths rising from 19 in 2024 to 28 in 2025, with 12 of the dead riding e-bikes: 43 percent of the state's bicyclist fatalities on a vehicle class that barely existed a decade ago. After a 12-year-old died on an e-bike in a pickup collision this summer, following another 12-year-old's e-scooter death last year, the state is debating Abby's Law: no riders under 16, helmets under 18, a 20-mph cap. It is the obvious policy, and it should not have required two dead children to become obvious.[2]

So here is what to actually do. Wear a helmet, because the 60 percent of injured riders who skipped it are the control group in an experiment nobody signed up for. Ride lit: 10 percent of injuries involved visibility problems. Know your bike's class, since a class-3 machine is a moped in everything but paperwork and deserves moped-grade paranoia. Never modify the battery, and charge it somewhere that is not your bedroom; half of the UK fire incidents started in homes or garages. And if you are buying one for a kid, remember the sharp end of this data is a 12-year-old versus a pickup truck. Pennsylvania is about to make that combination illegal, but you do not need a law to reach the same conclusion.[1][3]
