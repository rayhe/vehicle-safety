The Gap

Your Backup Camera Is Federally Required. GM's Glue Failed on 306,511 of Them.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 22, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. General Motors recalled 306,511 of its most popular small SUVs on September 17 because the rearview camera can display a distorted or completely blank image the instant you shift into reverse.[1][2] Since May 2018, federal law has required a working backup camera on every new car sold in America. GM just recalled three hundred thousand of the things because the glue failed.

33,000
Owner complaints tied to the affected build window, a 10.8% complaint rate against the recalled population

Start with the defect, because it is almost insultingly mundane. During camera assembly, a plasma-cleaning step weakened the adhesive bond sealing the camera housing.[2] Adhesive fails. Moisture and grime seep in over months, until the video feed distorts, flickers, or dies. Nobody hacked anything. No software update pushed the wrong line of code. A factory cleaning process and a glue joint had a disagreement, and now a third of a million federally mandated safety devices are slowly filling with water.

159,821
2025 Chevrolet Trax units recalled: 52% of the entire campaign, GM's best-selling subcompact

Now the concentration, which is the part that should annoy you. More than half the recalled population is a single model: the 2025 Chevrolet Trax, at 159,821 units. The 2025 Trailblazer adds 73,665, the 2024–2025 Buick Envista adds 41,323, and the 2024–2026 Buick Encore GX adds 30,852, with 850 additional 2024 and early-2026 Trailblazers rounding out the count.[2] These are not niche trims. The Trax is one of the best-selling subcompacts in the country, which means the used-car lots are about to be full of 2024–2026 GM crossovers with cameras that may or may not work, and the window sticker will not tell you which.

Do the complaint math, because nobody else is. 33,000 complaints against 306,511 recalled vehicles is a complaint rate of 10.8% (33,000 ÷ 306,511). For context, NHTSA investigations routinely open on dozens of complaints against millions of vehicles in the field. GM says it started looking into dead backup cameras last year, found no clear pattern, and reopened the case this year as the complaints kept arriving.[2] One in ten owners of the affected build window bothered to complain, which means the silent majority, nine in ten drivers whose cameras may be quietly fogging over, is the scary part.

And here is the remedy gap, the part where consumer anger is fully justified. Dealers are instructed to inspect and replace the camera if necessary.[4] Read that again, slowly: not replace the cameras, but inspect them, and replace only the ones that look bad. Moisture intrusion is progressive: a camera that passes a visual check in October can fog over by March. The defect gets worse with time, and the remedy is a snapshot. If your image is intermittent, photograph it before the dealer visit, because "could not reproduce" is the oldest sentence in the warranty dictionary.

Fair is fair, so here is the strongest version of GM's defense. The company investigated, found the root cause, and filed the recall; it reports no crashes or injuries tied to the defect, which means the failure mode may degrade gracefully rather than catastrophically. Plasma cleaning is a standard, legitimate industrial process, and the failure was a process-window error, not a design contempt for safety. Every automaker on Earth recalls something every year. All true.

But none of it changes what you should do. If you own a 2024–2026 Trax, Trailblazer, Envista, or Encore GX: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today, not when the letter arrives, because letters get lost and moisture does not wait.[1] Test the camera in reverse this week and look specifically for flicker or soft distortion at the edges, the early signs of intrusion. If you are shopping these models used, and you will be, because there are 306,511 of them, verify the recall repair with a dealer service record, not just an open-recall lookup: an "inspected, no fault found" visit is not the same as a replaced camera. And if your camera ever goes blank mid-maneuver, file a complaint at NHTSA. GM says there have been no crashes or injuries, and the only way that stays true is if the failure database keeps getting fed.[1]

Three hundred six thousand federally required cameras, undone by one glue joint, and the remedy is a dealer eyeball check. Your move, dealer.
