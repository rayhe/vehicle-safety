Los Angeles Finally Turned On Its Speed Cameras. San Francisco's Numbers Say They Work.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 28, 2026

Los Angeles spent three years as the worst student in California's speed-camera class. On October 26, it finally showed up: the city switched on the first of 125 automated speed cameras, with 26 issuing warnings on day one and the rest scheduled to go live by the end of 2026.[1]

72%
Average reduction in speeding at San Francisco's 15 key camera locations after activation

San Francisco is the preview. SFMTA's one-year report found the share of drivers going at least 10 mph over the limit fell from 11% to 2% at camera locations, a 79% drop worth roughly 40,000 fewer speeding incidents every day.[5] Citywide traffic deaths fell from 43 to 25 between 2024 and 2025.[5] An advocate's claim of a 42% fatality drop lines up with that count, but honesty requires the caveat: San Francisco's decline came alongside other safety measures, so cameras alone cannot claim full credit.[2]

Each LA camera triggers only at 11 mph or more over the posted limit, photographs the rear plate only, and never captures a driver's face.[2] Every camera starts with a 60-day grace period of written warnings, not fines. After grace ends, a first 11-to-15-mph offense still draws a warning; repeats start at $50, 16 to 25 mph over costs $100, 26-plus costs $200, and 100-plus costs $500.[3] Citations are civil penalties against the registered owner, with no license points; low-income drivers can choose a payment plan or community service instead.[3] Non-violation photos must be deleted within five days, and data cannot be shared with any agency without a court order.[2] Running the program costs about $7.95 million a year; citation revenue first covers operations, and any surplus goes to traffic-calming projects within three years.[3]

LADOT picked corridors using crash history, speeding data, and proximity to schools, senior centers, and high-injury zones, with signs at every site noting camera enforcement.[2] Speeding contributed to roughly 20% of LA's fatal collisions in 2024, and 290 people died in LA traffic incidents in 2025, more than the city's homicide total that year.[2] That urgency makes the backstory sting: SAFE's December 2025 report card graded LA's implementation a D, worst of the seven pilot cities, while San Francisco earned an A-plus with all 33 of its permitted cameras issuing citations.[4] Assembly Bill 645, signed in October 2023, authorizes the pilots through January 1, 2032, so LA has six years to close a gap San Francisco closed in months.[2]

Drivers should check LAspeedsafety.org for the full location list before assuming a familiar corridor is unwatched. If a camera near you just went live, you have 60 days of warnings before a fine can land, time to learn where the 11-mph threshold bites on your commute. One honest gap remains: LA has no results yet, and the first fine data arrives in late December 2026 at the earliest. San Francisco's numbers say the cameras work. Los Angeles now has to prove it on its own streets, where speeding still kills more people than homicide.
