# Research: #861 — The National Decline Is a Net, Not a Trend

Journalist: Axle McScatter | Kicker: By The Numbers | Date: 2026-09-09

## Angle (1-2 sentences)

The National Safety Council's preliminary H1 2026 estimate (released Sept 8, 2026) shows 18,460 motor-vehicle deaths, down 2% nationally — but the net hides two opposing Americas: 13 states saved a combined 499 lives while 11 states plus DC added 378, and Illinois alone accounts for over a third of the entire national decline. The headline decline is an arithmetic artifact of aggregation, not a uniform national improvement.

## Original computations (the novel contribution)

1. **Net vs gross.** National decline = 386 deaths (18,846 to 18,460). The 13 big decliners saved 499; the 11 big gainers added 378. Net of the listed states: -121. The headline "-2%" is the residue of two large opposing movements, not a uniform drift.
2. **Illinois is doing a third of the country's work.** 134 / 386 = 34.7% of the entire national decline comes from one state. Without Illinois, the national decline shrinks to 252 deaths (-1.3%).
3. **California erased Illinois.** California added 178 deaths (+14%), more than wiping out Illinois's 134. Two states, net +44, and the "national decline" is what survives them.
4. **Small-denominator percentage trap.** DC's +133% headline = 12 deaths. Rhode Island +22% = 4 deaths. Vermont +18% = 4 deaths. The scariest percentages attach to the smallest counts; always read the absolute.
5. **NSC vs NHTSA scope gap.** NHTSA's H1 2025 early estimate was 17,140 deaths; NSC's revised H1 2025 count is 18,846 — a 1,706-death (~10%) gap on the same six months. The two organizations count different things: NHTSA's early estimates derive from FARS traffic-crash reporting on public roads; NSC aggregates state DOT reporters' all-motor-vehicle fatality estimates. Same country, same half-year, 10% apart — which means the "decline" you read about is partly a function of who is counting.
6. **The rate story is real even where the count story is noisy.** 1.12/100M VMT down 10.4% from H1 2024 is a genuine rate improvement with mileage rising — the exposure-adjusted trend is better than the raw count suggests.

## Thesis

"Down 2% nationally" is true and meaningless at the same time. The decline is real in rate terms (1.12, down double digits from 2024), but the count improvement is the net of a lopsided tug-of-war: Illinois and twelve other states did the work while California quietly added back more than Illinois removed. If your state is California, Delaware, Kentucky, or Alabama, the national press release about declining deaths is describing someone else's roads.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)

The decline is still real and broadly shared: 13 states posted double-digit drops, the mileage-adjusted rate fell 10.4% in two years, and this would be the fifth straight year of improvement if it holds. State-level noise is expected — California's +178 could be partly reporting-timing effects, and June 2026 showed 28 states up on a single-month basis, which smells like volatility, not reversal. The preliminary estimates also mature; the H1 2026 figure will be revised, possibly downward (NSC's H1 2024 initial estimate of 20,900 came in 3.1% above the NCHS final of 20,278). And the scope gap cuts both ways: NSC's wider net makes its count higher, but its trend direction matches NHTSA's, so the disagreement is about level, not direction.

## Limitations (state explicitly)

- NSC figures are preliminary estimates from state reporters at "comparable maturity," not final counts; revisions go both directions (their own documentation notes numbers often increase as fatalities are confirmed, yet H1 2024 was initially overestimated vs NCHS final).
- The state percentages are computed on six-month windows; small states swing wildly (DC's +133% = 12 deaths).
- NSC vs NHTSA scope difference is described from each organization's published methodology; the exact decomposition of the 1,706-death gap is not published.
- No causal attribution is available: Illinois's decline could reflect enforcement, VMT mix, reporting timing, or weather. This article decomposes the arithmetic; it does not explain it.

## Actionable insight

If you live in one of the 11 states + DC where deaths rose 10%+, the national decline headline does not describe your roads — California added 178 deaths while the country supposedly got safer. Check your state's trend before deciding the roads are fine, and drive like your state is California, not Illinois.
