Mercedes Will Let You Go Hands-Free at 85 MPH. You Still Own the Crash.

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Editor
November 25, 2026

On November 16, Mercedes-Benz announced that starting in December, select California owners will be invited to take their hands off the wheel at up to 85 mph.[1] Its name is Hands-Free Highway Assist. It steers, holds speed, and changes lanes on its own, with a companion system, MB.DRIVE ASSIST PRO, that follows the navigation route through city streets, turns at intersections, and stops at traffic lights.[1] Both are SAE Level 2, which means, in Mercedes’ own words, the driver remains responsible for the driving task, must remain attentive, and must be ready to take control at all times.[1] Hands off the wheel. Eyes on the road. Liability on you.

85
mph, the top speed of Mercedes’ new hands-free highway system

Run the takeover math, because the physics does not care about the marketing. At 85 mph a car covers 124.7 feet every second, so a driver who takes two seconds to notice an alert, find the wheel, and take over has traveled about 250 feet with nobody driving, and a full stop from 85 mph, reaction time included, runs past 400 feet. Mercedes says the system operates only on eligible controlled-access highways and only under certain conditions, which narrows the exposure, but it does not change the arithmetic: every second of inattention at that speed is a football field of unguided travel. California’s maximum speed limit is 65 mph on most highways, so the car will not be doing 85 legally in its launch state anyway, which makes the 85 mph ceiling a capability number, not a speed anyone will sustain, and a strange number to lead with.

Now compare the two Mercedes systems, because the comparison is the story. Drive Pilot is Level 3: the car drives, the driver may look away, and Mercedes accepts legal responsibility while it is engaged. California’s DMV permit caps it at 40 mph, daylight only, on designated highways.[2] Hands-Free Highway Assist is Level 2: the driver keeps watching, keeps the liability, and it runs to 85 mph. In Mercedes’ own lineup, speed and responsibility run in opposite directions, and the faster system is the one the manufacturer will not stand behind. A November 19 report says Drive Pilot has been dropped from the facelifted S-Class and EQS, with Mercedes saying it came too early.[7] Read that twice. The eyes-off car is gone. The hands-off car, at more than twice the speed, is what replaces it.

IIHS has already run the experiment on what drivers do with this kind of freedom, and the results should be taped to every dashboard. In two studies with MIT’s AgeLab, published in September 2024, drivers using Volvo’s Pilot Assist were much more likely to check their phones, eat, or do other visual-manual activities than when driving unassisted, and the habit grew as they got comfortable with the system.[3] One group spent more than 30 percent of its driving time distracted.[3] Tesla Autopilot drivers learned the timing of the attention reminders so well that alerts per 1,000 miles rose 26 percent over a month, because the drivers were gaming the warnings to keep multitasking.[3] IIHS president David Harkey put it bluntly: train people to think that paying attention means nudging the wheel every few seconds, and that is exactly what they will do.[3] Mercedes’ safeguard is the same class of attention requirement IIHS already watched drivers learn to beat.

State the defense at full strength, because it deserves to be heard before it is answered. Eighty-five is a ceiling, not a cruising speed, and the system runs only on mapped, controlled-access highways in suitable conditions, changing lanes only when conditions permit.[1] Mercedes is not asking drivers to look away. It is asking them to keep watching while the car handles the steering, which is a smaller ask than Drive Pilot’s eyes-off promise, and one with a much smaller blast radius if the driver complies. Fair enough. But compliance is the whole question, and IIHS has data on compliance: it decays with familiarity. The honest version of this launch is that Mercedes built a system whose safety case depends on drivers behaving better than the last two generations of drivers did with the same kind of system, at higher speed, with their hands off the wheel.

What this piece did not prove: no crash data exists for either new system, because neither has shipped to customers. IIHS tested Volvo and Tesla systems, not Mercedes’. The December early access program is a small invited cohort in California, drawn from owners of the 2026 and 2027 CLA Electric, 2027 GLC Electric, S-Class, GLE, and GLS, not a general release.[1] And the Drive Pilot discontinuation rests on a November 19 trade-press report, not a Mercedes statement.[7] What to do: if you are invited into the early access program, treat hands-free like cruise control with a longer leash, not a chauffeur. Keep your eyes on the road, because the law says the crash is yours. Check whether your insurer treats Level 2 engagement any differently before you pay for a subscription, since Mercedes has not announced a U.S. price yet.[1] And if you are shopping for a car on the strength of its driver assistance, ask which system the manufacturer will stand behind. In Mercedes’ lineup, the answer is the slow one.
