The Ford E-350 Gets Into More Fatal Crashes Than the Mustang. Its Drivers Are Sober.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 28, 2026

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. Line up every vehicle in America by how often it shows up in a fatal crash, adjusted for how many of them exist, and the Ford E-350 lands above the Ford Mustang. A white work van, most often driven by a sober professional, gets into fatal crashes more often per registered vehicle than a 460-horsepower sports car. From 2014 through 2023, the E-350 logged 721 fatal crash involvements per 100,000 registered vans. Mustang owners managed 692.[1]

721
Fatal crash involvements per 100,000 registered Ford E-350s, 2014–2023 (Mustang: 692)

That is not a typo, and it is not the strangest part. Only 15.7% of E-350 drivers in fatal crashes tested positive for alcohol or drugs, against a fleet average of 20.0% and 21.9% for the Mustang. These are not joyrides ending in ditches; these are workdays.

So what is a sober plumber doing in a fatal crash more often than a Mustang owner? Blame physics, and count the passengers. For decades, the E-350 served as America's default 15-passenger van: the church bus, the school shuttle, the team transport. NHTSA's research found that a 15-passenger van carrying 10 or more occupants rolls over at nearly three times the rate of a lightly loaded one, and a fully loaded van carries about five times the rollover risk of a van with just the driver.[2] Load the back, raise the center of gravity, and a panic maneuver becomes a rollover. Eighty percent of the people who die in those rollovers were not wearing seatbelts.[3]

Maybe all work vans look like this; they do not. Consider the Chevrolet Express, the E-350's direct rival in the same segment doing the same jobs: 223 fatal crash involvements per 100,000 registered vehicles. Its rate runs 3.2 times higher. Same ladder-frame recipe, same commercial buyers, and one of them crashes like it has a personal grudge against guardrails.

Fair is fair, so here is the counterweight at full strength. Per mile driven, the Mustang is still the bigger menace: 6.02 deaths per 100 million miles against the E-350's 2.51. A work van logs brutal mileage while a sports car mostly poses in driveways, and the involvement-per-vehicle metric cannot separate exposure from danger. Per mile, the E-350 isn't deadlier. It is simply exposed more, doing a harder job, with more bodies aboard. Also worth noting: 84.4% of the E-350's deaths come from pre-2012 model years. Old ones are the problem, and they are still out there.

If your church, school, or nonprofit runs one of these, the precautions are not mysterious: fill the front seats first, buckle every passenger, check tire pressure before every trip (NHTSA found 30% of 15-passenger vans rolling on a tire at least 8 psi low), keep loads off the roof, and hand the keys only to someone who has driven one before, or retire it.[4] Ford replaced the E-350 van with the Transit after the 2014 model year, and the Transit shows up in fatal crashes 55 times per 100,000 registered vehicles, a thirteenth of the E-350's crash footprint. Check any van's recall status at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next trip.
