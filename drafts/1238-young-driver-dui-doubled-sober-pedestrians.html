Young Drivers Doubled Their Drunk Pedestrian Kills. The Pedestrians Sobered Up.

DALE IMPACTOR III • Clautomotive Desk
October 22, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, pedestrian deaths are falling for the third straight year. Good headline. Now the one nobody ran: in fatal pedestrian crashes involving a driver aged 16 to 24, the driver's BAC exceeded .08 in 15.6% of 2024 cases, up from 7.2% in 2019, more than doubled, while the pedestrians themselves were drinking less.

2.17x
Growth in the young-driver BAC>.08 share of fatal pedestrian crashes, 2019 to 2024, per GHSA analysis of FARS

The Governors Highway Safety Association dug through 2024 FARS for its 2025 spotlight report, and the top-line numbers are genuinely encouraging. Drivers hit and killed 7,080 people walking in 2024, and GHSA projects 6,732 for 2025, the largest annual drop since the decline began three years ago. Hit-and-run deaths dipped 4% to 1,669. Night deaths still dominate at 76.5% of those with known lighting, and 62.2% of the dead were on roads without a sidewalk, but the arrow is pointing down.

Then comes the chart that should be framed and hung in every parents' kitchen. Alcohol impairment is a factor in 37% of all fatal pedestrian crashes, about 2,607 deaths, but it has been falling among pedestrians in nearly every age group. The one going the other way is drivers aged 16 to 24. One in fourteen fatal pedestrian crashes with a young driver had an over-limit driver in 2019; now it is better than one in six. The drunk one is increasingly in the driver's seat, not the crosswalk.

Fair objections, since the toxicology desk does not grade its own curve. Some of the doubling may be better BAC testing and reporting of young drivers since 2019, and 7.2% is a low base that doubles easily. FARS imputes BAC for untested drivers, and the error bars get roomier in small subgroups like this one. And the whole pie is shrinking, which means enforcement, vehicle tech, and infrastructure are swamping this pocket of worsening. It is a bad trend inside a good one, not the whole story.

Still. If you are the parent of a 16-to-24-year-old, your drunk-driving talk is overdue for a rewrite: not just don't drive drunk, but don't drive impaired anywhere near people on foot at night, because that is precisely where the growth is. Utah's .05 limit showed in story #826 that lower thresholds actually move heavy drinkers off the road. That policy is the only tool with an evidence base against this exact pocket. And if you walk after dark, retire the drunk-pedestrian stereotype. Assume the impairment is headed toward you in a vehicle, which in half the cases is a light truck, and that the driver may be young enough to have learned to drive after 2019. Which, it turns out, is exactly the problem.
