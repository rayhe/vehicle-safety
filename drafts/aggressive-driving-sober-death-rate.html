47% of Fatal Crashes Involve Aggressive Driving. Here Are the Cars That Kill Sober People Fastest.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
July 28, 2026

A LendingTree analysis of 2024 NHTSA data, published this week, found that 47.2% of fatal crashes in Arkansas involved aggressive, careless, or inattentive driving.[1] Louisiana hit 46.2%. New Jersey 45.1%. Colorado 39.7%. Utah reported zero. None of those studies name the vehicles. FARS does.

of drivers in FARS fatal crashes tested negative for alcohol and drugs, 2014–2023

We built a metric nobody publishes: the sober death rate. Take each vehicle's fatality rate per 100 million miles driven, then strip out the impaired fraction using FARS toxicology. What remains is the rate at which stone-sober drivers die behind the wheel of that specific car. It captures everything impairment doesn't: speed, distraction, road rage, running a red, texting, falling asleep, and occasionally just piloting a vehicle whose crash geometry can't protect you from your own mistake.

Across 180,728 driver deaths in the FARS dataset, an estimated 144,831 were sober. Eighty percent. Four out of five people who died in a car crash in America over the past decade were not drunk, not high, not anything. They were just driving.[2]

Among current-production vehicles, the Ford Mustang leads with a sober death rate of 4.70 per 100 million VMT, nearly five times the sedan class average. An estimated 2,139 sober people died in Mustangs over the observation window. Behind it: the Nissan Maxima at 4.04, the Chevrolet Camaro at 2.65, and the Honda Accord at 2.46. Nobody thinks of an Accord as dangerous, and yet more sober drivers have died in Accords than in any sports car in the database.[2]

Volume explains part of it. Honda sold millions of Accords. But even after normalizing for miles driven, the Accord's sober rate of 2.46 is still 2.4 times the sedan class average of 1.02. Nissan's Altima sits at 2.30. Chevy's Malibu at 1.61. America's default sedans kill sober drivers at rates that should embarrass the industry.[2]

LendingTree's state-level data assigns 47% of Arkansas's fatal crashes to aggressive or careless behavior.[1] Colorado hit 39.7% while Utah reported zero, a 40-point gap between neighboring states sharing terrain and weather but not enforcement regimes. Something fills the gap between "not drunk" and "dead," and the state data suggests it is speed, distraction, and rage.

Among SUVs, the Chevrolet Tahoe leads with a sober rate of 1.98. Ford's Expedition follows at 1.85. Jeep's Cherokee at 1.42. Every one marketed as a family hauler. Pickups have slightly higher impairment, so their sober-adjusted rates converge with mid-tier sedans. Both the Silverado and F-150 each logged more than 7,400 sober deaths, but their sober rates land at 0.99 and 0.84 respectively, below the sedan average. Per mile, a sober Accord driver faces more danger than a sober F-150 driver. Not a defense of trucks. An indictment of sedans.

Calculation: sober death rate = vehicle fatality rate per 100M VMT × (1 − impairment fraction). For the Mustang: 6.02 × (1 − 0.219) = 4.70. FARS toxicology supplies the impairment fraction: the share of that vehicle's fatally-crashed drivers who tested positive for alcohol or drugs. This is an estimate. FARS toxicology testing rates vary by state, and in jurisdictions with low testing rates, some impaired deaths go uncounted, inflating the sober figure. Any bias would understate the problem for the impaired and overstate it for the sober. Even with generous error bars, four out of five is not close to one out of two.

If you are shopping for a car and you don't drink, don't text, and don't speed, your risk still varies by more than 5× depending on what you drive. Toyota's Highlander has a sober rate of 0.34. Subaru's Outback: 0.36. Honda's Pilot: 0.23. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, buy the lowest-rate vehicle in your class, and accept that American road safety starts well before anyone touches a bottle.
