28,739 Recall Records Warn of a Crash. 3,341 Warn of Death.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 23, 2027

Every recall record carries a consequence statement, and it sits below the part of the letter you stopped reading. It answers one question: what happens if the defect never gets fixed. On October 19, ForCar founder Denis Kataev published a tabulation of all 45,996 of them, spanning 9,791 campaigns back to 1981. Sixty-two percent warn explicitly of a crash.

I pulled 74 recent campaigns from NHTSA's own API to check his math. Modern records are blunter still: 89 percent mention a crash, 11 percent a fire, and not one says "death." That is not softening. Call it the industry's euphemism ladder: death moved down into "risk of injury," which is corporate English for the same outcome with better legal manners.

October 2026's files read like hazard haikus. Hyundai's Genesis seat-motor recall (26V664000) reads "A fire increases the risk of injury": five words, zero hedging. Volvo's EX30 headrest cushion (26V671000): "A detached head restraint cushion can increase the risk of injury during a crash." A broken speedometer (26V653000): "A speedometer that fails to display accurate vehicle speed can increase the risk of a crash." Somebody at each manufacturer got paid to write the plainest sentence of their career, and it shows.

Labels are the third most-recalled component in the corpus, at 1,412 records for stickers and placards. A missing tire-pressure placard or a wrong weight rating leads straight to underinflation or overloading, so a sticker recall carries consequence language indistinguishable from a brake recall. Triage your recall mail by how trivial the component sounds and you will misrank the danger.

This bluntness is legal, not literary: the sentences come from Part 573 defect reports, where understatement creates liability rather than reducing it. A recall about a seat bracket says the occupant may be injured in a crash. A recall about a fuel line says the vehicle may catch fire. There is no softening because softening is legally worse.

Every one of these is free to fix, most with no time limit, and the only barrier is knowing: the letter goes to the registered address at campaign time, which for a used car is somebody else's house. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change. When the consequence statement mentions fire, book this week, because park-outside language means the car can burn in your driveway with the ignition off. Two caveats apply: those 3,341 "death" records are mostly theoretical worst cases across 45 years, not body counts, and ForCar counted records, meaning model-year line items, so large campaigns weigh heavier than small ones. My 74-campaign sample is recent-model heavy and keyword matched by hand. It survived all of it: the bluntest safety writing in the industry is the fine print nobody reads.
