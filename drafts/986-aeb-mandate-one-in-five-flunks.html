AEB Is Mandatory by 2029. One in Five Systems Flunks the Hard Test.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 24, 2026

Here’s a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. By September 2029, federal law requires every new car and light truck in America to brake itself: full avoidance of a vehicle ahead at up to 62 miles per hour, pedestrian detection in daylight and in darkness.[3] That mandate assumes performance that, per a census published this week, 117 of 524 rated vehicles — more than one in five — cannot currently deliver: a Poor or Marginal score on the IIHS front crash prevention test.[1]

22%
Of 524 vehicles with an IIHS front crash prevention rating score Poor or Marginal — and every one of them has automatic emergency braking fitted.

This is not the test your car passed. Under the old IIHS evaluation — the one we wrote about in May — 85 percent of 2022 models earned the top rating, because the test topped out at 25 miles per hour in daylight. The current version adds a pedestrian target, adds darkness, and adds speed.[1] In February 2024, IIHS folded its day and night pedestrian tests into one evaluation, and now an acceptable rating is required for any Top Safety Pick award.[2] The cars did not get worse. The question got harder, and the answers changed immediately.

Failure is not evenly distributed, and Jeep leads the casualty list: 14 of its 23 rated vehicles come back Poor or Marginal, a 61 percent failure rate, nearly triple the fleet average. Chevrolet and Ford each fail half their rated lineups.[1] And here is the part that should embarrass the marketing departments: Audi, whose entire identity is engineering excellence, fails 13 of 37. Price predicts almost nothing; hardware generation does — camera-only systems go blind in low light, camera-and-radar combinations see in the dark — and the gap between two model years of the same car can exceed the gap between two brands.

Which brings us back to the mandate: FMVSS 127 requires pedestrian detection in darkness by September 2029, projecting 360 lives and 24,000 injuries saved per year.[3] The industry petitioned for reconsideration within weeks; the revised rule kept the requirements nearly intact, and the Alliance for Automotive Innovation’s president called it a disastrous decision that “won’t really improve driver or pedestrian safety.”[4] He may be wrong, but the census gives his complaint an uncomfortable shape: Washington is mandating performance that one-fifth of the current fleet cannot demonstrate.

The honest counterargument deserves its paragraph: front crash prevention is among the most proven safety technologies ever fitted to cars, and even a Poor-rated system still brakes for a stationary car in daylight.[1] The new test is a stretch goal, not a minimum bar. The fleet is improving (26 percent failed in 2024, 19 percent in 2026), and the mandate will force the laggards onto better hardware by 2029 anyway. Do not keep driving your 2012 sedan with no AEB at all because a 2024 model’s system is “only” Marginal. That is not how probability works.

What this means for you: the feature list lies by omission. “Automatic emergency braking” on a listing covers everything from a system that stops for a pedestrian at night to one that will not. Check the IIHS front crash prevention rating for the exact model year — not the model, the year — before you buy.[5] Treat the system as a backstop for your attention, not a substitute. The universe does not grade on a curve, and neither does a crosswalk at midnight.
