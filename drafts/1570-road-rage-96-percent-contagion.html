By The Numbers

96% of Drivers Admit to Driving Aggressively. The Federal Crash Count Sees 699 of Them.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 24, 2026

According to the surveys, and there are a lot of them, you are an aggressive driver, and so am I, and so is nearly everyone reading this sentence. AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety asked more than 3,000 licensed drivers about their behavior behind the wheel over the past year, and 96% admitted to at least one aggressive driving behavior.[1] Not 96% of some subgroup or 96% of young men in sports cars: 96% of everybody.

11%
Drivers who admitted to violent actions, like intentionally bumping another car or confronting another driver. That is roughly 26 million people.

Read that number again, slowly, because the survey's softer numbers let you off too easily. Ninety-two percent admitted to behaviors that put others at risk, speeding or cutting people off, which is the kind of admission that sounds almost polite until you remember what it describes. Eleven percent admitted to violence, intentionally bumping another car or getting out to confront another driver. Apply that 11% to the roughly 236.5 million licensed drivers on American roads and you get about 26 million people who have done something violent with a two-ton machine and then told a survey taker about it, one driver in nine, so count nine cars at the next light.

AAA ran this study before, in 2016, which is what makes the new numbers worth more than a shrug. Aggression did not fall over nine years; it changed shape. Cutting off other vehicles is up 67% and honking out of anger is up 47%. Tailgating is down 24% and yelling at other drivers is down 17%, which sounds like progress until you notice what replaced them. Drivers stopped following too close and started swerving in front of each other instead. Hostility moved from behind the other car to beside it, where it is harder to ignore and easier to escalate.

Escalation is the study's real finding, and it is the one nobody quoted. AAA found that drivers exposed to higher levels of aggressive driving also engaged in higher levels of it. Exposure predicts engagement, which is to say aggression is contagious. Road etiquette, signals, letting someone merge, a wave, showed up in the data as a protective factor, which is the polite way of saying that courtesy might be contagious too, and that the driver who lets you in is doing more than being nice. Asked why they did it, drivers gave the two oldest answers in traffic: to get there faster, and to avoid perceived danger. Speed and fear, the whole American driving psyche in two phrases.

Young drivers lead the table, as they lead every table nobody wants to top. Thirty-six percent of drivers aged 16 to 24 showed high levels of aggressive driving, against 10% of drivers 75 and older.[6] More than half of all drivers wondered in the past year whether another driver had a weapon in their vehicle. One in five personally knows someone who has been threatened by a weapon in a road rage incident. Fear is now a standard feature of the American commute, right up there with cupholders.

Now the part that should bother a data desk. While 92% of drivers admit to behavior that puts others at risk, the National Safety Council's analysis of federal crash data found police coded aggressive driving or road rage as a factor for just 699 vehicles involved in fatal crashes in 2024, out of 36,297 fatal crashes, fewer than 1 in 50.[2] FARS can count bodies and blood alcohol and seat belts. It cannot count a middle finger, a brake-check, or a horn held down for ten seconds, so it mostly does not try. What the system can see is speed, and speed alone killed 11,288 people in 2024, 29% of the entire toll.[5] Speed is the one aggressive behavior with a body count attached, and it carries nearly a third of all road deaths by itself.

Meanwhile the most-cited aggressive driving statistic in American traffic safety is old enough to rent a car. You have heard it a hundred times: aggressive driving causes two-thirds of traffic deaths. It traces to one sentence from NHTSA Administrator Ricardo Martinez, testifying to Congress in July 1997, who said about two-thirds of the 41,907 deaths in 1996 could be attributed to behavior associated with aggressive driving, a claim now twenty-nine years old, built on 1996 deaths and repeated ever since as if it were measured yesterday.[7] If the two-thirds claim were literally true of 2024, aggressive driving would account for roughly 26,000 of the 39,254 deaths. Compare that with the 699 vehicles police actually coded. One number overcounts by motive, the other undercounts by paperwork, and they disagree by a factor of 37. Somewhere between them sits a real number that nobody has measured.

Guns make the gap worse, and the trend is the ugliest line in this story. People shot in road rage incidents rose from 92 in 2014 to 481 in 2023, a 423% increase, with 777 people killed across the decade.[3] Total road deaths rose about 20% over the same span, from 32,744 to 39,254. Gun violence on the road grew roughly 21 times faster than road deaths overall. Everytown's 2025 count found 883 news-documented firearm road rage incidents in a single year, with 114 deaths.[4] Four hundred eighty-one people shot is 1.2% of the annual road death toll, which is worth stating plainly, because shootings are not the road death crisis. They are a small, fast-growing, entirely preventable slice of it, and they are the slice that makes half of all drivers wonder whether the car next to them is armed.

Fairness requires the skeptic's case, and it is strong. First, the 96% figure is partly a survey artifact. Ask 3,000 drivers whether they have ever sped up at a yellow light or honked in annoyance and nearly everyone says yes, because nearly everyone has, which makes the definition of aggressive so broad that it captures ordinary driving. Second, the only number tied to actual dead bodies is the 699-vehicle FARS count, and it is small, which suggests that whatever is happening on the road, the thing that kills people in crashes is still speed, alcohol, distraction, and no seat belt, not a gesture. Third, the shooting trend starts from a tiny base, and 481 people shot in a year when 39,254 died in crashes is a rounding error in the toll. Fourth, the contagion claim confuses selection with causation: aggressive people cluster on the same congested roads at the same hours, so of course exposure and engagement correlate. All of that is correct. Against it, the 11% violent-admission figure survives the survey-breadth objection, because bumping a car on purpose is not ordinary driving. The 67% rise in cutting off and the 47% rise in angry honking are changes inside the same survey instrument, so the trend is real even if the level is debatable. And the shooting curve, 92 to 481, is measured in bodies, not opinions. Aggression is both overcounted by surveys and undercounted by police reports. The honest sentence is that the truth is a number nobody has.

What this does not prove matters too. The survey is self-reported, and people lie to survey takers in both directions. FARS coding depends on police reports that vary by jurisdiction and rarely capture motive, so 699 is a floor, not a count. The Gun Violence Archive and Everytown series measure different things, people shot versus news-documented incidents, and cannot be compared directly. The contagion finding is correlational: AAA's design cannot prove exposure causes engagement, though the focus groups point that way. And the age split, the 53% weapon figure, and the 7% road rage figure come from the report's tables as summarized by secondary sources, not from AAA's newsroom release itself.

What to do with all of this, starting tomorrow, because Thanksgiving week is the heaviest driving week of the year. AAA's own advice, backed by its own data: do not engage, with no eye contact, no gestures, and no response; let the aggressive driver pass, and if threatened, call 911 or drive to a public place, but never drive home. Second, the study found etiquette is a protective factor, so use your signals, let someone merge, wave them in. Courtesy may be contagious too, and it is the only intervention in this entire story that costs nothing. Third, leave early. AAA found the top motivator for aggressive behavior was getting to the destination faster, and a 15-minute head start is cheaper than a confrontation. Fourth, if you have a teen driver, the conversation before Thanksgiving travel is not about their driving. It is about everyone else's, because 36% of their age group runs hot and the other 64% shares the road with them. Fifth, if you are shopping for a car, know that AAA found the vehicle plays a role: sports cars, big trucks, and motorcycles are perceived as more aggressive, and some drivers said they felt more powerful and dominant in them. Buy the car for the driver you want to be, not the one the road brings out.
