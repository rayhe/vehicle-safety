NHTSA Is Deleting Its Rollover Test. The Replacements Keep Slipping.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 13, 2026

In three days, the comment period closes on a federal proposal to delete two vehicle safety tests that have been on the books for more than two decades. NHTSA wants to pull the dynamic rollover Fishhook maneuver and the side airbag out-of-position checks out of NCAP, the program behind the five-star ratings on every new car's window sticker. The cuts take effect with model year 2027; the upgrades they are supposed to pay for do not arrive until 2029 at the earliest, and some not until 2033.[1]

15 years
Since any vehicle tipped up during the NCAP Fishhook test. NHTSA's own regression equations show a tip-up moved the calculated rollover risk only 1.6 to 4.8 percentage points: the tape measure was doing the heavy lifting all along.

Congress ordered the dynamic rollover test in the TREAD Act of 2000, the law written in the wreckage of the Ford Explorer and Firestone tire crisis. NHTSA added the Static Stability Factor in 2001 and the Fishhook maneuver in 2003, and the rollover star has appeared on the Monroney label since 2007. In 2005, the highway bill wrote 15-passenger vans into the program, because loaded vans were rolling and killing people. Physically, the test is a steering flick: a hard input one way, then a violent correction back, meant to imitate a driver overcooking an evasive maneuver. If both inside wheels lift two inches off the pavement at the same moment, the vehicle tipped up.[1]

NHTSA's case for killing it is stronger than it looks. Run the agency's own two regression equations and a tip-up shifts the calculated rollover risk by only a few percentage points; all along, the tape measure did the heavy lifting while the stunt driving was a rounding error with a steering robot attached. Electronic stability control, required across the fleet since the FMVSS 126 phase-in finished in 2011 and verified by a nastier maneuver called sine-with-dwell, made the Fishhook redundant. IIHS's numbers back the agency: ESC reduces the risk of fatal single-vehicle rollovers by 75 percent for SUVs and 72 percent for cars, and NHTSA credits the technology with roughly 4,100 lives saved from 2010 to 2014.[3] A test that passes everyone tells buyers nothing. Deleting it is defensible.

Deleting it is also the easy half. NHTSA banks the savings starting with model year 2027 to fund upgrades that, in the very same filing, just slid another two years to the right. For the replacements: the advanced THOR frontal dummy, plus a small-female dummy in the driver's seat, arrives in the fourth quarter of 2029. Its frontal oblique test, the one that finally reflects how cars actually hit each other, follows in 2029. Smallest occupants wait longest: the small-female THOR, the dummy built to measure what happens to a 108-pound adult in a crash, debuts in 2031, and its side-impact equivalent in 2033. And the crash-avoidance rating Congress ordered in the FAST Act of 2015, then ordered again in the 2021 infrastructure law, is now penciled in for 2028.[2] Thirteen years.

Meanwhile the second deletion moves a whole category of occupant protection to the honor system. There is no federal standard governing how hard a side airbag may strike a child leaning against the door; the out-of-position requirements in FMVSS 208 cover frontal airbags only. The side airbag protocol was written in the late 1990s by an industry working group, and NCAP was the only mechanism auditing the homework. Starting with model year 2026, manufacturers merely attest they passed and hand over a report number. NHTSA's own retrospective is brutally candid: roughly two failures a year across two decades, none ever tied to a real-world injury, and results so non-reproducible by design that the agency could never have opened a recall proceeding off one.[2] So yes, a check that can never trigger enforcement is arguably worse than no check. It is still true that a category of protection that was never federally regulated is about to stop being federally audited.

What this means for your next car purchase is blunt. From model year 2027 on, the rollover star is decided almost entirely by one ratio: half the track width divided by center-of-gravity height, measured once. That rewards exactly one thing, being low and wide. An EV with hundreds of kilograms of battery bolted to the floor buys a healthy score before its chassis engineers do anything clever, while rollover-triggered curtain airbags and roll-rate-sensor stability control earn no rating credit above the legal floor. Some buyers lose more than others. Anyone buying a 15-passenger van for a church, school, or shuttle loses the only dynamic test ever applied to the class Congress wrote into the program, even though a van's center of gravity climbs with every passenger and every bag on the roof. Parents of small children lose the only federal check on side airbags firing into leaning kids.

So do three things. First, if you have a view on any of this, file a comment at regulations.gov under docket NHTSA-2026-1651 by October 16. Second, when you shop a 2027 or newer vehicle, read the rollover star as geometry only and ask the dealer specifically about rollover-triggered curtain airbags and roll stability control, because the stars will not tell you. Third, keep small children out of the front seat and out of the path of seat-mounted side airbags: that 2003-era worry about a child leaning against the door is now a manufacturer's self-attestation, not a tested claim.

Limitations: Every program statistic here comes from NHTSA's own notice; there is no independent replication inside a 60-day comment window. Those roadmap dates are proposed, not final, and public comments can move them. FARS cannot isolate the predictive power of the Fishhook maneuver, so there is no cross-tab proving what the deletion costs in lives; the honest framing is lost rating information, not observed harm. And ESC's success story carries a footnote the agency underplays: fleet turnover spreads the protection slowly, with the oldest, cheapest, most rollover-prone vehicles on the road carrying the least of it.

A safety rating is worth exactly the distance between it and the law. That gap is the product, the whole reason a star on a window sticker ever changed an engineering decision. Everything in this notice narrows the gap, and everything that would widen it again keeps sliding to the right. NHTSA is right that the Fishhook stopped telling anyone anything useful. It is right that a test it could never enforce was worse than no test. But deleting the procedure that no longer fails anybody is the easy half of the job. Writing one that does is the hard half, and the hard half is now scheduled for sometime around 2033.
