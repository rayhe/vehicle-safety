One in Five Trucks Failed Inspection. The Brakes Failed Most.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 5, 2026

Fifty-four thousand five hundred seventy-five trucks rolled through inspection stations across North America in three days in May, and inspectors parked 10,350 of them, one in five. Not for a burned-out marker light, though there was plenty of that too. The single largest reason, by a margin that has not moved in two years, was the brakes.[1]

19%
of inspected trucks ordered off the road in 72 hours

Do the brake math first, because it is the whole story in miniature. Inspectors logged 13,924 vehicle violations, and brake systems accounted for 3,379 of them, while the separate category for trucks with 20 percent or more of their brakes defective added 2,072 more. Combined: 5,451 brake violations, or 39.1 percent of everything wrong with a truck that inspectors found. That works out to one brake violation for every 8.1 vehicles inspected. Last year the brake share was 41.1 percent. Two years of data. Same verdict. The thing that stops eighty thousand pounds is the thing most likely to be broken.[1][2]

Now the inspection paradox. A Level I inspection is the full 37-step procedure, inspector under the truck, hands on everything, and it failed 24.8 percent of vehicles. A Level V inspection happens at a terminal with no driver present, and it failed 3.8 percent. Same fleet, same continent, same week, so which number describes the trucks next to you on the interstate? The honest answer is the first one, and the headline rate of 19 percent is the diluted average of both. One in four trucks fails the real inspection. Sit with that at seventy miles an hour, in the rain, behind a tanker.[1]

Drivers had their own leaderboard, and it flipped. In 2025 the top reason a driver got parked was hours of service, 1,076 violations. This year the top spot belongs to the medical card, or rather its absence: 1,072 drivers put out of service for missing or expired medical certification, up from 493 a year ago. That is a 117 percent jump in a single year, and a paperwork violation now parks more drivers than fatigue does, which says something unflattering about both the paperwork and the fatigue. Hours of service fell to second, and no commercial license took third. English proficiency, never before in the top five, debuted at fourth with 361 violations, 9.1 percent of the driver total. And the year's special focus, electronic logging device tampering, produced 146 out-of-service orders, the seventh most common driver violation. Somebody, somewhere, is still rewriting the little black box.[1][2]

Here is the number that should keep you up at night, and it is not the one you expect. Inspectors cited 667 drivers for not wearing a seat belt, about 1.25 percent of everyone inspected. Sounds small, so now open the fatality file. In 2021, the last year with a published split, 64 percent of truck drivers killed in crashes were not wearing a belt, 555 of 872 dead drivers, and roughly three-quarters of the unbelted dead were thrown clear out of the cab. Run the ratio and an unbelted truck driver is on the order of 140 times overrepresented among the dead relative to their share of drivers on the road. One in eighty skips the belt, and they are nearly two-thirds of the funerals.[1][3]

Fair objections, stated plainly. Roadcheck is a blitz, announced months ahead, with focus areas published in advance. Fleets clean up before the inspectors arrive, so the failure rates are a best case, not a representative sample. Inspectors also work the corridors where violations concentrate, which pushes rates the other way. Both selection effects are real, and they partly cancel. What survives is the year-over-year comparison, same method, same three days, same inspectors working the same corridors, and it shows near-stasis: brakes still near forty percent, one in five trucks still parked, and all the churn confined to the driver leaderboard while the vehicle side barely twitched. The medical-card surge may also reflect enforcement emphasis rather than a sudden epidemic of expired paperwork; a new verification push will find violations that were always there. And the 140-times figure crosses years and sampling frames, 2021 fatalities against 2026 inspections, so treat it as an order of magnitude rather than a precise multiplier, because a blitz changes driver behavior while it is happening and the dead cannot be re-surveyed. Even the friendlier denominator, the FMCSA estimate that 14 percent of commercial drivers skip the belt, still leaves the unbelted overrepresented by double digits.[2][3]

So what do you do with this? If you drive a car, give trucks room, especially downhill, and remember the truck in your mirror has a one-in-four chance of failing a full inspection. If you run a fleet, brakes plus tires were 60 percent of every vehicle violation. A pre-trip brake check is the cheapest compliance in the industry. If you drive a truck, the belt is the whole game. One in eighty skips it, and they are nearly two-thirds of the dead drivers. And if your medical card has a date on it, check the date. It parked more drivers this year than fatigue did. Buckle up.
