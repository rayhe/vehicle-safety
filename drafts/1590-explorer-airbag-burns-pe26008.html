The Airbag Saved Them From the Crash, Then It Burned Them

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

On February 20, 2025, a UPS truck hit the right front of a 2022 Ford Explorer Police Interceptor. Side airbags deployed exactly as designed, the officer walked away from the impact, and then the burns showed up: his vest burned, three layers of clothing underneath burned, and what was left underneath needed surgery and a skin graft.[3] Nothing in that sentence is a malfunction. That is the problem NHTSA is now investigating, in a probe covering 1,236,155 Ford Explorers from model years 2021 through 2026.[1]

1,236,155
2021-2026 Ford Explorers under preliminary evaluation PE26008. Ten burn injuries reported across 11 incidents, with zero deaths in the federal file.

Start with what the federal file actually contains, because it is thin and NHTSA says so by opening the probe at all. Eight vehicle owner questionnaires and four Early Warning Reporting death-and-injury reports, deduplicated to 11 incidents and 10 injuries. Burns were reported for drivers and front passengers alike, and NHTSA's summary says they were typically diagnosed as second- or third-degree burns requiring medical treatment. Several complaints involve law enforcement officers in Police Interceptors, which makes sense once you think about it: cops drive Explorers all day, crash them at work, and file paperwork about it.[1]

Now the engineering, because this is where a safety device becomes a design question. Side airbags inflate faster than frontal bags, because they have to: there is almost no space between an occupant and whatever is coming through the door, so the bag has milliseconds to get between you and the intrusion. Fast inflation means a pyrotechnic inflator, and a pyrotechnic inflator means hot gas that has to go somewhere after the bag fills. It goes out a vent.[5] Vents are not an afterthought. Somebody chose where each one points, and somebody signed off on that choice.

Three of the eight complaints describe the same arrangement, unprompted: the exhaust vent facing inward, toward the person in the seat, instead of outward toward the door, and the sentence is worth reading twice. Three strangers, in three separate crashes, in three separate vehicles, all noticed the same thing about a component they had never thought about before the day it burned them. NHTSA's own resume says nothing about vents, so treat this as a hypothesis drawn from complaint text, not a federal finding. But 37.5 percent agreement among uncoordinated complainants on a detail this specific is the kind of signal that makes an engineer stop scrolling.

Somebody will quote the population rate at you, so let me do the math first and then tell you why the math is incomplete. Ten injuries divided by 1,236,155 vehicles is 0.0008 percent, which sounds like nothing, but it is also the wrong denominator. Burns can only happen when a side airbag actually deploys, and nobody publishes how many Explorers in this fleet have ever had a side airbag go off. Ten injuries out of every Explorer ever sold is the floor of the true rate, not the rate itself, and the ceiling is unknowable from public data. That gap is not a footnote; it is the whole reason the frequency question exists, and it is the question PE26008 was opened to answer.[1]

Fairness demands the other side, stated at full strength. Eight complaints across 1.24 million SUVs is statistical noise, and a side airbag that vents hot gas in a violent crash is doing exactly what physics requires of it. Ten injuries, zero deaths, in a fleet that has absorbed thousands of side impacts, could be read as proof the system works: the bag took the hit, the occupant lived, and the burns were the price of the milliseconds that saved them. Maybe the three vent reports are three people describing the same normal deployment from three different angles. NHTSA opens preliminary evaluations on thin files constantly, and most of them close with no action. All of that is true, and none of it answers the vent question, which is why the probe exists.

What do you do with a probe that is not a recall? If you own a 2021 through 2026 Explorer, nothing is being fixed and nothing is being mailed to you, because there is no recall and no repair, so run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls anyway. That lookup surfaces open investigations on your vehicle alongside recalls, and it takes ninety seconds. If a deployed side airbag ever burned you or a passenger, file a complaint at NHTSA.gov or call the Vehicle Safety Hotline at 888-327-4236. Complaints are the entire reason this probe exists; eight of them opened a case covering 1.24 million vehicles. Police fleets running Interceptors should log every airbag burn, however minor, because NHTSA's frequency case is built from reports that exist, and unfiled burns do not exist in the data. If you are shopping for a used Explorer, add the VIN check to the pre-purchase routine, next to the Carfax and the cold start.

One more thing worth saying plainly is that this is a preliminary evaluation, the first rung on NHTSA's ladder, and it ends one of three ways: closed, escalated to an engineering analysis, or a manufacturer recall. Ford had not commented publicly on the probe as of November 9, and no defect has been found. The vent-direction pattern comes from complaint text as read by reporters, not from NHTSA's resume, and three complaints cannot prove a design flaw. But a device that saves your life and then puts you in a burn unit deserves a closer look than eight complaints usually get, and NHTSA is giving it one.
