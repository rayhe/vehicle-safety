## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0/10
No individual victims are named or dramatized, no crash gore, no moral judgment of Mississippi or Maine residents. The piece frames the gap as a geographic fact about roads and exposure, not a verdict on the people who live there. The small-state caveats are honest rather than performative. Nothing here exploits grief for clicks.

## 4. Social / Shareability — 8.8/10
Headline is built to travel: a national improvement undercut by one state's 31% jump is a ready-made argument in a feed. The 5.4x pull stat is a quotable number, and the state-specific framing gives every reader a reason to check their own state. Slight deduction: the piece has no single villain or hero to argue about, which costs it a little heat, but the numbers carry it.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
Every factual claim traces to a cited primary source: NHTSA final FARS 2024 (DOT HS 813 809), the FARS Encyclopedia state tables, IIHS Fatality Facts 2024, and the NHTSA 2025 early-estimates press release. No statutes, case law, or regulatory claims are made, so there is nothing to misstate. "Lie by averaging" is flagged as rhetoric, not a factual claim about NHTSA.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.9/10
Original contribution is real and checkable: the 16-worse / 2-flat / 33-better count of the full state table, the 5.4x per-capita and 3.1x per-mile ratios, the Maine fixed-object cross-tab (38.9% vs 27.8% national), and Minnesota as the largest absolute increase among worsening states (+75). Limitations are explicit (FARS records counts, not causes; IIHS confounders listed; one year is not a trend) and the counterargument is stated at full strength, including the small-numbers problem. Pre-panel self-review caught and fixed two errors: a 4-fatality crash was first described as moving the needle "nearly 10 percent" when it is a tenth of the year's *increase*, and an unverified causal claim about Arizona highway driving was cut. Deduction: the piece does not test whether Maine's jump persists in 2025 data, because that data does not exist yet at state level.

## 7. Data Presentation — 8.8/10
Both denominators are explained in plain language before they are used, counts sit next to rates (753 vs 52 grounds the 5.4x ratio), and the Arizona example shows why the denominator matters instead of just asserting it. References are linked and specific. Minor: the per-mile paragraph never gives Arizona's per-capita rank explicitly, so a reader has to trust "merely bad" as a characterization; the number (16.2) is there, the rank is not.
