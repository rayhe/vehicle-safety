Utah Moved the Legal Line to 0.05. Four Years of Data Say It Worked.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 29, 2026

According to the toxicology reports — and there are a lot of them — buzzed driving was already drunk driving, and Utah just moved the line to match. In December 2018, Utah became the first and only state to lower its per se BAC limit from 0.08 to 0.05. A new IIHS study of the first four years reports alcohol-related crashes fell nearly 9 percent, and that number survived a stress test nobody designed for: a global pandemic with a drinking problem.[1]

9%
Drop in alcohol-related crashes over four years under Utah's 0.05 law (IIHS)

The study, by IIHS senior research scientist Angela Eichelberger and independent researcher Scott Tippets, did the unglamorous version of the job. It counted not just police reports of alcohol involvement but late-added alcohol-positive tests, and it counted crashes where the driver refused the BAC test. Then it compared Utah before and after against Arizona, Colorado, and Idaho.[2] Alcohol-involved injury crashes dropped 12 percent. Utah's alcohol-involved share of crashes fell 12 to 21 percent relative to the comparison states, which spent those four years getting worse, and that last part matters more than the headline.

Then came the stress test: alcohol-involved crashes in Utah spiked 46 percent from March through May 2020, and the average monthly alcohol share ran 6 percent above normal across the 34-month pandemic window. The pandemic erased the gains on a monthly chart and the four-year signal still held.[1] Policies that survive a lockdown-era drinking binge are policies worth taking seriously. Most DUI interventions never face anything harder than a budget cycle.

Here is the fine print, and Dale always reads the fine print. Utah was already the driest-driving state in America — a 2024 analysis had it lowest in the nation for alcohol-related fatality share, while South Carolina, Texas, New Mexico, Wyoming, and Montana topped the list.[2] A 12-21 percent relative decline on the smallest base in the country is a small number of actual bodies, which is the unit that matters. The comparison states' alcohol-involvement rates rose during the study window, so some of that "decline" is Utah standing still while the controls deteriorated. The law was also front-page news for more than a year before it took effect, which means some of the behavior change may have arrived before the legal change. And counting BAC-test refusals as alcohol-involved is an assumption wearing a lab coat. For national context: across FARS 2014-2023, 15.1 percent of drivers in fatal crashes were alcohol-positive, so a state starting at a 30 percent share has far more lives on the table than Utah ever did.[3]

Counterargument at full strength, because a columnist who skips it is a fan with a spreadsheet: you cannot cleanly separate the law from the year of publicity that preceded it. If drivers started self-policing in 2017 because the headlines told them 0.05 was coming, the study is measuring a media effect with legal dressing. Police-reported alcohol involvement is a judgment call, not a lab result. And the absolute gains on America's lowest baseline are, in body-count terms, modest.

So what do you do with this. If your state legislature floats a 0.05 bill, the only real-world experiment in American history says it works: 9 percent on alcohol crashes, 12 percent on injury crashes, four years, through a pandemic. IIHS president David Harkey puts it plainly: crash risk spikes noticeably at 0.05, and a lower limit sends the message that buzzed driving is drunk driving.[2] The NTSB recommended 0.05 back in 2013. Utah remains the only state listening. Forty-nine states are an experiment waiting to happen, and now they have a control group.
