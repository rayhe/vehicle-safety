Existential Dread

The First Federal Robotaxi Rule Lands in 2030. About 85 Million Rides Happen Before It.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 22, 2026
~85 million
Paid Waymo rides projected between today and the earliest plausible date of the first enforceable federal robotaxi safety standard, at the current pace of half a million rides a week.

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. There is no federal safety standard for the robot doing your driving. Not a weak one, not a draft one, not anything at all. On September 17, in Pittsburgh, NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison announced that the process to write the first one has formally begun, a cooperative agreement with SAE International called A2SCEND: three years, five million dollars, and a mission to figure out what "drives competently" even means in a way you can measure. Outside analysts, doing the arithmetic the agency would not do out loud, put an enforceable rule no earlier than 2030. Four years of research, then rulemaking, then public comment, then enforcement. Four years of nothing anyone can cite in court.

Meanwhile, the robots are extremely busy. Waymo is running more than 500,000 paid rides a week across 15 US cities, with expansion underway in Europe and Asia. From today to New Year's Day 2030 is 171 weeks. Multiply, and you get roughly 85.5 million paid rides from one company, at its current pace, under no federal competency standard whatsoever. That number is conservative in the way a countdown is conservative. It assumes no growth, no new cities, and it counts only Waymo, as if Tesla and Zoox were standing still somewhere, which they are not.

They are, in fact, sprinting through the regulatory gap Morrison's speech was really about. On July 30, NHTSA granted Zoox the first commercial exemption for a purpose-built robotaxi, up to 2,500 vehicles a year for two years, a vehicle with no steering wheel and no pedals, after four years of federal scrutiny that included recalling its entire 105-vehicle fleet. Tesla chose the other door. On September 3 it started charging money for Cybercab rides in Austin in a car with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no mirrors, having certified itself compliant rather than asking permission first. NHTSA opened an audit the same day, escalated to a Special Order on September 10, and Tesla now owes answers signed under oath by September 30. The federal safety framework for the driverless car, as it currently stands, is a calendar full of deadlines for homework about whether the law applies.

The part that should keep you up is not the paperwork. In July, NHTSA issued a formal call to action to AV developers after documenting what Morrison called a clear pattern: driverless vehicles driving into active emergency scenes, blocking ambulances, ignoring flares, cones, and hand signals. Real incidents. Firefighters' signals. The agency says it has seen concrete responses since, including at least one recall. But days after the Cybercab entered commercial service, an Austin fire captain told NBC News he had drilled with the car and remained unconvinced the touchscreen emergency controls were a silver bullet for every scenario. The machine that never gets tired also never reads a flare.

Now the case for the defense, stated properly, because it deserves it. This is not anarchy. The Part 555 exemption process exists and is working exactly as designed; Zoox spent four years inside it and came out with a commercial deployment. Writing the competency standard from three years of real operational data instead of inventing it in a conference room is arguably the only sane way to regulate a technology this new, and Morrison is explicit that he is not taking a hatchet to the old rules, just a scalpel. State and local laws exist in the meantime, and nobody is driving on Mars.

True, all of it; and also true is this: the scalpel work finishes, on the most generous outside estimate, around the time Waymo's fleet passes tens of thousands of vehicles and the House considers letting each automaker exempt up to 90,000 cars a year instead of 2,500, a 36-fold increase currently sitting in the Self Drive Act of 2026. We are scaling the exception while the rule is still a research project. So here is what you can actually do. There is no federal robotaxi competency standard to look up, so stop assuming one exists; the vehicles on the road were certified under standards written for humans with hands. If you ride in one, the documented weak point is not the crash structure, it is the emergency interaction, the flare, the hand signal, the ambulance behind you. Watch the two dates that matter now: September 30, when Tesla's sworn answers are due, and the slow three-year data-gathering phase that follows. A word on honesty: the 2030 date is analysts' arithmetic, not NHTSA's promise, since the agency attached no completion date at all; the 85-million figure assumes today's pace holds and ignores growth, so the real number is likely larger; and the 500,000-rides figure is Waymo's own, reported secondhand. Check the math yourself, because that is the entire point.
