Motorcycle Deaths Fell in 2024. Injuries Didn’t. A Third of the Dead Weren’t Licensed to Ride.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, 2024 was the year motorcycle deaths went down and almost nothing else did. 6,228 motorcyclists were killed, down 2.2% from 6,366 in 2023.[1] That is the headline, and it is true, and it is also the least interesting number on the page. Injuries went the other way: an estimated 86,887 riders hurt, up 5.2%, fewer funerals and more hospital beds.[1] Somebody should explain that to the families of the 138 fewer dead, because the rest of the sheet reads like a sport where the scoreboard improved and the players got worse.

Start with the denominator, because the denominator is doing all the work. Registrations fell to 9,261,249 bikes in 2024 from 9,516,910 the year before, which means the fatality rate per registered motorcycle actually rose, from 66.89 to 67.25 per 100,000.[1] Deaths fell because there were fewer bikes, not because riding got safer. Motorcycles are 3% of registered vehicles and 0.7% of all miles driven, and they produced 16% of everyone killed on American roads.[1] Per mile traveled, a rider died at 28.00 per 100 million vehicle miles, against 1.05 for a passenger-car occupant, and if you do the division yourself it comes out to 27.

Now the behavioral box score, which is where this desk lives. Thirty-five percent of riders in fatal crashes had no valid motorcycle license.[1] Compare that with 14% of passenger-vehicle drivers in fatal crashes. Riders in fatal crashes were also 1.3 times more likely than car drivers to carry a prior suspension or revocation on their record. Thirty-seven percent of riders in fatal crashes were speeding, against 22% of car drivers, and the kids were the worst of it: riders aged 15 to 20 were speeding in 56% of their fatal crashes, the highest rate of any age group.[1] While the national total fell, deaths among riders under 15 rose 114% (14 to 30), ages 15 to 20 rose 20% (508 to 609), and ages 21 to 24 rose 15% (613 to 705). Average age of a rider killed was 40, and the average age of the trend is getting younger.

Alcohol fills in the rest of the stat line. Twenty-six percent of the 5,950 riders killed were alcohol-impaired at .08 or higher, 1,529 people, and another 8% had alcohol below the legal limit.[1] Riders and car drivers tied for the highest impairment share of any vehicle type in fatal crashes, 25% each. Split it by crash shape and the pattern sharpens: 40% of riders who died alone, in single-vehicle crashes, were impaired, against 18% in multi-vehicle crashes, and on weekends single-vehicle impairment hit 43%.[1] Riders killed at night were 2.6 times more likely to be impaired than riders killed in daylight, 37% against 14%. Night plus alcohol plus no license is a parlay, and the house keeps winning it, which nobody needs a lecture to understand.

One policy number deserves its own paragraph, because it is the cleanest natural experiment in the whole federal dataset. In states without universal helmet laws, 48% of motorcyclists killed in 2024 were not wearing helmets, based on known helmet use, against 11% in states with universal helmet laws.[1] Only 17 states plus DC and Puerto Rico require helmets for all riders. Overall DOT-compliant helmet use sat at 73.8% in 2023, the last year measured.[4] Four to one is not a rounding error. It is what a law looks like when you can see it in a morgue.

Florida led the state count with 642 motorcyclist deaths in 2024, ahead of Texas at 583 and California at 522.[3] And the crash shape has not changed in a decade: 60% of motorcycles in fatal crashes hit another vehicle in transport, 24% hit a fixed object, and in two-vehicle fatal crashes 78% of bikes were struck on the front while 45% involved the other vehicle turning left across the rider's path.[1] We covered the left turn in detail in our earlier piece on 2024 rider deaths; it is still the most common way a car kills a motorcyclist, and it is still not the rider's fault.

The counterargument, at full strength: 2024 is Annual Report File data, which NHTSA revises when the Final File lands, so the 2.2% decline could shrink or grow. Injury figures are survey estimates from the Crash Report Sampling System, not counts, and a 5.2% rise could partly reflect reporting changes rather than more crashes. Registrations are FHWA estimates, so the per-registered-bike rate increase is softer than the death count. BAC values are imputed where test results were missing. And none of the licensing, speeding, or alcohol shares prove causation on their own: unlicensed riders may also ride older bikes, at night, on worse roads. All of it is fair, and none of it rescues the headline. Even on the kindest reading, riding got no safer per bike in 2024; it got slightly more dangerous, with more survivors carrying the injuries instead.

What this does not prove: this fact sheet cannot say why registrations fell, whether fewer new riders or an aging fleet, and it cannot separate the effect of helmet laws from the states that pass them, which differ in weather, riding season, and enforcement. It cannot prove a license would have saved any individual rider, because the unlicensed share is a correlation across a population, not a reconstruction of 2,000 crashes. It does not record training, gear beyond helmets, or bike condition. And it says nothing about the riders who survived, which is most of them, every single day.

What This Means for You

If you ride, the license is the whole ballgame. A third of riders in fatal crashes did not have one, and the MSF Basic RiderCourse is the standard path to getting it, with most insurers offering a discount on completion. Wear a DOT-compliant helmet on every ride, full stop: the dead were unhelmeted four times as often where the law did not require one. If you ride at night, understand that the impairment rate among the dead more than doubles after dark, which means the cars around you are drunker, too, so give them room and assume the left-turning sedan has not seen you. Parents of teenage riders should know the youngest cohorts are the only ones getting worse: 56% of 15-to-20-year-old riders in fatal crashes were speeding. And if you drive a car, the left turn is still the kill shot: 45% of fatal two-vehicle motorcycle crashes started with you turning across a rider going straight. Look twice, specifically for the bike, before you turn.
