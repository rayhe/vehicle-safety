GM Fixed the Trailblazer's Rear Seat Belts. IIHS Tested It Again. Still Poor.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
February 14, 2028

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds: the front of the car crushes, the driver rides down the crash on a belt that pays out webbing at a tuned force, and behind the driver sits a second dummy the size of a small woman. That dummy is the whole reason the 2026 Chevrolet Trailblazer now carries a Poor rating in IIHS's updated moderate overlap front test, even though GM already tried to fix it once.[1]

Feb 2026
Build month after which GM's “improved” rear belts still rate Poor in the updated test

IIHS's own test notes tell the story in one dry sentence: beginning with 2026 models built after February 2026, GM made improvements to the rear seat belt load limiters specifically to improve rear occupant safety in moderate overlap front crashes. Even so, the retested vehicle earned Poor overall. Rear passenger chest injury measures came back Moderate. Rear passenger restraints and dummy kinematics: Poor.[1]

A load limiter is a torsion bar inside the belt retractor. When crash forces spike, it twists and lets a controlled length of webbing feed out, capping the load on the occupant's chest. It is elegant, cheap, and tuned in a lab. What it cannot do is fix geometry. If the lap belt rides up off the pelvis, or the dummy submarines under the belt during the ride-down, no amount of force capping saves the kinematics score. GM retuned the force curve, and the kinematics score failed anyway. That is an engineering miss, not a paperwork one, and it is the kind of miss that makes me reach for the technical measurements before the press release.[1]

Here is why this stings: the same test, the same failure mode, and one manufacturer solved it with a single running change. Consider the redesigned Nissan Kicks: it scored Poor on this exact test. Nissan revised the rear seat belts on Kicks models built after June 2026, IIHS retested, and the car earned Good plus a Top Safety Pick award.[3][4] Audi tried the same playbook on the redesigned Q3, modifying rear belts after May 2026, and still scored Poor, with the rear lap belt slipping off the pelvis.[4] The fix is demonstrably possible. It is also demonstrably not trivial. GM is now in Audi's column, not Nissan's.

Credit the attempt. On the credit side, the Trailblazer's structure and safety cage rated Good. Every driver injury measure rated Good. Small overlap front: Good across the board. Updated side test: Good overall. Drivers are well protected in this car. That Poor is rear-occupant-specific, which is exactly the population the updated test was created to protect when IIHS added the rear dummy in 2022 and tightened the scoring in 2024.[2][5] Five years ago this nameplate earned Top Safety Pick+, back when the award did not require surviving a test that now exists. Standards moved. This car didn't move enough.

What this means for you

If you are shopping a 2026 Trailblazer, open the driver door and read the certification label on the B-pillar. A build date before February 2026 means the old rear belts, on a car whose improved replacement still rates Poor. A build date after February 2026 means the improved belts and a certified Poor. Neither date buys you out of this rating on the current model year; the 2027 refresh is GM's next chance to join Nissan's column. If kids ride in your second row, the Kicks built after June 2026 now carries a Good in this test and a Top Safety Pick, and the 2027 VW Jetta earned Good in the same round of testing.[4][6] Check the specific build month at iihs.org/ratings before you sign anything, and run your VIN through nhtsa.gov/recalls while you are at it.[1][4]
