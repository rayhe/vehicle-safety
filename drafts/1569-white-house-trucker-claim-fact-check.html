The White House Took Credit for the Safest Half-Year on Record. The Truck Numbers Say Otherwise.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 23, 2026

On November 16, the White House issued a release titled “President Trump’s Immigration Policies Are Saving American Lives.” Its opening line reads: “Traffic fatalities have fallen to the second-lowest level in American history following President Donald J. Trump’s crackdown on illegal alien truck drivers.”[2] One sentence, three claims, and all three wilt under the federal government’s own numbers, which the release itself links to.

13.6%
Large trucks’ share of all American road deaths, in 2024 and in 2025 alike, identical to two decimal places.

Start with the share, because it is the cleanest test of the claim. In 2024, 5,340 people died in crashes involving at least one large truck, out of 39,254 total road deaths.[4] Divide the first number by the second and you get 13.60 percent. In 2025, NHTSA’s early estimate puts large-truck-crash deaths at 4,982, out of 36,640 total deaths.[3] Divide again and the answer is the same: 13.60 percent. Truck deaths fell 7 percent in 2025 while total deaths fell 6.7 percent. Trucks fell at the same rate as everything else, which is exactly what you would expect to see if the crackdown had no truck-specific effect at all.

Now the “second-lowest level in American history” part. NHTSA’s November 10 early estimate for the first half of 2026 puts deaths at 16,750, down 2.5 percent from 17,175 in the first half of 2025, with a fatality rate of 1.02 deaths per 100 million miles driven.[1] NHTSA’s exact words: “If realized, the rate of 1.02 represents the second lowest first half fatality rate, compared to the 1.01 achieved in the first half of 2014.” That is the rate, not the death count. On raw deaths, the first half of 2026 ranks third in NHTSA’s 13-year table, behind the first half of 2014 (15,035 deaths) and the first half of 2015 (16,193). Second-lowest rate is real and genuinely good news. Second-lowest death count is not what the table says, and “in American history” is doing a lot of work for a comparison that runs back to 2014.

Then the timing, which is where the claim stops being sloppy and starts being impossible. NHTSA’s own chart counts 17 straight quarters of year-over-year declines, running from the second quarter of 2022 through the second quarter of 2026.[1] Executive order on English proficiency for truck drivers: April 28, 2025. That is 11 quarters, roughly two years and ten months, of decline before the order existed. Large-truck deaths were already falling too, down 8 percent in 2023 and 2.5 percent in 2024, both before any crackdown.[4] A cause cannot start three years after its effect.

Even the half-year the White House is celebrating undercuts the story. Deaths fell 5.0 percent in the first quarter of 2026 and 0.2 percent in the second.[1] Deaths actually rose in May and June 2026 compared with the same months in 2025. Enforcement numbers the release cites accumulated mostly later in the year: the out-of-service count was 26,000 in August and reached 34,000 by November.[5] So the “sharp drop” happened in the quarter before the enforcement it supposedly followed had mostly happened. Chronology is not kind to this press release.

Credit where it is due: the enforcement numbers themselves check out. Transportation Department figures from November 12 confirm 34,000 drivers placed out of service for failing English standards and more than 31,000 commercial licenses revoked as illegally issued.[5] Nobody at this desk disputes that 34,000 unqualified drivers were taken off the road. What is disputed is the causal arrow drawn from that fact to the death count, because NHTSA’s report attributes no part of the decline to the driver enforcement, to immigration policy, or to commercial vehicle inspections. NHTSA’s April 2026 release did list enforcement among several factors behind the 2025 decrease, and that deserves an honest hearing, which it gets below. But the November report, the one the White House linked, says nothing of the kind.

Fairness requires stating the administration’s best case at full strength. Removing 34,000 drivers who cannot read road signs should, on first principles, make roads safer, and the full effect of removals that accumulated through late 2025 may not be visible in full-year 2025 numbers at all. A flat 13.6 percent share is an aggregate; it cannot rule out that truck crashes fell in specific corridors or that worse outcomes were prevented. And the rate claim is real: 1.02 genuinely is the second-lowest first-half rate NHTSA has recorded. Against that, a claim that X caused Y needs Y to move differently where X acted, yet truck deaths moved exactly like everything else. The decline predates the policy by three years. The celebrated quarter came before the enforcement numbers it cites had mostly accumulated. Credit requires evidence of a difference, and there is none in the federal record.

What this does not prove matters as much as what it does. NHTSA’s first-half 2026 report does not break out large-truck deaths, so the share comparison uses full-year 2024 against full-year 2025, not the first half of 2026. A truck-specific effect in early 2026 would not show up in this arithmetic yet. All 2025 and 2026 figures are preliminary projections, subject to revision when the FARS Annual Reporting File lands. A separate federal count from FMCSA puts 2025 truck-crash fatalities at 4,330, down 13.7 percent, which differs from NHTSA’s 4,982 because the two agencies count different things; mixing the series would be a category error, so this article does not. And a flat aggregate share does not prove zero effect, only that no truck-specific effect is visible in the numbers the government has published. The claim being checked is the White House’s; the evidence does not support it, which is not the same as proving the opposite.

What to do with all this: first, read the federal number before the press release about it. NHTSA’s Crash Stats reports are public, and the first page states exactly what “second lowest” refers to. Second, when a politician claims credit for a trend, check the start date. A decline that began in the second quarter of 2022 cannot have been caused by an April 2025 order. Third, rate and count are different numbers, and the gap here is about 1,700 lives. Fourth, mark late December, when NHTSA’s next release covers the first nine months of 2026 and will include the first sub-category data covering the crackdown period in full. That is the number that can actually test the claim. Until then, the honest sentence is the boring one: deaths fell, the rate is near a record low, trucks fell with everything else, and nobody’s press release gets to rewrite the table.
