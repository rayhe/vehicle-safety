The 2019 Forte Won Every Safety Award. Its Body Count Doubled Anyway.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 28, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens when a car gets safer on paper and deadlier on the road. The 2019 Kia Forte earned the IIHS Top Safety Pick+ award, the institute’s highest honor[3]. Its 2019 through 2021 model years then piled up 182 deaths in federal fatal-crash data, exactly double the 91 deaths from the 2010 through 2012 cars[1]: more awards, more bodies. Somebody’s math is wrong, and it isn’t the coroner’s.

2.0×
Deaths in 2019–2021 Fortes vs 2010–2012 Fortes, before adjusting for road exposure

Raw counts understate it, because new cars haven’t been on the road as long. The 2010–2012 Fortes averaged about twelve years of exposure inside the 2014–2023 FARS window; the 2019–2021 cars barely three. The newer cohort outsold the older one by only 1.35 to 1[2]. Run the division — deaths per vehicle per year on the road — and the 2019–2021 Forte dies at roughly five times the rate of the 2010–2012 car, a vehicle that added forward collision avoidance, lane keeping, and a stiffer structure in the 2019 redesign.

Maybe the whole segment got worse and the Forte rode the wave; it didn’t. Run the same model-year split on its three cross-shopped rivals: Elantra 0.49, Civic 0.31, Corolla 0.47[1]. Every rival declined the way exposure math predicts, and only the Forte doubled. It also survived the variable everyone reaches for first: impairment. Forte drivers test positive at 20.1 percent, effectively identical to Civic (20.4), Corolla (19.2), and Elantra (18.6)[1]. Not a drunk-driver story — a car story. The obvious objection is the pandemic: the 2019–2021 cars did most of their dying when empty highways invited higher speeds. But the rivals lived through the same pandemic and declined anyway, so COVID distortion, while real, cannot explain a divergence this specific.

So what changed? The headlights — I already did the teardown on this car. Most Fortes sold were LX and LXS trims, and those volume cars shipped with halogen projectors IIHS rated Poor from 2019 through 2024, throwing 22.5 meters of usable light at the left edge[5]: 1.12 seconds of visibility at 45 mph against a 2.5-second reaction standard. Now read the award citations: the 2019 TSP+ and the 2020 TSP were granted “when equipped with front crash prevention and specific headlights”[3][4]. But the car IIHS tested is not the car most people bought. The trophy lives in the GT trim; the volume cars never earned it.

What I can’t prove deserves its own paragraph. FARS records fatal crashes, not injury-only ones, so a car can look safe here while maiming elsewhere. Sales stand in for fleet size because model-year mileage data doesn’t exist, and scrappage differences add noise. Nor can I fully separate machine from mission: as rivals abandoned the sub-$20,000 segment, the Forte absorbed younger, higher-mileage, more nighttime urban drivers. But demographics don’t explain why the Elantra — same price point, same buyers — moved the opposite direction. One more caveat: FARS doesn’t record trim level, so the concentration of deaths in LX and LXS cars is inferred from sales mix and headlight ratings, not confirmed body by body.

Bottom line for your driveway: a used 2019–2021 Forte is not the bargain its sticker suggests next to a same-year Elantra or Civic. If you buy one anyway, buy the GT — its LED headlights are the TSP-qualifying configuration, the only lamps on this car that can see around a corner. Check any VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands. And the next time a commercial brags about a safety award, ask which trim earned it, because usually, it wasn’t yours.
