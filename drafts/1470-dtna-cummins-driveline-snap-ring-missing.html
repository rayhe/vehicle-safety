The Driveshaft Can Fall Out. Cummins Knew First.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 11, 2026

Somewhere on an American highway right now, a 2027 Freightliner Cascadia is hauling freight with a driveshaft held together by optimism. Daimler recalled 3,894 brand-new trucks last week because the driveline may be missing a snap ring in the slip yoke assembly, and a missing snap ring means the driveshaft can separate from the vehicle while it is moving.[1]

A snap ring is a circle of spring steel about the size of a bracelet. It sits in a groove and keeps the bearing cup from walking out of the slip yoke, the joint that lets a driveshaft change length as the suspension moves. Leave it out and nothing fails immediately. Vibration does the rest, the cup creeps, and one day the shaft lets go at speed. NHTSA’s consequence sentence is unusually blunt about what happens next: detached components can become a road hazard for other vehicles.[1] That is the agency saying the part that falls off your truck is somebody else’s problem too.

9 days
Between Cummins filing its own driveline recall and Daimler filing the truck recall for the same defect

Cummins filed first. On October 28, the engine maker recalled 206 Meritor drivelines under its own equipment campaign, AFT26001, naming the identical defect: a snap ring missing from the slip yoke, a bearing cup that separates.[2] Nine days later Daimler filed the vehicle recall covering 3,894 trucks. Same failure, same consequence language, word for word, and one population is nineteen times the other. Cummins mails its owners on November 20, twenty-three days after filing. Daimler mails its owners on January 5, sixty days after filing, the full length of the owner-notification window.[1][2]

This is not the first time a DTNA truck has gone out the door without a snap ring it was supposed to have. In May 2024, Transport Canada recalled 2025 Freightliner Business Class M2 and 114SD trucks because the snap rings for the wheel hub fasteners could be missing, and a wheel could detach.[3] Same manufacturer. Same two nameplates, both of which show up in this week’s recall too. Different assembly, same failure class, same warning that the part coming off endangers everyone behind it. Eight hundred eighty-nine days separate the two filings.

The counterargument, at full strength: no crashes, no injuries, no deaths are reported in either filing, and the defect rate is unstated, which means it could be a handful of trucks out of 3,894. A missing snap ring is a manufacturing escape, not a design flaw, and the recall system did what it is supposed to do: the supplier flagged the part, the truckmaker flagged the trucks, and the fix is free. All true. Also true: the supplier flagged the part nine days before the truckmaker flagged the trucks, the truck owners wait sixty days to hear about it, and the same two truck lines already lost a snap ring once before, on a different part. A driveshaft bouncing down the interstate at seventy miles an hour does not care which column of the spreadsheet it came from.

What this does not prove: NHTSA’s record gives one population number and no per-model split, so nobody outside DTNA knows how many of the 3,894 actually lack the ring. No field failures are documented in the filing, so the separation scenario is a predicted failure, not an observed one. And the nine-day supplier-first sequence is read off two report dates, not a disclosed internal timeline; Cummins and DTNA may have been talking for weeks before either filed.

What This Means for You

If you run a 2027 Cascadia, Fifth Gen Cascadia, 114SD, Business Class M2, 108SD, or Western Star 49X or 47X with a Cummins driveline, check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and cite 26V737 or DTNA F1046. Inspection and replacement are free, and there is no do-not-drive order, so the trucks keep working while they wait. If you bought a Meritor RPL-series driveline as a part, check Cummins campaign AFT26001; those letters went out November 20. And if you are the driver behind the truck instead of in it: give it room. Federal paperwork now says, twice in two weeks, that the thing falling off the truck ahead of you is a foreseeable event.
