Toyota's Drunkest Drivers Survive. Its Soberest Drivers Don't.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 29, 2026

Two Toyotas, opposite drivers, opposite outcomes, and a lesson about what actually keeps people alive in a crash. In fatal crashes involving the Toyota Solara, only 4.1% of drivers tested positive for alcohol or drugs, the lowest figure in the federal database. In fatal crashes involving the Toyota FJ Cruiser, 25.3% did, more than six times higher. Now the part that should not be possible: the Solara kills 4.25 people per 100 million miles traveled, nearly ten times the FJ Cruiser's 0.43.[1] The drunkest Toyota survives; its soberest sibling somehow does not.

9.9x
Solara fatality rate (4.25) vs. FJ Cruiser (0.43), per 100M miles

Explanations for this kind of inversion are measured in pounds, not blood-alcohol content. Weighing in at 4,300 pounds, the body-on-frame FJ Cruiser earned Good ratings from IIHS in frontal-offset and side-impact testing.[2][3] The Solara is a Camry-based coupe, roughly 3,400 pounds in V6 trim and lighter than the FJ by nearly a thousand, riding on an older fleet with fewer modern structural protections.[5] When the crash happens, the laws of physics do not check your sobriety, because mass wins and structure wins, and four thousand three hundred pounds of steel wrapped around the cabin absorbs what the Solara's sheet metal cannot. That advantage does not care who was drinking. That is a statement about physics, not a permission slip: 67 of the tested FJ drivers in fatal crashes were impaired, and the model still killed 95 people.[4]

Across every brand in the database, taking max minus min for models with at least 100 tested drivers, Toyota's own lineup spans the widest range of any manufacturer: 21.2 percentage points from the Solara's 4.1% to the FJ Cruiser's 25.3%. Cadillac, the next-closest brand, manages 15.4. Somewhere in Toyota's product planning, the badge that means reliability to a Camry buyer means something very different to the person buying a retro off-roader or a cheap used coupe. A brand is not the driver.

21.2pp
Toyota's intra-brand impairment spread, widest of any manufacturer

The honest objection is sample size and exposure, because 265 tested FJ drivers and 195 Solara drivers over a decade is not a census. FJ Cruisers are weekend toys, off-roaders, and low-mileage second vehicles, and their mileage estimates carry wide uncertainty. Solara deaths skew toward an aging fleet of cheap used coupes bought by younger drivers, a demographic story this publication has told before. Impairment testing varies by state, so some of the 21-point gap is geography rather than behavior. Normalize for miles, age, and crash type, and the inversion would narrow, though it would have to narrow a great deal to erase a 9.9x fatality gap, and no exposure adjustment turns 4,300 pounds of Good-rated steel into a disadvantage.

Both vehicles are discontinued, the Solara after 2008 and the FJ after the 2014 model year in North America, so this is a used-lot story now. Anyone shopping either one should take the general lesson: buy the model, not the badge, and check the IIHS rating for the specific model year. Side airbags were optional on the FJ's earliest 2007 models, became standard in 2008, and most of its deaths cluster in those first two years. Numbers do not lie; they just refuse to flatter anyone, drunk or sober.
