Work Trucks Just Took Their First IIHS Auto-Brake Test. All Four Failed.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2027

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. On October 8, 2026, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety published its first-ever front crash prevention ratings for commercial vehicles, eleven cargo vans and Class 3 pickups driven at test dummies on a track in Virginia, and not one of the four heavy-duty pickups earned a Good rating in either test.[1]

Eight of eleven scored Poor in at least one category. Built on platforms that last saw a meaningful redesign during the Clinton administration, the Express and Savana scored Poor in both, mostly because neither vehicle offers automatic emergency braking at all. At least the Transit's pre-collision system warned the driver before impact: by 0.6 to 1.6 seconds. Against a pedestrian, that is roughly the time it takes to say "oh no" and then stop saying anything. Motorcycle detection was the weakest point across the board; the systems mostly failed to produce a timely warning regardless of speed or position.[2]

Now the number that actually matters. In 2024, 6,215 people died in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans, which is 16 percent of all traffic deaths in the United States.[1] One in six road deaths involves these vehicles, and until October, nobody had ever graded whether their crash-avoidance systems work. Federal AEB requirements that apply to passenger cars do not apply to these trucks, and some airbag requirements do not either. IIHS president David Harkey called the results "a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers," which is institute-speak for: you have had decades, and the van with the best score is the one built by a German luxury brand.[1]

The hopeful reading of this table is that every category has at least one Good performer, which proves the engineering is not the problem. Engineering is not the problem, as it turns out: the Sprinter stops for cars, and the BrightDrop and the Amazon van stop for pedestrians, which means the problem is deployment and regulation. Roughly 8,000 pounds is what an F-350 weighs, and its auto-brake is rated Poor at avoiding the vehicles in front of it, which is the sort of statistic you can meditate on while waiting at a red light behind one.

What to actually do with this, starting with the easy part: if you run a fleet, the scorecard is now public and free, and the answer is uncomplicated: Sprinter 2500 for vehicle-to-vehicle, BrightDrop 400 or Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 for pedestrian protection. If your plumber, electrician, or parcel driver shows up in an Express or Savana, their van has no AEB whatsoever, so give them room the way you give room to someone texting. And if you are buying a used commercial van, remember that these ratings did not exist before October 2026, so any older "five-star safety" claim in a listing is describing a different test entirely.

Limitations, because I insist on them: these are controlled track tests under IIHS's own protocol, not real-world crash outcomes, and a Poor rating measures behavior on the course rather than confirmed on-road danger. Keep the sample in perspective: eleven vehicles chosen by notoriety and insurance registrations, not the full commercial fleet, and most heavy-vehicle fatalities involve tractor-trailers, which IIHS has not scored yet (Class 4-6 trucks are next, after headlight evaluations). To steelman the other side: Class 3 pickups are a small share of registrations, and per-mile exposure for work trucks differs wildly from passenger cars, so raw score comparisons across segments need care. Still. Zero Goods out of eight possible scores, from the vehicles that share your commute every morning, and I checked twice.
