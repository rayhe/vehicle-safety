Jaguar Had Zero Recalls in 2026. Land Rover Had All Seven.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 13, 2026

One company. Two badges. Seven recalls. Jaguar filed none of them. Land Rover filed all seven, covering 345,704 campaign-units across its 2026-model-year vehicles, and the split is the whole story: a manufacturer that presents itself as a single house, Jaguar Land Rover, behaved in 2026 like two different companies that happen to share a letterhead. Jaguar’s 2026 lineup is exactly one model, the F-PACE, and it went the year without a single NHTSA filing. Land Rover filed seven.[1]

345,704
Campaign-units across JLR’s 7 recalls in 2026. Two campaigns carry 99.75% of them. The other five combined cover 850 vehicles.

Method first, because a census is only as honest as its counting rules, and this one has a trap in it. NHTSA’s recalls API returns a 400 error when no recall records exist for a model year, which looks exactly like a broken query if you are not expecting it. I queried every model the federal vehicle database lists for Jaguar Land Rover in 2026, eight nameplates plus Hybrid and plug-in suffix variants, 48 queries in all, kept only campaigns numbered 26V filed in calendar 2026, and deduplicated by campaign number. Seven survived. Raw results are in the research file. Jaguar’s only 2026 recall anywhere in the database is 25V705, filed in October 2025 for moisture in the gear shift module, which misses the census window by two and a half months. Close, but a census counts filings, not feelings.[1][2]

Two campaigns carry nearly everything. In June, Land Rover filed 26V389: the driver’s airbag clockspring connector may corrode, which can keep the airbag from deploying, across 2020 to 2026 Defenders, 2021 to 2026 Discoveries, and 2022 to 2026 Range Rovers. Population: 250,857 vehicles, 97,552 Defenders, 83,620 Discoveries, 69,685 Range Rovers. Cause: fretting corrosion from vibration during normal driving, which means the act of driving the car degrades the part that is supposed to save the driver. NHTSA estimates 100 percent of the population carries the defect, and Land Rover has received zero US claims of an airbag actually failing to deploy, zero injuries, zero fires. Remedy: dealers apply a protective lubricant gel to the connector terminals, free of charge. Gel. A syringe of gel is what stands between a quarter-million luxury SUVs and a silent airbag, and Land Rover’s own engineering analysis says the warning lamp illuminates 300 to 400 miles before the connector reaches the point where it could fail, which is the only reason this reads as a controlled problem instead of a catastrophe.[7]

October brought the second giant. Recall 26V678 covers 93,997 Range Rovers from model years 2022 to 2026, because the tailgate trim does not have enough drain holes, so rainwater collects around the rearview camera and kills it. Land Rover received 2,295 US claims and field reports before filing, again with zero injuries, accidents, or fires. Remedy: dealers drill additional drain holes in the underside of the tailgate trim, then inspect and replace the camera if needed. A $110,000 SUV that cannot survive weather gets fixed with a drill, and the interim owner letters do not go out until December 11, which means the fix was announced before it was ready. This is the second JLR rearview-camera recall in two years, and its campaign number is nearly identical to the 2024 one, 24V678.[8]

Then the five small ones, which are the more interesting half of the census. A fuel tank recall covering six Defenders, because the tank walls were manufactured too thin. A certification-label recall covering eight Range Rover Sports, because the sticker lists the wrong vehicle weight. A seat belt chime recall covering 76 vehicles across three models, because water-damaged audio amplifiers cannot sound the unbuckled warning. A sunroof trim recall covering 178 Range Rovers and Range Rover Sports, because the glue used to reattach the finishers during factory rework failed. A seat belt anchorage recall covering 582 Defenders, because a supplier shipped weld studs contaminated at the material level, and Land Rover added more vehicles in April after discovering at-risk parts its initial recall and quarantine had missed. Five campaigns, 850 vehicles combined, and every one of them is a manufacturing escape caught in a batch of dozens. That is a quality-control story, not a design story, and it is the opposite of the two big campaigns, which are design-level defects affecting a quarter-million older SUVs.[3][4][5][6][9]

Per-model counts sharpen the picture. Range Rover carries four of the seven campaigns, Range Rover Sport three, Defender three, Discovery one, Velar one. Evoque and Discovery Sport carry zero, and so does the F-PACE. So the two nameplates JLR sells on invincibility, the Defender and the Range Rover, are the two most-recalled nameplates in its own census, while the volume sellers nobody writes poetry about went clean. None of the seven campaigns carries a park-outside advisory, a do-not-drive order, or an over-the-air fix. Four of the seven carry a 100 percent defect estimate. Zero injuries were reported in the three filings where the count was verified.[1]

Where does seven put JLR in the series? Exactly mid-pack, tied with BMW and Nissan at seven, behind Ford at 41, Stellantis at 27, GM, Toyota, and Hyundai at 15 each, and Volvo at 12, and ahead of Subaru and Lucid at five, Honda, Kia, Volkswagen, and Tesla at four, and Mazda and Porsche at zero. But the ranking counts campaigns, not units, and on units JLR would sit far higher, because 26V389 alone is larger than the entire recall population of most brands in the series. Both numbers are true, and they describe different years: a quiet year for new models, and a very loud year for old ones. JLR is the 21st brand counted in the series, and the last major manufacturer without a census.[10]

What to do, concretely, starts with the airbag. If you own a 2020 to 2026 Defender, a 2021 to 2026 Discovery, or a 2022 to 2026 Range Rover, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for 26V389 first. It is the largest recall in this census by a factor of two and a half, and the remedy is a free gel application at the dealer, but the warning sign matters more than the appointment: if your airbag warning lamp is illuminated, get the car in now, because Land Rover’s own analysis puts 300 to 400 miles between that lamp and a connector that may not fire the bag. Then check for 26V678 if you own a 2022 to 2026 Range Rover, because a dead rearview camera is a federal visibility failure and the interim letters arrive in December. If you own a 2026 Defender, check for 26V163 and 26V263 as well, the seat belt weld and the fuel tank, because both populations are tiny and a dealer may not think to look. If you are shopping used, the 2026 F-PACE is the only clean sheet in the company, and even that one carries a 2025 gear-shift-module recall, so run the VIN regardless. Every fix in this census is free.

Limitations, stated plainly, before anyone mistakes this census for a ranking. This is a filing census, not a danger ranking. A campaign counts as one whether it covers six fuel tanks or 250,857 airbags, whether the defect estimate is under 1 percent or 100 percent. Campaign-unit totals count a vehicle once per campaign, so a 2022 to 2026 Range Rover can appear in both 26V389 and 26V678, and the 345,704 total is not a count of distinct vehicles. Counts are a snapshot as of November 13, 2026, and campaigns filed later in the year are not included. Scope is 2026-model-year vehicles with 2026 filings, which excludes by design the company’s biggest 2026 recall by units, the 170,169-vehicle mild-hybrid DC-DC converter recall filed in April, because it covers 2019 to 2024 models only. Unit counts come from Part 573 reports and press quoting NHTSA filings, and 26V163’s 582 is the global total, with 498 in the US. Injury counts are as stated in the verified filings, zero for 26V097, 26V389, and 26V678; the other four campaigns’ summaries state no injury counts.[1]

Counterargument, at full strength, because the honest reading deserves a hearing. JLR had a competent, even boring, recall year. Seven campaigns is mid-pack, zero injuries were reported in any verified filing, and the two big campaigns are precautionary recalls for defects that have not hurt anyone, with remedies already defined and letters already mailed. The genuinely-2026 exposure is five tiny campaigns totaling 850 vehicles, which is a rounding error against a company that sells hundreds of thousands of cars a year. Jaguar’s zero may say less about quality than about arithmetic: one model, one nameplate, a company winding down its old lineup before an all-electric relaunch, and a census window that happened to miss its one filing by ten weeks. And the census’s own scope hides the real story, a 170,169-unit power-loss recall with a remedy still under development at filing, now facing a proposed class action alleging the company knew about the converter defect for years. By any danger ranking, JLR’s 2026 was quieter than Ford’s or Stellantis’s, and the small-batch recalls are the system catching escapes early, which is what it is for. All of that is fair, and this article grants it. Counterweight is the shape of the count. A quarter-million airbags that may not deploy because driving the car corrodes the connector is not a quiet year, even with zero injuries, and a camera that dies in the rain on a $110,000 SUV is not a rounding error. Seven campaigns, zero injuries, and two design defects that should have been caught before the first customer took delivery.
