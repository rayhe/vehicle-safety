GM Fixed Your Lyriq's Brakes in 2024. The Car That Proved It Didn't Work Hit a Garage in April.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

On April 10, 2026, a GM technical expert filed an internal safety report about a 2024 Cadillac Lyriq that had struck a garage at low speed. Nothing about that sentence should alarm you. Low speed, minor impact, one car, one garage. What makes it the most important sentence in GM's newest recall filing is the part GM added next: the Lyriq was built with the software improvements implemented as the remedy for the previous brake recall.[1] The car that proved the fix failed was driving on the fix. Six months later, on October 22, GM's safety authority decided to recall 67,380 electric vehicles all over again.

Of the 24 field complaints tied to this defect, 20 involved a minor accident

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds, because that is where this defect lives. The brake system control module, the BSCM, constantly calculates wheel slip to decide how much braking force each wheel gets. Under one specific sequence, the calculation gets it wrong. You are driving slowly, turning sharply, and braking lightly. Your wheels hit a bump or a dip, the kind at the end of a driveway. You lift off the pedal for an instant and press it again. In that instant the software misreads wheel movement, and braking drops out.[1] Press the pedal again and braking returns, which is why GM calls the loss temporary. Temporary is doing a lot of work in that sentence when the car in front of you is a garage door.

Only performance all-wheel-drive models are affected, the ones carrying GM's RPO X0E code. That narrows the recall to certain 2023 through 2027 Lyriqs, 2024 through 2027 Blazer EVs, 2025 through 2026 Celestiqs, and 2026 through 2027 Vistiqs.[1] Run the population math and the Lyriq dominates the list: 44,585 of the 67,380 vehicles, about two-thirds. The 2024 Lyriq alone accounts for 22,819, a full third of the entire recall, and it is the same model year as the car that hit the garage. Two of the 20 accidents carried allegations of minor injuries. Nobody died. That is luck, not engineering.

Now the part that should bother you most. The fix is a free software update, delivered over the air or at a dealer, and GM has already corrected the software in production for the newest model years.[1] But the update is not ready for the 2023 and 2024 Lyriq models.[3] Read that again. The oldest cars in the recall, the ones that have been through this once already, the ones carrying the largest single cohort, are the ones waiting longest for the remedy. Owner notification letters go out December 14. For a 2024 Lyriq owner, the letter will arrive before the fix does.

Two things this story is not. First, it is not the eBoost investigation. NHTSA opened Engineering Analysis EA26006 in August 2026 into a different GM brake mechanism, a spindle fracture that can kill brake assist, covering 1,164,820 vehicles with 745 reported incidents.[6] Different part, different failure, different paperwork. Do not merge the two numbers. Second, it is not proof that GM sat on this. GM duplicated the failure in its own testing, opened a new investigation on August 18, and reached a recall decision in about six months. A fair reading says the recall system worked: a narrow software edge case, caught, filed, and fixed at no cost to owners.

Fair does not mean finished. The complaint window in GM's own filing opens February 28, 2024, which means some of these 24 complaints predate the 2024 recall and were effectively counted twice: once as evidence the first fix was needed, and once as evidence it failed. Twenty of 24 complaints involved a crash, an 83 percent accident rate per complaint that no ordinary recall produces. And the trigger sequence is not an exotic edge case but the exact choreography of leaving a driveway or turning into a parking garage. Low speed, sharp turn, bump, release, reapply. You have done it a thousand times. Your car is supposed to have done it a thousand times too.

So what do you do with this. If you own a 2023 through 2027 Lyriq, a 2024 through 2027 Blazer EV SS or police pursuit model, a 2025 through 2026 Celestiq, or a 2026 through 2027 Vistiq with performance all-wheel drive, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. VINs have been searchable since October 29.[2] If you drive a 2023 or 2024 Lyriq, there is no update to accept yet, so drive the driveway maneuver like it matters: slow down before the turn, leave room ahead, and remember that a second firm press of the pedal restores braking if the first one goes soft. Everyone else in the recall should accept the over-the-air update the moment it is offered. A brake pedal that needs a second press is a brake pedal that has already failed once.
