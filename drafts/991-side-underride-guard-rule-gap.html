The Crash Your Airbags Can't Survive Has No Federal Guard

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 26, 2026

There are three ways to drive underneath a semi-trailer. Federal law guards against exactly one of them. Out back, a steel bar keeps your car from sliding under the bed, and that guard has been federally required for decades. Along the sides, sixty feet of open gap per trailer, there is no guard at all. A trailer floor rides higher than your bumper, your crumple zone, and every airbag sensor your car owns. In a side underride, the entire safety cage you paid for watches the crash happen above its head.[1][2]

31,500
Underride deaths since NHTSA was founded, per the sponsors of the Stop Underrides Act 2.0. No side-guard rule exists.

NHTSA opened a formal review of a side-guard mandate in 2023. Three years later, the agency is still reading public comments. The trucking industry's entry in those comments prices the fix at $3,740 or more per trailer, plus 450 to 800 pounds of added weight, and argues the retrofit burden makes the rule unworkable. Safety advocates counter with crash tests showing guards stop a car from sliding under at 40 mph, and note that aerodynamic side guards can save fuel. What NHTSA is actually weighing is a cost-benefit spreadsheet, and so far the spreadsheet has been winning.[3][4]

Congress tried to settle it by legislation. One bill would settle the debate: the Stop Underrides Act 2.0, H.R. 7354 in the House and S. 3775 in the Senate, would require side guards on new trucks and trailers rated to keep a passenger compartment intact in impacts up to 40 mph. Its sponsors, Reps. Steven Cohen, Mark DeSaulnier and Deborah Ross, and Sens. Kirsten Gillibrand and Ben Ray Lujan, put a number on the failure: 31,500 lives claimed by underride crashes since NHTSA was founded, with, in their words, "no substantial progress" toward stopping them.[2] The bill also demands better data collection, which matters more than it sounds, because right now nobody can agree on the body count.

Here is the part that should embarrass everyone involved. Officially the tally runs about 219 underride deaths a year, but states report these crashes inconsistently, and researchers who have dug through the records put the real number between 300 and 600 a year, possibly more. Run the bill's own number and it lands at the top of that range: 31,500 deaths over roughly 55 years is about 570 a year, which suggests the official count misses more than half the bodies. You cannot run a cost-benefit analysis on deaths you refuse to count, and you cannot finalize a rule whose benefits you cannot measure. The undercount is not a footnote. It is the mechanism.[4][5]

Last month, advocates invited NHTSA Administrator Morrison and the FMCSA administrator to Raleigh to watch an underride crash test in person, at the Southeast Commercial Motor Vehicle Safety Summit. Nothing clarifies a regulatory docket like a sedan disappearing under a trailer in front of the people in charge of preventing it.[5]

What to do depends on which side of the trailer you sit on. Fleet operators can spec side guards right now, including designs that have qualified for IIHS recognition, without waiting for a rulemaking that has been stuck since 2023. Drivers get a geometry lesson: never loiter alongside a trailer's midsection, in the blind gap where a lane change or a turn puts your windshield under the floor. No airbag deploys for a trailer floor, because the sensors are three feet below the impact. Voters get the simplest ask of all: the bill is live. Co-sponsor H.R. 7354 or S. 3775, or pressure NHTSA to finish the review it opened three years ago.

A fair case for the delay, stated straight. Retrofitting millions of trailers costs real money, the weight penalty burns real fuel, and guards rated for 40 mph impacts add maintenance burden to equipment that already lives a hard life. Those figures deserve scrutiny in return: $3,740 per trailer sounds large until you price it against one wrongful-death settlement, and fuel savings from aerodynamic guards cut against the weight argument. Every death count in this article is attributed, because the numbers are contested and the honest range is wide. But wide uncertainty is not the same as zero. Three hundred deaths a year is the low estimate, and each one died in a crash the government has known how to prevent for decades.
