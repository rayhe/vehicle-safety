One Bad Hose, a Dozen-Plus Truck Brands: PACCAR Joins the Cummins L9 Fuel-Line Cascade

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 30, 2026

PACCAR filed the paperwork on October 21. 26,729 Kenworth and Peterbilt trucks, model years 2022 through 2027, are being recalled because the remote fuel filter hose on their Cummins L9 diesels can separate from its fitting, spraying diesel where no diesel belongs and stalling the engine where no stall should happen.[1] NHTSA calls it 26V700; Paccar calls it 26PACJ. You can call it the fifth verse of the same song.

65,781
Known Cummins L9 engines and trucks caught in the fuel-hose recall cascade so far — with more manufacturers still to file

Because this exact hose has been recalled five times now. Cummins recalled 39,052 L9 engines in September after four earlier filings on the same remote fuel filter hose, and the fifth recall voided the fixes from the previous four.[6] The new design didn't hold. Now the truck manufacturers who bought those engines are filing their own recalls, one by one, like dominoes with corporate letterhead.

Here is the cascade so far. Cummins: 39,052 engines. PACCAR: 26,729 trucks across nine model lines, from the Kenworth T270 to the Peterbilt 567.[1] Autocar: its 2022-2027 Xpeditor, same hose, same failure mode.[4] Daimler Truck: filed, count unconfirmed, but Land Line Media reports Paccar and Daimler together account for 75 percent of every L9 engine recall ever filed.[2] More than a dozen manufacturers bought the affected engines, which means the filing queue is nowhere near empty.

Cummins estimates only 3 percent of the hoses are actually defective, which sounds reassuring until you do the arithmetic. Three percent of Paccar's 26,729 is roughly 800 trucks that will leak fuel if their owners do nothing, and nobody knows which 800. No crashes, no fires, no warranty claims reported yet.[2] And the only early warning system available to the other 25,900 owners is the human nose: owner letters do not start arriving until December 20, a full 60 days after the recall was filed, and until then the detection protocol is smell the diesel, see the puddle, watch the low fuel pressure light.[3]

Meanwhile, PACCAR is fighting a second fuel-system war in a courtroom. On October 1, federal judge Robert S. Lasnik ruled that 31 truck buyers can take their MX-13 fuel-injector class action to trial in October 2027, rejecting Paccar's argument that the Clean Air Act barred the claims.[5] The allegation: deposits inside injectors on EPA 2021 MX-13 engines disrupted fuel delivery, causing knocking, violent vibration, and sudden power loss, with one Kansas fleet reporting eight fuel-system incidents by 206,600 miles and a Missouri carrier logging 19 repair visits and 110 days of downtime across five trucks.[7] In a May 2024 internal email, a Paccar engineer wrote that three years in, the company still didn't know how to fix the injector issue; Paccar instead extended injector warranties to 10 years or 435,000 miles, which the plaintiffs characterize as a substitute for a recall.[7]

What this means for you

If you run a Kenworth or Peterbilt with a Cummins L9, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today and skip the December 20 letter. A diesel smell, a puddle under the filter housing, or a low fuel pressure warning means park it and call the dealer; the fix is free under 26PACJ. Fleet buyers shopping used 2022-2027 medium-duty Paccars should confirm the hose replacement in the service history, because these engines will circulate for years. And anyone spec'ing an MX-13 from the 2021-2023 model years should read the injector service records like a biography: the trial starts next October, and the judge has already said the warranty argument won't get the case thrown out.
