Pontiac Died 16 Years Ago. The Grand Prix Still Kills 97 People a Year.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 28, 2026

The spreadsheets smirk, at least: General Motors announced the death of Pontiac on April 27, 2009, the last cars rolled off the line that winter, and the dealer franchises expired in October 2010. Sixteen years later, the brand's most popular sedan is still killing 97 Americans a year: 970 deaths in the Grand Prix between 2014 and 2023, at a rate of 2.14 deaths per 100 million miles traveled.[1][2]

7x
death-rate spread across six cars built on the same GM W-body platform

The Grand Prix rode on GM's W-body platform, the same bones underneath five other nameplates in the FARS data. Rank them by death rate and the family portrait gets strange: the Chevrolet Impala kills at 5.00 per 100M miles, followed by the Buick Century at 2.41 and the Grand Prix at 2.14, then the Buick Regal at 1.01, the Buick LaCrosse at 0.72, and the Chevrolet Monte Carlo at 0.71, the same platform under all six, with a sevenfold spread from top to bottom.[1]

The obvious explanation is drunk drivers, and the data declines to cooperate: pull the toxicology cross-tab and impairment is flat across the whole family: Century 21.8%, Grand Prix 21.5%, Impala 21.4%, Monte Carlo 21.3%, LaCrosse 20.1%, Regal 19.3%. Monte Carlo drivers are exactly as impaired as Impala drivers, and the Monte Carlo kills at one-seventh the rate. Whatever is slaughtering people in Impalas, it is not the bottle. Which means the drunk-driver theory of the W-body is dead, and it died sober.[1]

What varies when the platform and the impairment don't is the lives the cars lead. Consider the Impala, America's default fleet sedan: rentals, government motor pools, livery work, cars that accumulate brutal urban mileage and get maintained on somebody else's budget. By contrast, the Monte Carlo was a two-door coupe bought by enthusiasts who chose it on purpose. A platform is a skeleton; the risk lives in who climbs inside and how many miles they ask of it. That is also why the lab results mislead: the Grand Prix earned a Good in the IIHS moderate-overlap frontal test and five stars in NHTSA's frontal rating for the driver, and still posts 2.14. A crash test measures the car; the morgue measures the car's life.[3][4]

Now the part where I argue with myself, because the strongest objection deserves full strength. That 5.00 may be partly measurement fiction: fleet and rental cars pile on miles that estimated VMT figures can undercount, which inflates the rate, and the Impala nameplate in this window blends two different platforms (W-body through 2013, Epsilon after) while the Grand Prix is pure 2004-2008 W-body. Fair enough, but halve the Impala's rate to be generous and you still have a 3.5x spread sitting on top of flat impairment, and every car in this comparison was at least six years old in the FARS window, so age effects cut against the finding rather than for it, and the core survives the error bars.[1]

If you are shopping the used market, this is the rare dataset that prices the badge. A Monte Carlo and an Impala share a platform and a parts bin, and one kills at seven times the rate of the other, so buy the coupe. If you already own a Grand Prix, know that its side-impact rating was Marginal and its rear-impact rating Poor, side curtain airbags were optional, and you should verify the car you bought actually has them rather than assuming the brochure applied to your trim. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change. The brand is dead, but the cars are not.[3][5]
