The Gap

America Broke Below 1.0 Deaths Per 100 Million Miles. Norway Is at 0.2.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
August 24, 2026

NHTSA announced on July 8 that America's first-quarter traffic fatality rate dropped to 0.99 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, the lowest Q1 figure since 2014 and only the second time the rate has dipped below the symbolic 1.0 threshold. Headlines used words like "historic." Reuters led with "fell sharply." Transportation Secretary rhetoric included "encouraging trend."

0.99 vs. 0.2
U.S. Q1 2026 fatality rate per 100M miles vs. Norway's rate per 100M km. Even after unit conversion, America kills at 3× the pace.

Then I converted the units, and the celebration collapsed.

International road safety data is measured in vehicle kilometers traveled, not miles, and one mile equals 1.609 kilometers. Divide America's 0.99 per 100 million VMT by that factor and you get 0.62 deaths per 100 million VKT.[1] That number sits next to Norway's 0.2, Sweden's 0.3, and Iceland's 0.2, all from OECD reporting through the Australian National Road Safety Data Hub.[2] America's "breakthrough" is three times Norway's rate, more than double Sweden's, and higher than Australia's 0.5 despite Australia being a country with vast rural distances and a love affair with V8 utes.

Per-capita, the picture darkens further. The United States kills 11.78 people per 100,000 population on its roads, while Norway loses 1.73 and the United Kingdom manages 2.26. Japan, with 125 million people crammed onto mountainous islands with winter ice and typhoon season, loses 2.72 per 100,000.[3] The OECD median sits at 4.09. America is not in the same league as its peers, not in the same sport, not in the same stadium.

I ran the excess-death math because someone has to. In Q1 2026, Americans drove approximately 784.8 billion miles, which converts to about 1.26 trillion kilometers. At Norway's fatality rate, that volume of driving would have produced roughly 2,526 deaths. America recorded 7,770.[4] The difference is 5,244 people in 90 days who would be alive if American roads performed like Norwegian ones. Annualize that and you are looking at approximately 21,000 excess deaths per year attributable to the gap between what America accepts and what peer nations achieve.

For context, the Q1 record is 0.98, set in 2011, fifteen years ago. America has spent a decade and a half trying to match a number it already hit, while Norway dropped from 0.4 to 0.2 over the same period. The U.S. treadmill is not getting faster; the other runners have simply lapped it.

Meanwhile the Q1 data arrives alongside full-year 2025 numbers showing cyclist deaths rose 4 percent to 1,148, their highest sustained level in four decades, even as overall fatalities fell 6.7 percent to 36,640.[4] The gains accrue to people inside vehicles. The people outside them keep dying at flat or increasing rates, which is precisely what you would expect from a country that treats road geometry as a local zoning decision and vehicle front-end height as a branding choice.

None of this is inevitable. Norway has snow, Sweden has moose, and Japan has earthquakes and 17 million elderly drivers. They built different infrastructure, enforced different speed limits, designed different vehicle fronts, and accepted different political costs for doing so. America chose not to, and 21,000 people a year pay for the choice with a currency they cannot earn back.

What this means for you: The 0.99 number is real and the trend is moving in the right direction. But if you are comparing American roads to peer nations and wondering whether your commute is as safe as it could be, the answer is no, not by a factor of three. Check your vehicle's recall status at nhtsa.gov/recalls. If you are shopping for a new car, look for vehicles with pedestrian AEB and the IIHS's highest-tier crashworthiness rating. And if your city is debating road diets or protected bike lanes, the international data says those interventions are not cosmetic. They are the difference between 0.99 and 0.2.

Limitations

The Q1 2026 figure is a preliminary estimate; final FARS data will not be available until early 2028. OECD VKT rates are from 2023 and may have shifted. The excess-death calculation assumes Norway's rate is achievable at American driving volumes, which ignores differences in road network composition, rural vs. urban splits, average trip lengths, and vehicle fleet age. Per-capita comparisons are complicated by the fact that Americans drive roughly twice as many miles per capita as Europeans, inflating both exposure and the per-capita death count. The cyclist divergence noted here is covered in more detail in a separate Crash Report analysis.

The Strongest Counterargument

America is geographically enormous, overwhelmingly car-dependent, and drives far more miles per capita than any European country. The per-capita gap partly reflects driving volume, not just driving danger. Norway has 5.4 million people, a compact road network, and brutal winters that suppress VMT for months. Comparing the two may overstate the policy gap and understate the structural difficulty of achieving Scandinavian rates across 4.2 million miles of American roadway serving 340 million people. The VKT-adjusted gap (3×, not 6×) is the fairer comparison, and even it doesn't fully account for network complexity.
