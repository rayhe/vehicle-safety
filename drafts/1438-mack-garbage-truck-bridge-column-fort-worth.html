The Crash Cushion Never Got Touched. The Garbage Truck Went Behind It.

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
November 8, 2026

On September 23, 2026, at 6:11 in the evening, a 2017 Mack LR633 garbage truck finished its route in Fort Worth and headed south on US 287 toward the Waste Management yard, and it never got there.[1] Drifting out of the center lane, the truck crossed the right lane and the shoulder, rode a concrete embankment up behind a traffic barrier, and hit the first bridge column of the Erath Street overpass, killing the driver. His passenger went to the hospital with serious injuries.[1]

How much more lethal a bridge-pier strike is than the average heavy-truck run-off-road crash — 8.8% fatal vs 1.9% (FHWA Texas data)

Here is the part that should bother every highway engineer in Texas. That bridge column was protected. A concrete barrier with an integrated crash cushion stood between the travel lanes and the column, exactly where the design manuals say it should, and the truck never touched it. It arrived from behind, climbing the embankment that slopes up past the barrier to carry the overpass approach, a wall with an open back door. The embankment doubled as a ramp, and the cushion sat there guarding a direction nobody came from.[1]

Federal data says this was not a freak outcome, only a rare one. A Federal Highway Administration study of Texas heavy-truck crashes found 159 run-off-road bridge-pier strikes on divided highways, with 14 of them fatal.[2] That is an 8.8 percent fatality rate, against 1.9 percent for the average truck run-off-road crash. Divide the two and a pier strike comes out about 4.6 times as lethal. On tangent sections the gap widens: 10.9 percent fatal, 5.6 times the baseline. Minnesota’s numbers point the same way, 3.6 percent against 1.2 percent. And then there is the cell this crash landed in. Texas logged 48 run-off-road crashes involving garbage trucks on divided highways. Zero of them hit a bridge pier, until September 23.[2]

Damage to the column was so extensive that TxDOT removed the section of Erath Street crossing southbound US 287, and as of the NTSB’s preliminary report on October 15, the street was still closed while a new overpass goes up.[1] NTSB investigators are still working the case with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, TxDOT, and Fort Worth police. No probable cause yet, and no answer yet to the only question that matters: why did the truck leave its lane in the first place? A medical event, a distraction, a mechanical failure, all of them still on the table.

One crash proves nothing about intent, and the numbers come with their own fine print. The Texas study counts 159 pier strikes, a small sample, and its data predates the current fleet. Fixed-object crashes still kill about one in five people who die on American roads, according to National Safety Council figures, and in 2004 alone embankments killed 525 people.[3] That last number is old, and it is the best national figure available, which says something about how little attention this corner of road safety gets.

What changes now depends on who is reading. For the agencies that build these roads, the lesson is blunt: a crash cushion protects a column from one direction only, and wherever a sloped embankment runs behind the barrier, the barrier is a fence with a gate. Extend the barrier, regrade the slope, or accept that the next truck will find the same gap. For everyone else, the takeaway is simpler. Give work trucks room. A garbage truck finishing its route at 6:11 p.m. is a tired driver in a 30-ton truck with a cab built for visibility, not for crashworthiness, and on US 287 that was the difference between a bad evening and a closed road.
