The Gap

The Bigger the SUV, the Bigger the Body Count

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026

A Chevrolet Tahoe weighs north of 5,500 pounds. In June 2024, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety gave it a Poor in the updated moderate-overlap crash test. Ten years of federal fatality data put Tahoe occupants’ death rate at 2.49 per 100 million miles. Kia’s Telluride, smaller, lighter, and unibody, sits at 0.04. That works out to the three-ton truck killing its own occupants at 62 times the rate of the family hauler. Read it again, because it does not get less true.[1][2]

Tahoe death rate (2.49) vs. Telluride (0.04) per 100M miles, FARS 2014–2023

Start with the lab, because the lab is the part nobody can argue with. In June 2024, IIHS put three full-size SUVs through its updated protocols: the Tahoe, the Expedition, and the Jeep Wagoneer. Tahoe’s report card: a Poor in the updated moderate-overlap test, the one with a second-row dummy the size of a small woman. Measurements from that dummy showed a high risk of head and neck injuries, plus abdominal injury risk from seatbelt forces. Expedition did marginally better, which is to say it earned a Marginal, with a compromised safety cage, severe footwell intrusion, and a driver-side curtain airbag that did not deploy. Wagoneer, body-on-frame just like the other two, drove home with a Top Safety Pick, which means the frame is not the excuse.[2][3][6]

Then the lights. Every Tahoe trim ships with Poor headlights. Expedition manages a Marginal. Tahoe’s pedestrian crash prevention earned a Marginal because, in the Institute’s phrasing, it faltered in the dark. At 12 mph, high beams on, facing a pedestrian dummy at night, the Tahoe scrubbed 3 mph. Three whole miles per hour, a deceleration you could out-brake on a bicycle.[2]

IIHS president David Harkey gave the eulogy himself: the huge mass of these large SUVs provides some additional protection in crashes with smaller vehicles, though it also means they present more danger to other road users.[2] And the flip side of their large size is that there is a lot more force to manage when they crash into a fixed obstacle like a tree or bridge abutment. Translation: a Tahoe will win a fight with a Civic. Guess who loses: the Civic’s driver. Against a tree, the Tahoe’s extra ton is just extra energy looking for somewhere to go, and the IIHS barrier found exactly where it goes: the footwell, the A-pillar, the second row.[3]

Now the road: FARS 2014–2023, nameplate death rates per 100 million miles. Yukon 2.55, Tahoe 2.49, Expedition 2.31, Suburban 1.36. Unibody three-rows for comparison: Grand Cherokee 0.51, Highlander 0.42, Pilot 0.29, Palisade 0.06, Telluride 0.04. Combined body count for the big body-on-frame four: 5,814 dead. Telluride plus Palisade: 69. Before you blame the drivers, the toxicology files disagree: 20.6 percent of Tahoe drivers in fatal crashes were impaired, versus 16.8 percent for the Palisade. Four points of impairment does not explain a forty-fold death gap.[1]

Here is the asterisk, and it is the size of a Tahoe. Almost 89 percent of the Tahoe’s deaths happened in pre-2010 model years. For the Telluride, 100 percent happened in 2020 or later, because the Telluride did not exist before 2020. This comparison is, in large part, a 2007 truck versus a 2021 crossover. Explorer proves the point from the other direction: unibody since 2011, death rate 1.54, because 91 percent of its fatal crashes involved pre-2015 vehicles. And the Sequoia, body-on-frame to its bones, sits at 0.83, which means frame alone is not destiny. The Wagoneer proved the same point in the lab: body-on-frame, Top Safety Pick, so the difference is engineering, not architecture. Old is deadly, and the big GM and Ford nameplates have the oldest fleets on the road.[1][6]

Which is why the lab matters more than the league table. Those IIHS tests controlled for age: current model years, same barrier, same dummies. Big trucks still flunked. New does not fix the physics of three tons meeting an immovable object. It does not fix headlights the Institute rated Poor on every single Tahoe trim.[2]

For the final insult, check the trophy case. In July 2026, J.D. Power named the Tahoe a segment winner in its Initial Quality Study, tied with the Sequoia, with the Expedition close behind. Initial quality measures problems per 100 vehicles in the first 90 days: squeaks, glitches, trim fit. Tahoe is superbly screwed together. It simply earned a Poor when IIHS screwed it into a barrier at 40 mph. Buyers are being told these are the best-built big SUVs on sale, which is true, and the safest-feeling, which is also true, right up until the feeling meets the data.[4]

What to do: shopping for a three-row, the unibody crowd owns both the lab and the road. Telluride, Palisade, Pilot, Highlander. Check the current IIHS ratings before you sign, because the Institute now scores second-row dummies and the Tahoe’s report card shows why. Already own a Tahoe or Expedition: the weak points are specific. Second-row restraints, headlights on all trims, and night pedestrian braking. Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because big SUVs accumulate big recall lists. And if you are buying a used Tahoe, understand what you are actually buying: the safety engineering of its model year, not its curb weight. A 2008 Tahoe has the mass of a tank and roughly the crash structure of one.[2]

Honest caveats, because the data deserves them. These rates use estimated vehicle miles, not odometer readings, so treat low-volume models like the Sequoia as fuzzy. FARS counts each model’s own occupant deaths, not the deaths it inflicts on other cars, where the big SUVs are worse. Telluride’s 0.04 rests on 31 deaths, early-fleet data that will move. And none of this says a 2024 Tahoe is a 2007 Tahoe. It says the nameplate earned its number the hard way, over ten years, and the lab says the current one still has homework to do.
