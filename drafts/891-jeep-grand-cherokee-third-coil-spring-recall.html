Jeep Recalled the Grand Cherokee's Rear Coil Springs a Third Time. The Springs Were Never the Problem.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 12, 2026

Stellantis just recalled 328,381 Jeep Grand Cherokees because their rear coil springs can detach while driving. That number would be unremarkable, except this is the third recall of the same springs in three years, and the previous two were the recalls that failed.[1]

3
Recalls of the same rear coil springs: May 2023, February 2026, September 2026

In May 2023, FCA launched campaign 23V413 against more than 345,000 Grand Cherokees and Grand Cherokee Ls with improperly installed rear coil springs. Dealer techs were instructed to inspect each spring's position and orientation against its isolator, the molded rubber seat the coil rides in. Simple. Visual. Hard to get wrong, presumably.[3]

They got it wrong. Twenty-one months later, NHTSA received a second filing admitting the first recall's inspections had themselves been botched. Campaign 26V051, February 2026, pulled 80,620 vehicles back for what Stellantis's own paperwork amounts to calling a repair of a prior repair. During the 23V413 work, some springs had been installed incorrectly or left incomplete. Stellantis logged 20 customer-assistance records and 284 warranty claims on that second-generation defect, every one of them traceable to springs that the first recall's dealers had seated wrong or left half-finished, and the company reported no known crashes or injuries.[3][4]

Now comes 89D, NHTSA 26V562000, issued September 2 and announced this week. It covers the 2022–2023 Grand Cherokee, the 2022–2023 Grand Cherokee 4XE, and the 2021–2023 Grand Cherokee L, and it supersedes both prior campaigns outright. If your Jeep was fixed in 2023, or re-fixed in February, you get to do it a third time. This time the remedy finally changed: instead of another inspection, dealers replace the rear coil spring lower isolators with new hardware.[1][2]

Somebody should be embarrassed, and it isn't the spring. The spring did its job. A coil spring is a hunk of bent steel. An isolator costs pennies and installs in minutes. No exotic metallurgy, no software, no battery chemistry to get wrong. The component never failed; the process did, twice, in the dealership service bays that NHTSA's recall system trusts to execute the remedy. When the fix becomes the defect, the defect is not in the car. It is in the repair.[3]

If you own a 2021–2023 Grand Cherokee L, a 2022–2023 Grand Cherokee, or a 2022–2023 Grand Cherokee 4XE, you are in this recall whether or not you already answered the first two letters. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Interim owner notifications mail September 29, with a follow-up letter once the final remedy is fully available. When you call the dealer, ask a specific question: has the isolator replacement under 89D been performed, or just the earlier inspection? Owners who stop at "I already did that recall" are exactly who this campaign is hunting for.[2]

Limitations: No accidents or injuries have been reported across any of the three campaigns, so the severity here is hypothetical; a spring detaching at speed is alarming, but zero events occurred in roughly three and a half years. Population overlap between the campaigns is not published, so no claim is made about any individual vehicle being recalled three times. This is a recall-procedure story, not a fatality-rate analysis; FARS data was not used. The 20 assistance records and 284 warranty claims are Stellantis's self-reported counts from the 26V051 filing.[3]

The strongest counterargument: three campaigns for one component could be read as the system working. NHTSA caught the bad repair, Stellantis kept widening the net, nobody got hurt, and the remedy kept escalating until the root fix, hardware replacement, finally landed. That reading is fair. It is also a reading that required 328,381 owners to visit the dealership up to three times for a rubber seat.
