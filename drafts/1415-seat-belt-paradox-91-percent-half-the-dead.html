Existential Dread

91% of America Buckles Up. Half the People Who Die in Cars Don't.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

Nine out of ten Americans buckle up, and NHTSA watched them do it: the 2024 National Occupant Protection Use Survey put observed belt use at 91.2 percent, statistically flat against 91.9 percent in 2023.[2] Now open NHTSA's other publication from the same year. Of the 22,713 people who died inside passenger vehicles in 2024, 9,758 were not wearing a seat belt. Among the dead whose belt use was known, 48 percent were unrestrained.[1][5] Both numbers are true, and they describe two different Americas, only one of which shows up in the survey.

48%
of passenger-vehicle occupants killed in 2024 with known restraint use were unrestrained, while observed belt use hit 91.2%

That split has not moved in a decade: unrestrained deaths were 48 percent of the known-restraint dead in 2015 and 48 percent in 2024, with a pandemic-era peak of 51 percent in 2020,[1] while observed use climbed from 88.5 percent to 91.2 percent over the same ten years. The belt campaign spent a decade convincing the convincible, and those still dying unbelted were never in the audience: they are the 8.8 percent the survey barely sees, because it watches daytime traffic at intersections and the dead skew toward everything it does not watch.

Start with who they are: men made up nearly two-thirds of passenger-vehicle deaths, and 53 percent of the men who died were unbelted, against 40 percent of women.[1] Worst are ages 21 to 24 and 25 to 34, where 60 percent of the dead were unrestrained, and pickup drivers are the worst vehicle class, 59 percent of them killed unbelted against 47 percent in cars and 46 percent in SUVs. Night is worse than day, 56 percent against 41 percent. The back seat is where 58 percent of the dead were unrestrained, against 47 percent up front. The second-row middle seat hit 67 percent.[1]

Physics is not subtle here: in fatal crashes, 48 percent of those killed were unrestrained, against 13 percent of those who survived.[1] Ejection is blunter: 84 percent of occupants totally ejected from their vehicles were killed, and only 1 percent of belted occupants in fatal crashes were ejected at all, against 24 percent of the unbelted.[1] A belt cuts fatal injury by 45 percent in a car and 60 percent in a light truck. An air bag on its own cuts fatalities 14 percent without a belt and 11 percent with one.[1] Air bags are garnish. Belts are the meal.

State by state, the paradox gets stranger: Hawaii observes 97.2 percent belt use and 65 percent of its dead were unbelted, the highest share in the country, while Wyoming observes 73.4 percent, the lowest anywhere, and also buries 65 percent unbelted. Massachusetts observes 84.4 percent and buries 63 percent; Nevada observes 94.8 percent and buries 36 percent, the lowest share anywhere, and observed use does not predict who dies unbelted, because the survey and the morgue sample different populations.[1][3][4] Neither does the law: primary-enforcement states observed 91.7 percent use against 89.2 percent in secondary-law states, a 2.5-point gap, and New Hampshire, the only state with no adult belt law, sits at 59 percent unbelted dead.[1][6]

Run the counterfactual on NHTSA's own effectiveness numbers: apply the 45 percent car and 60 percent light-truck reductions to the 9,758 unbelted dead, weighted by the car-truck split, and roughly 5,200 of them would likely have survived. That is a back-of-the-envelope estimate, not a NHTSA figure: it assumes the crash itself is unchanged, which flatters the belt, since the unbelted dead are also likelier to be speeding and impaired, but even discounted heavily, it is the largest single preventable pile of bodies in American road safety. Twenty-seven people a day, every day, died in 2024 for want of a three-second motion.

So buckle up in the back seat, where the reminder chime does not arrive until September 2027. Buckle up at night, when the unbelted share jumps to 56 percent. If you drive a pickup, the truck will not save you: 59 percent of pickup drivers who died were unbelted. And look up your state: in half of them, most of the people who die in cars were not wearing a belt. Surveys say 91 percent of America buckles up; morgues say the other 9 percent is where the dying happens.
