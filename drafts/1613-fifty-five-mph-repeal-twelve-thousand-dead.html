Congress Repealed the 55 MPH Limit 31 Years Ago Today. The Published Death Toll Is 12,545.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 28, 2026

Thirty-one years ago today, Congress voted to repeal the National Maximum Speed Limit, and nobody in the room was voting about safety. President Nixon had signed the 55 mph limit into law in January 1974 to save gasoline during the oil embargo, not to save lives. Safety was a side effect. It turned out to be the largest side effect in the history of American traffic law: in the first year under the limit, road deaths fell from 54,052 to 45,196, a drop of 16.4 percent, the sharpest single-year decline ever recorded.[1] Congress spent the next two decades dismantling that accident anyway, and researchers have since put a number on what the dismantling cost.

12,545
Deaths attributed to raised speed limits in the decade after the 1995 repeal, per a peer-reviewed study in the American Journal of Public Health

Start with 1974, because the numbers there are almost indecently clean. Total traffic fatalities fell by 8,856 in a single year. Some of that drop came from the embargo itself: people drove about 3 percent fewer miles while gas was scarce, and a recession was already underway. Even after accounting for less driving, the Transportation Research Board credited the slower, more uniform speeds with saving 3,000 to 5,000 lives in 1974 alone.[2] No airbag mandate, no crumple-zone rule, no electronic stability control has ever produced a one-year improvement in that range. A number on a sign did it, at a cost of zero dollars per car, and the country kept the limit for twenty-one years largely because the bodies kept not showing up.

Then came the repeal, and the experiment ran in reverse. Congress loosened the limit to 65 mph on rural interstates in 1987, and 41 states raised their limits within months. Studies of that change found rural interstate fatalities rising 15 to 30 percent, depending on the method.[2] Full repeal arrived on November 28, 1995, signed by President Clinton on December 8, and states moved fast: within a year, 23 states had posted 70 or 75 mph on rural interstates. An Insurance Institute for Highway Safety study of the 24 states that raised limits found interstate fatalities up 15 percent and fatality rates up 17 percent against states that held the line.[3] Another study found rural interstate fatalities 35 percent higher than expected in states that went to 70 mph, and 38 percent higher in states that went to 75.[4] Every time the limit went up, the morgue got busier, on a schedule too tight to be coincidence.

Short-term studies can be argued with. Long-term ones are harder. Friedman, Hedeker, and Richter tracked the full decade after repeal in the American Journal of Public Health, modeling every state, every road type, and every limit change through 2005, controlling for traffic volume, population density, fleet size, and vehicle age.[1] Their estimate: raised speed limits caused a 3.2 percent increase in road fatalities nationwide, concentrated where the limits actually rose, 9.1 percent on rural interstates and 4.0 percent on urban ones. Over ten years, that works out to 12,545 deaths, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 8,739 to 16,352, plus 36,583 injuries in fatal crashes. Take the low end of the interval and it is still more than twice the death toll of September 11, spread over a decade in increments of one and two, on roads nobody was watching.

Physics explains why the effect is so stubborn. Crash energy scales with the square of speed, so the penalty for going faster is not linear. Moving from 55 to 75 mph adds 86 percent more kinetic energy to the same crash: (75² − 55²) / 55² = 2,600 / 3,025. Moving from 55 to 85 mph, the limit Texas now posts on some highways, adds 139 percent. Even the modest-looking jump from 65 to 75 mph adds a third more energy. Every safety system in the car, belts, airbags, crumple zones, has to absorb that extra energy, and none of them got a third better in the years the limits went up. A 2026 car is far safer than a 1974 car at any speed, which is exactly why the repeal's toll is so damning: modern engineering spent fifty years buying back safety margin, and higher limits spent a large share of it.

Fairness requires the counterargument at full strength, because 12,545 is a model estimate, not a count from death certificates. Its confidence interval spans 8,739 to 16,352, which is wide enough to drive a truck through at 75 mph. Some of the 1974 improvement came from less driving, not slower driving, and at least one study of the 1987 increase found no fatality-rate rise on the raised roads. Higher limits also buy real time: a 100-mile trip takes 12 fewer minutes at 75 than at 65, multiplied across millions of trips. And the repeal's defenders have a point about the modern fleet: a 75 mph crash in a 2026 car with a five-star rating is more survivable than a 55 mph crash in a 1974 car with a lap belt. None of that erases the direction of the evidence. It only argues about the size, and every serious estimate of the size is in the thousands.

What to do with this depends on who you are. If you drive, the cheapest safety upgrade available is already installed in your car: the speedometer has a needle, and the needle is a choice. Going 65 instead of 75 on a 100-mile interstate trip costs 12 minutes and sheds a third of your crash energy. If you set policy, the record is unusually clear for a traffic debate: the only national speed experiment America ever ran produced the largest one-year fatality drop on record going in, and a five-figure death toll coming out, and the follow-up study in 2017 put the excess deaths at 33,000 through 2013 alone.[2] Thirty-one years after the vote, the repeal's defenders still have not produced a study showing the higher limits saved lives. They have only produced faster trips, and the invoice for those trips is still being paid, one crash at a time.
