The Gap

Five Stars Up Front, Poor in the Back Seat

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 4, 2026

Start with the first 150 milliseconds. A car hits a deformable barrier at 40 mph with 40 percent of its front end, and two dummies ride it out: a man-sized dummy up front, and a dummy the size of a small woman or a 12-year-old child in the seat behind the driver.[6] In the Volkswagen Tiguan and the Honda CR-V, the man-sized dummy comes through fine. Both cars earn Good for structure, Good for the driver, and Good in the small overlap and side tests.[1][2] Then the rear dummy reports back, and both cars fail the same way.

Poor / Poor
IIHS updated moderate overlap ratings for the Tiguan and the CR-V, driven by identical rear-seat subscores: rear chest Marginal, rear restraints and dummy kinematics Poor

IIHS scores the redesigned Tiguan Poor overall in this test, CEF2556, a rating applying to 2025-26 models.[1] Its rear subscores: head and neck Acceptable, chest Marginal, thigh Good, restraints and dummy kinematics Poor.[1] IIHS scores the CR-V Poor overall in the same test, CEF2343, applying to 2023-27 models.[2] Its rear subscores: head and neck Good, chest Marginal, thigh Good, restraints and dummy kinematics Poor, with a rear chest index of 42.[2] Side by side, the back-seat rows match: Marginal chest, Poor restraints. In Tiguan footage, the shoulder belt rides toward the rear dummy's neck while the lap belt slides from pelvis to abdomen.[4] That migration is the failure. A belt that leaves the pelvis stops restraining the body it was built to hold.

Both cars still carry five-star NHTSA overall ratings, and both still miss any IIHS award, because a Good grade in this test is now required for one.[3][6] No contradiction is hiding here, only two different exams. NHTSA's star program tests front occupants in full-frontal and side configurations. IIHS added the rear dummy to an offset frontal test in 2022 precisely because that seat had been graded on hope for years. A window sticker can therefore tell the truth and still leave out the seat your kids occupy.

Road data then splits the pair. FARS 2014-2023 gives the Tiguan 126 deaths on a fleet of 743,750, a rate of 0.14 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled.[5] It gives the CR-V 2,072 deaths on a fleet of 3,150,000, a rate of 0.53.[5] Divide the rates and the CR-V runs 3.8 times higher. Impairment does not explain that gap: Tiguan drivers in fatal crashes test any-impaired at 20.0 percent, CR-V drivers at 17.6 percent.[5] Same lab failure, different body counts. A Poor grade names a belt-geometry weakness with precision. It does not, by itself, predict how often that weakness turns into a death.

Fairness requires the limits stated plainly. FARS covers all model years in its 2014-2023 window, while the IIHS grades apply to the current generations, so this is a generational comparison, not a same-car measurement.[5] FARS also does not break deaths down by seating position at the model level, so no claim follows that rear-seat occupants account for either rate.[5] None of that rescues the belt geometry. It only stops the grade from being oversold.

If you are shopping either SUV, read the IIHS moderate overlap result before you sign, not just the stars.[1][2] Check the model year against the rating's coverage: 2025-26 for the Tiguan, 2023-27 for the CR-V. Keep second-row belts snug and low on the hips, and never let a child ride with the shoulder belt under an arm or behind the back, since belt migration is exactly what this test caught. Finally, check any used Tiguan or CR-V for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because an IIHS grade is not a recall and a clean recall search is not a safety grade.
