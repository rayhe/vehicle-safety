Tesla Certified 15 Canadian Cars as U.S.-Legal. One Airbag Says Otherwise.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 24, 2026

Fifteen cars. That is the entire population of NHTSA recall 26V445, filed July 17, 2026, and every one of them is missing a knee airbag that federal law requires, which makes each car illegal to drive in the United States even though the identical vehicle is perfectly legal in Canada. Instead of repairing them, Tesla is buying them back.[1]

15
Vehicles in recall 26V445, 0.00004% of the 34.8 million units recalled in 2026, and the only ones whose remedy is a buyback

Twelve are 2024 Model 3 sedans, built between December 2023 and July 2024. Three are 2026 Model Y crossovers, built between August 2025 and February 2026. All fifteen rolled out of Tesla's Shanghai or Berlin factories built to Canadian Motor Vehicle Safety Standards, sold to Canadian customers, and then imported into the United States by their owners.[1] Under Declaration Box 2B, a private importer can bring a car across if the manufacturer certifies in writing that it meets every applicable U.S. safety standard. Tesla wrote those letters. Between June 2024 and April 2026, the company certified these cars as compliant, with exceptions only for labeling and daytime running lamps.[1]

Every letter was wrong, because Canadian-market cars do not carry the driver knee airbag that U.S. rules demand for the unbelted crash test, so the cars fail FMVSS 208. Model Ys in the group also lack the interior-impact countermeasures FMVSS 201 requires, and the Model 3s were never put through the bumper impact testing Part 581 demands. Nobody at the border opens a dashboard to verify an airbag exists. Tesla's signature was the entire inspection.[1][3]

What caught it was not a crash, a complaint, or an audit. On April 28, 2026, a customer sent Tesla a compliance-letter request through a channel the company describes in its federal filing as outside the customary communication channel, and somebody inside Tesla finally asked whether the cars it had certified for two years actually met the standards on the letter. Tesla stopped issuing letters for 2024-and-newer Model 3 and Model Y cars that same day, widened the freeze to every Shanghai- and Berlin-built vehicle on May 8, sat down with NHTSA's Office of the Chief Counsel on May 28, and concluded on June 29 that the cars do not comply. Zero warranty claims, zero field reports, zero collisions, zero injuries, zero deaths.[1] A clerk routing a letter to the wrong inbox did what two years of federal oversight did not.

Here is the case against treating this as a real safety story, and it is a strong one. Fifteen cars, no injuries, no crashes, a paperwork violation with no demonstrated harm, and a manufacturer that volunteered the buyback before anyone asked. A belted driver still has the rest of the restraint system, and the missing airbag only matters in the unbelted test, where the occupant has already declined the car's primary safety device. By that reading, 2026 filed 709 other campaigns covering 34.8 million vehicles, most of them with actual defects and actual victims.[1][2] Fair enough, but the counterargument is also the point. Federal safety compliance is binary, and the import system runs on manufacturer honesty with no second check at the border. Fifteen is the number Tesla found, not necessarily the number imported. And a buyback, the rarest remedy in the federal system, is what a manufacturer offers when a car cannot be fixed, because the car itself is the defect.

What to do with this: if you privately imported a Tesla from Canada, or any car on a manufacturer compliance letter, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today, because a letter in your glovebox is not proof of compliance, and Tesla has now rescinded its letters for Shanghai- and Berlin-built cars. If yours is one of the fifteen, Tesla owes you a buyback at no cost, and the letters went out in early September 2026. If you are shopping for a used Tesla with a Canadian history, ask for the compliance letter, then verify it, because the last fifteen owners learned the hard way that the letter was the only thing standing between their car and a federal violation.[1][2]
