# Research: IIHS First-Ever Commercial Vehicle Crash-Avoidance Ratings

## Story angle
IIHS published its first crash-avoidance ratings for commercial vehicles (cargo vans and Class 3 pickups) on Oct 8, 2026, reprinted as an IIHS-byline release Oct 19. The vehicles that pose the most danger to other road users carry the weakest front crash prevention: 9 of 11 rated marginal or poor in vehicle-to-vehicle. The best-selling work vans in America, the Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500, have NO automatic emergency braking at all (forward collision warning optional only) and scored poor in both tests. Meanwhile the only good scores went to cargo vans, two of them electric (Chevrolet BrightDrop 400, Rivian RCV-Delivery 500, pedestrian) and one Mercedes Sprinter 2500 (vehicle-to-vehicle). The novel angle: the tech that has been nearly universal on family cars for a decade is optional or absent on the 14,000-pound vehicles sharing your lane, and the systems that do exist can't reliably see motorcycles or children.

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy: YES. First-ever IIHS ratings for a whole vehicle segment, with an IIHS president "wake-up call" quote and a fleet-purchasing implications angle. Multiple trade-press follow-ups (Automotive Fleet Oct 22, collisionweek, claimsjournal).
- Novel angle on data: YES. The article's original contribution is the asymmetry framing: 6,215 people killed in 2024 in crashes involving heavy/medium-duty vehicles or light vans (16% of all US roadway fatalities), yet these are the vehicles where AEB is optional. Plus the two blind spots nobody headlines: motorcycle detection (several systems gave no warning and no braking in most motorcycle trials) and the child dummy that "disappeared" from sensors as tall trucks approached.

## Key facts to use
- 11 vehicles tested: 7 vans, 4 Class 3 pickups
- Vehicle-to-vehicle: only 1 Good (Sprinter 2500); 9 of 11 marginal or poor
- Express 2500 and Savana 2500 (2025-27): no AEB, poor in both tests, forward collision warning optional
- Pickups: zero good/acceptable in vehicle-to-vehicle; 3 of 4 poor
- Motorcycles: several systems gave no warning and no braking in most trials
- Child dummies "disappeared" from sensors as tall trucks approached (sensors near rearview mirror)
- 6,215 killed in 2024 in crashes involving heavy/medium-duty vehicles or light vans; 16% of all US roadway deaths
- Federal airbag standards don't apply to many of these vehicles (regulatory gap)
- Same protocols as passenger cars; at least one good performer per test, so better is achievable
- Harkey quotes: "wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers"; danger to other road users

## Actionable insight for readers
If you buy or manage a fleet: spec the Sprinter 2500, BrightDrop 400, or Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 for front crash prevention, and treat the Express/Savana as what they are — 2026-model vans with no AEB. If you're a driver or pedestrian sharing the road with work trucks: the Class 3 pickups cannot be counted on to warn or brake for a motorcycle, so lane-splitting or riding in their blind zone is a worse bet than ever. Everyone else: these ratings will now sit next to the sticker on fleet lots, and IIHS is building an ROI calculator that turns safety into a line item.

## Limitations / counterargument
- IIHS ratings are test-track protocol scores, not real-world crash-outcome data; the espresso/collisionweek coverage itself notes no defect has been established and this measures behavior under IIHS's protocol, not crashes on public roads.
- Fleet crash rates depend heavily on driver behavior, hours, mileage, and load; fleets argue AEB presence is one variable among many, and the cheapest vans stay cheap partly because fleets self-insure and train drivers.
- Only 11 vehicles, one snapshot of 2025-27 model years; manufacturers can and do update systems.
- Strongest counterargument at full strength: commercial fleets have driven with minimal driver-assist for decades without catastrophe; crash rates for these segments are not purely a function of sensor suites; mandating AEB raises purchase prices that fleets pass on to every customer; and the Express/Savana's simplicity is exactly why they dominate municipal and utility fleets that maintain their own shops. A rating is not a body count.
- That said: 6,215 deaths is a body count, and the physics argument cuts the other way — a 14,000-pound truck with optional collision warning is a policy choice, not a law of nature.
