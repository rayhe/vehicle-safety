# Research: #988 — "IIHS Spent $600 Million on Crash Tests. It Got Back $538 Billion."

**Slug:** `988-600m-crash-lab-50k-lives-roi`
**Journalist:** Clara Rollover (consumer safety advocate; kicker: By The Numbers)
**Peg:** IIHS 30th-anniversary study, released June 24, 2026.

## The news
- IIHS study: vehicle improvements spurred by its crashworthiness testing program saved an estimated **48,352 lives between 1999 and 2024**, worth **$538 billion** in societal savings.
- Program launched 1995; anniversary head-to-head demo crashed a 1996 Chevrolet Blazer into a 2026 Blazer (moderate overlap). 2026 driver: bumps and bruises. 1996 driver: serious, potentially fatal injuries; compartment crushed, dashboard and steering column pushed into the dummy's lap, airbag snapped the dummy's head back.
- Quote: Joe Nolan, IIHS COO — "These results highlight the role of IIHS ratings in driving many of the key vehicle safety improvements of the past 30 years."
- Method: compared real-world fatality rates of vehicles rated Good vs Acceptable/Marginal/Poor across five evaluations (moderate overlap front, driver-side small overlap, passenger-side small overlap, side, roof strength); computed counterfactual deaths if the Good-rated share had never risen.
- **Most lives saved came from the moderate overlap test** — the oldest test in the program (introduced 1995).

## Kill test
- Newsworthy? Yes: fresh June 2026 IIHS study, first self-quantification of the program's impact in 30 years, zero coverage angle like ours in queue.
- Novel angle? Every outlet ran the celebration ("50,000 lives saved!"). Nobody ran the ROI math or the "the oldest test did the most saving" inversion. Proceed.

## Original contributions (the part nobody else ran)
1. **ROI:** $538B societal value / ~$600M program cost = roughly **900-to-1 return**. Per averted death: ~$600M / 48,352 = **~$12,400 in program spending per life saved**.
2. **Pace:** 48,352 lives / 26 years = **~1,860 lives per year** — roughly 4-5% of annual US traffic deaths, every year, for a generation.
3. **The first test did the most work:** the moderate overlap front test (the program's original 1995 evaluation) accounts for most of the saved lives. The newer, headline-grabbing tests (small overlap, updated side) contributed less in absolute terms — because the first fix was the biggest leak.
4. **Scale check:** 48,352 averted deaths vs roughly one million US traffic deaths 1999-2024 — the program clawed back about **1 in 20** of the period's deaths. Enormous for a private lab; sobering against the total.

## Limitations (dedicated, honest)
- Counterfactual estimates, not counted bodies. The method assumes the Good-vs-worse fatality gap is caused by test-driven design changes — but the same decades brought the ESC mandate (FMVSS 126), side-curtain airbags, better trauma care, and seatbelt use rising from ~70% to ~92%. Some of the "saved" lives belong to those forces.
- The dollar figure values each life at the statistical value of life (~$11M+); it is an economic construct, not money anyone received.
- Window is 1999-2024: excludes the program's first four years and everything since.
- IIHS graded its own homework. The funding (~$600M from auto insurers) is disclosed, and insurers profit when crashes cost less — the incentive alignment is the story, but it is still self-reported.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
"The 48,352 number is IIHS taking credit for a rising tide. Fatality rates fell for dozens of reasons — ESC, airbags, trauma surgery, drunk-driving enforcement, an aging-into-safer-drivers population. Comparing Good-rated cars to Poor-rated cars in the real world doesn't isolate the test program; it isolates 'newer, more expensive, better-maintained cars bought by richer, safer drivers.' The lab is claiming the harvest from a field it only partly planted."
Rebuttal (kept proportional): the Good-vs-non-Good comparison is within-era and within-vehicle-class, which controls for a lot of the tide. And the Blazer demo is a physical fact, not a model: the same nameplate, the same test, 30 years apart, and the old one's cabin collapses. But the confounding critique is partly right, and the article says so.

## Actionable takeaways
- Shopping used? Pull the IIHS rating history at iihs.org/ratings — and check the *test date*, not just the word "Good." A 2010 Good predates the small-overlap test entirely.
- The cheapest safety upgrade in the used market is a small-overlap Good rating (test introduced 2012): it separates structures engineered this decade from last decade's.
- The $600M figure is the pitch for every safety program ever: testing is cheap, funerals are expensive.
