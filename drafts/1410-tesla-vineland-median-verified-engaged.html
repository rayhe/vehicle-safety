Police Said the Driver Lost Control. Tesla's Filing Says the Driver-Assist Was Engaged.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

On Saturday, August 22, a 2021 Tesla Model Y left the southbound lanes of Route 55 in Vineland, New Jersey, crossed the grass median, hit a tree, and slid into oncoming traffic. It struck a Chevrolet SUV head-on. Phillip Eastman, 68, a newly sworn-in school board member and a youth hockey coach for nearly three decades, was killed in the SUV. Police told the public the Tesla driver lost control. Tesla's own report to federal regulators says the car's driver-assist system was "Verified Engaged." No news report mentioned it.[1]

Matching the two records takes about a minute, once you know where to look. Tesla logged the incident at 12:29 UTC, about 8:29 a.m. local time, within two minutes of the 8:31 a.m. time the New Jersey State Police reported. Same town, same vehicle, same fatality, same month. Electrek had flagged this crash weeks earlier as a fatal Tesla wreck with no corresponding entry in the federal data at all; the latest NHTSA release added it, flagged as a fatality with the system verified engaged. What Tesla filed is thin in a specific way. It says the company learned of the crash from the media. It logs a pre-crash speed of 37 mph, low for a freeway, which reads like a single telematics snapshot rather than the speed the car left the road. It claims only the car's telematics, not the event-data recorder or the video. And it redacts the three fields that would explain what the car did: the crash narrative, the software version, and whether the road was within the system's approved operating area.[1][2]

Vineland is the ninth confirmed case in Electrek's series, the series this site covered as #905. Nine sounds small until you see the denominator it sits on. Under a 2021 Standing General Order, Tesla accounts for roughly 85 percent of the entire industry's driver-assist crash reports, nearly 4,000 of them. July 2026 alone produced 236 Tesla reports, the most in any month, including four fatal crashes. Meanwhile NHTSA has been investigating Tesla's crash reporting since August 2025, after the company submitted reports late in a batch. So the pattern is not one missing record. It is a system that reports crashes to the government, blacks out the explanation, and leaves the public with the police version, which in Vineland never contained the robot at all.[2][3]

Cross-referencing the Model Y against a decade of FARS data sharpens the question instead of softening it. In the repo dataset, the Model Y carries an estimated fatality rate of 0.03 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled across 57 deaths from 2014 through 2023, the lowest rate of any Tesla model and roughly one two-hundredth of the Mustang's 6.02. By the federal government's own numbers, this is one of the safest cars per mile in America. A car that safe still crossed a median with its driver-assist on, and the only record of what the system did in those seconds is sealed. Rates built on estimated miles are soft, and the Model Y fleet is young, so read the ranking, not the point estimate. Even so, nothing about this crash is explained by the car being dangerous. Everything turns on what the software did, and that is exactly the part nobody outside Tesla can see.[4]

Now the honest objection, stated at full strength. "Verified Engaged" means the system was on within 30 seconds of impact. That is the reporting trigger, not proof the system was steering when the car crossed the median. A driver can override it, fall asleep, suffer a medical episode, or panic. Nine confirmed cases are nine anecdotes selected precisely because they were matchable, drawn from nearly 4,000 reports. And the redactions are a confidentiality process any manufacturer can use. All true. But the claim here is not that the software caused this crash; nobody outside Tesla can establish that from a redacted filing. The claim is narrower: the public was told a story with the robot edited out, while the complete record sits in a sealed federal file, and the company under investigation for its reporting decides what counts as a trade secret. That gap does not depend on resolving causation. It is the story.[1]

What to do with that gap is mostly unglamorous. If you drive a Tesla, remember that "Full Self-Driving" is Level 2 driver assistance, not self-driving, and supervise it like a student driver. If you are ever in a crash involving a Tesla, ask the investigating agency whether it requested the car's raw telemetry or only Tesla's summary, because that is the question to ask everywhere. And if you want to see the federal record yourself, NHTSA publishes the Standing General Order crash data as CSV files, and the engagement field, the vehicle, the month, and the pre-crash speed are not redacted. What the car did in the seconds that matter is. Until that changes, these crashes will keep arriving in two versions, and the public will keep getting the one that leaves Tesla out of it.[5]
