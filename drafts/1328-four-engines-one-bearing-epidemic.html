Four Automakers, Four Engines, One Failed Part

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 30, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a bearing seizing. The crankshaft is spinning at 2,000 rpm when the thin metal shell it rides on welds itself to the journal. The connecting rod snaps. It punches through the engine block. The engine stops, and at highway speed you have just lost motive power, power steering, and power brakes. That is the engineering event behind today’s deadline: NHTSA’s six-question demand to GM about its failing 6.2-liter V8s is due today, October 30, and roughly 3.4 million vehicles now sit inside four separate NHTSA engine programs that all fail the same way.[1]

3.4 million
Vehicles inside the four engine programs’ stated populations, per NHTSA records. The component that fails is the same in all four: the bearing.

Here is the census that nobody else has run, and it starts with GM. The 6.2L L87 V8 is under Engineering Analysis EA26005, covering 997,743 trucks and SUVs from 2021 to 2026, after owners reported engines failing after the original recall repair. GM’s own count of post-remedy complaints is 6,953.[2] Toyota’s twin-turbo 3.4L V35A has been recalled three separate times (24V-381, 25V-767, 26V-320), roughly 272,000 Tundras and Lexuses, because machining debris left at the factory migrates to the number-one main bearing.[3] Nissan’s VC-Turbo family carries two bearing recalls, 25V-437 (443,899 vehicles) and 26V-080 (323,917), with overlapping populations Nissan says cannot be summed.[4] And Honda’s 3.5L V6 sits under Preliminary Evaluation PE25008: 1,410,806 vehicles, 3,012 reports of connecting-rod bearing failure, fourteen months in, no wider recall.[5]

The physics is identical every time. A bearing is a thin shell, often less than a millimeter thick, that keeps the crankshaft and connecting rods spinning on a film of oil a few microns deep, and when it goes, it goes without negotiation. What differs is the federal posture, and the ladder runs in every direction at once. Toyota has issued three recalls and replaced whole engines. Nissan issued two bearing recalls and then doubled the long-block warranty to ten years and 120,000 miles. GM issued one recall and its fix is now under a federal Engineering Analysis whose deadline is today. Honda issued one recall and has spent fourteen months under a Preliminary Evaluation that has produced nothing, which leaves four different versions of accountability for the same component.[6]

Now the part I owe you, because a forensic editor who only tells you the pattern is selling you a horoscope: four engine programs, four different root causes. GM blames a supplier manufacturing and quality problem, while Toyota simply left metal chips in the block. Nissan had a bearing manufacturing defect plus an oil-temperature problem that degraded lubrication. Honda ground its crank pins into the wrong shape and starved the bearings of oil. These are not the same defect, and a bearing is also the most mechanically stressed component in any engine, which means bearing seizure is the default way a modern engine dies catastrophically. Of course the terminal component matches in all four. That is what engine failure means, mechanically. It is entirely possible this is coincidence in a trench coat, and that the real story is simply that downsized, turbocharged engines running hotter cylinder pressures and tighter tolerances are less forgiving of manufacturing sloppiness than the lazy V8s they replaced. I cannot rule that out, and nobody can yet.

What I can tell you is what to do. If you drive a 2021–2026 Silverado, Sierra, Tahoe, Suburban, Yukon, or Escalade with the 6.2L, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 25V-274 and watch what comes out of today’s EA26005 deadline.[7] If you own a 2022–2024 Tundra, LX, or GX with the twin-turbo V6, there are three campaigns on your engine (24V-381, 25V-767, 26V-320), and the newest wave’s remedy was still under development when Toyota filed it. Rogue owners: 25V-437 and 26V-080, and your long block is now warranted to 120 months or 120,000 miles. Honda and Acura owners with the 3.5L V6 from 2016–2020 (Pilot, MDX, Odyssey, TLX, Ridgeline): campaign 23V-751, and the open PE25008 population has no fix to check for.[8]

The warning signs are the same across all four, and Nissan’s own filings admit the failures announce themselves: a metallic knock or rattle that was not there before, especially at cold start, a check-engine light, rough running, or metal in the oil. If your engine starts making a sound it has never made, stop driving it and have it towed. I have spent my career being judgmental about bad vehicle design, but here the design is not the problem. A bearing is a solved technology; we have known how to make them since the 19th century. Four automakers are failing at it in the same decade, and that should bother everyone who signs off on a build sheet.
