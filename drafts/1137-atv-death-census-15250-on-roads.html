By The Numbers

15,250 ATV Deaths, and Most of Last Year’s Were on Pavement

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

I ran the numbers on the federal government’s ATV death census. Then I ran them against the road-type breakdown. They tell two different stories about where “off-road” vehicles actually kill people.

Since the early 1980s, the Consumer Product Safety Commission has kept a running count of Americans killed on all-terrain vehicles, assembled from death certificates, coroner reports, and news clippings. Last month, reporting by FairWarning put the cumulative total above 15,250 dead, and the agency itself calls that an undercount because counting is still incomplete for recent years.[1] In recent years the annual toll has mostly run 550 to 650, occasionally topping 800, with more than 100,000 emergency-room injuries in most years.

Share of documented 2025 ATV deaths that happened on roads, versus 23% off-road (Consumer Federation of America)

For the freshest full-year breakdown, I used the Consumer Federation of America’s 2025 fatality tables, which document 331 ATV deaths from news reports filed between January 1 and December 31, 2025.[2] The road-type split is the whole story: 209 deaths on roads, 75 off-road, 47 unknown. That is a 2.8-to-1 ratio in favor of pavement. Sixty-nine percent were single-vehicle crashes, and nearly half of those involved a rollover. Of the 109 rollover deaths, 65 happened on roads and 40 off them. Another 52 riders died in collisions with on-road vehicles, which is what happens when a 900-pound quad with low-pressure tires and a high center of gravity meets a Camry at an intersection.

Manufacturers, the CPSC, and every safety group involved all say the same thing: these machines are for trails, not pavement. Small-town America is doing the opposite. Officials in places like Cerro Gordo County, Iowa, have opened public roadways to off-road vehicles, in some cases explicitly to attract tourist dollars, and FairWarning reports that manufacturers publicly oppose the loosening while funding rider clubs that lobby local governments for it.[1] Studies linked by IIHS show more than half of ATV deaths occur on private or public roads. The policy trend points directly at the surface where the bodies pile up.

Kids absorb a share of this that should end the debate about whether the machines are the problem. In 2025, 58 of the 331 dead, 18 percent, were under 16, and 27 of those children were driving.[2] Across the full 15,250-death census, more than one in five victims were under 16, which works out to over 3,000 children since the survey began. In April, the CPSC recalled 4,900 Rex110 and Sierra110 youth ATVs after a 6-year-old died in 2025; the machines exceeded youth speed limits, the throttle could stick open, and the footwells could burn riders.[4] Wisconsin, which leads the nation, had 30 ATV and UTV deaths through August 8 of this year, with at least 26 of the 30 unhelmeted, and the state projects 42 for 2026, its second-highest total ever.[3]

Now the part that makes the 15,250 figure look optimistic. The census covers ATVs only. It excludes recreational off-highway vehicles, the side-by-side machines with bench seats and roll cages, even though ROVs now outsell ATVs according to manufacturer reports. Last October, CPSC commissioners voted 3-2 against expanding the death surveys to include ROV casualties, with the deciding vote calling it a drain on staff time.[1] The consumer federation, working from news reports alone, is aware of 646 ROV deaths since 2013. The official body count has a blind spot, and the blind spot is the segment that is growing.

Fairness requires the strongest version of the other side. Most of these deaths are behavioral, not mechanical: no helmet, alcohol, children on adult-size machines, passengers on single-rider ATVs. ATVs are farm equipment and rural transport, not just toys, and nobody measures hours ridden, so no honest per-mile comparison to cars exists. The CPSC commissioner who oversees this docket is himself an ATV owner with two young riders. Towns opening roads argue it moves riding onto supervised routes and keeps dying main streets alive. All of that can be true while the road-type split stays damning.

Limitations, plainly: the federation’s 331 is assembled from news reports and captures roughly half of what the CPSC’s death-certificate census finds, so treat every count here as a floor. Helmet use was unknown in 82 percent of 2025 cases and road type in 14 percent. ROV deaths are excluded from both datasets. Without exposure data, these are counts and shares, not risk rates.

If you ride: stay off pavement, full stop, because the tires and the physics agree with the data. Put kids only on age-rated youth machines, never adult ATVs, and check whether yours is in the April recall. One rider per machine. Helmet every time; 87 percent of Wisconsin’s 30 dead this year were unhelmeted. Take the hands-on safety course before the first ride, not after the first scare. And if your county is voting on an OHV road ordinance, bring the 209-to-75 split to the meeting.
