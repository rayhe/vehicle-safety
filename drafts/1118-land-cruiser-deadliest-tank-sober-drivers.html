Toyota's Heaviest SUV Is Also Its Deadliest, and Its Drivers Are Sober.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. The Toyota Land Cruiser weighs 5,700 pounds, rides on a body-on-frame chassis engineered for war zones, and carries a reputation as the last indestructible vehicle on sale in America. It also has the highest fatality rate of any large SUV in the country: 6.27 deaths per 100 million miles, seven and a half times the Toyota Sequoia, its own stablemate.[1] Somewhere between the mud flaps and the mystique, the tank became the thing you need armor against.

7.5x
How much deadlier the Land Cruiser is than the Toyota Sequoia, its body-on-frame stablemate

Run the lineup and the anomaly sharpens. Consider the scores: the Chevrolet Tahoe lands at 2.49, the Ford Expedition at 2.31, the Chevrolet Suburban at 1.36, and the Jeep Wrangler at 0.84, all clustered within the same order of safety while the Land Cruiser lives several orders away. The Sequoia, a three-row Toyota built on the same truck architecture by the same company for the same country, sits at 0.83.[1] Only three vehicles in the entire dataset score worse, the Veloster, the Tracker, and the Mustang, and all three are small, fast, and cheap, which is the usual way of dying in America. The Land Cruiser is the only entry on that end of the table that requires a step ladder.

What makes it strange is who is dying. Across 417 Land Cruiser drivers in the toxicology subset, just 8.9 percent tested positive for alcohol or drugs. For comparison, the Corvette sits at 26.2 percent and the Buick Park Avenue at 31.7 percent, because apparently even grandpa's sedan has a wilder night out.[1] The usual excuse for a deadly vehicle, that its drivers are maniacs, does not survive contact with this data. Land Cruiser drivers are sober, and they are dying anyway. Death, it turns out, does not require intoxication, merely physics.

Physics, specifically, is the tall part, because IIHS has shown for decades that mass protects you when two vehicles collide; the Land Cruiser's numbers suggest mass is a liability when the collision is with a ditch, a tree, or your own center of gravity.[2] A narrow-track, body-on-frame SUV with the ride height of a watchtower is a rollover waiting for a sharp input, and the fatal crashes skew toward older model years (2001 through 2013), vehicles built before stability control calibrations caught up with the laws of leverage.[1] These trucks also go where pavement ends, and off-pavement miles happen on unlit, undivided, unwitnessed roads where every crash is automatically more lethal. Weight wins the fender bender and loses the cliff.

Now the honest counterargument, stated at full strength: some of this could be the denominator lying. The Land Cruiser fleet in the data is 43,750 vehicles, tiny next to the Tahoe's 831,250, and the rate is built on VMT estimates, not odometer readings, with uncertainty that bites hardest at low volume. If Land Cruisers spend weekends on trails and ranches that never show up in VMT estimates, the miles are undercounted and the rate inflates. But exposure mixing explains a fraction of a 7.5-to-1 gap, not the gap itself. Suburbs for the Sequoia, the apocalypse for the Land Cruiser, and exposure alone cannot explain the rest of the gap.

What you should do: If you own a 100- or 200-series Land Cruiser, respect the center of gravity more than the marketing. Slow down on gravel transitions, keep roof loads minimal, and treat every off-camber dirt road like the rollover laboratory it is. If you are shopping for a large used SUV and survival is the goal, the Sequoia's 0.83 rate is the buy and the Land Cruiser is the legend. Legends, the data reminds us, are mostly about people who did not come back.
