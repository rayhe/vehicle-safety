NHTSA Banned These Airbags in April. One Killed a Driver in August Anyway.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 6, 2026

Before you sign for that used Equinox with the clean Carfax, you might want to ask who replaced the airbag. On August 27, a driver was killed in Dallas in a 2018 Chevrolet Equinox in what federal investigators call an “otherwise survivable crash,” because the replacement airbag inflator in the steering wheel did not inflate so much as detonate, firing metal fragments into the driver compartment.[1] That was the eleventh American killed by these inflators. It was the first death since NHTSA banned them in April. The ban did nothing to stop it, because the ban was never aimed at the cars that already have them.

120
Days from NHTSA’s finalized April ban to the August 27 death in Dallas. The inflator was installed before the ban, and no ban can reach it now.

Run the timeline and watch the system do everything right while the body count climbs anyway. In January, NHTSA issued an urgent warning to owners and repair shops. On April 2, the agency issued an initial decision that inflators made by Jilin Province Detiannuo Safety Technology Co., marked DTN60DB and manufactured in 2021 and 2022, contain a safety defect, and on April 29 it finalized the action, the first forced recall order in twenty years.[3][5] Thirteen incidents have been reported since May 2023.[1] The inflator that killed the Dallas driver was built years ago, imported by unknown smugglers, and installed after some earlier crash by some shop that picked the $30 part over the $300 factory module, and it sat in that Equinox through every warning, every headline, and four months of the ban itself, waiting for the one crash that would ask it to work.

Then there is the matter of the car. The first ten deaths happened in Chevrolet Malibus and Hyundai Sonatas, the rebuilt-title sedans where a cheap replacement airbag is practically standard equipment, and it was easy to tell yourself this was somebody else’s problem.[5] The Equinox ends that. It is a mainstream family SUV, not a rebuilt-title special, and NHTSA said back in April that it had no information indicating the problem was limited to the known models.[3] Now it has information that it is not limited, and the at-risk population just got enormously bigger in a way nobody can measure, because NHTSA openly admits it does not know how many of these inflators were imported or where they went. There are zero VINs in the recall file. The entire recall apparatus runs on lists, and this defect has no list.

As for the manufacturer, DTN responded to the agency that it “cannot be proven” the inflators were made by the company, nor that defective inflators caused the accidents, and added that it never directly sold them in the United States.[1] The company’s initials are etched on the inflator cap. Draw your own conclusions. With the importer unknown and the manufacturer in China denying everything, two states have started doing the job the feds cannot: Wisconsin’s governor signed a bill in March making it a felony to knowingly install a non-genuine airbag when someone is hurt or killed, and Idaho signed its own version the same month.[4] You cannot extradite a supply chain, so punish the installer.

Now the strongest case for the other side, stated at full strength, because anger without honesty is just noise. Eleven deaths across thirteen incidents in three years is a small toll beside forty thousand annual traffic deaths, and NHTSA genuinely did everything available to it: urgent warning, investigation, the first forced action in two decades, and state laws now trailing behind. Nobody at the agency sat on this. But that is precisely the point, and it is a frightening one. Every tool in the federal kit, the VIN lookup, the import ban, the public warning, assumes a traceable supply chain, and this defect lives entirely outside one. Doing everything right still produced an eleventh body. When the system works perfectly and someone dies anyway, the system has a hole shaped exactly like this.

On practical matters, because this column exists to tell you what to do. NHTSA’s guidance is specific: if your vehicle was in a crash with an airbag deployment since 2020 and the repair was not done at a manufacturer dealership, have the airbag inspected by a reputable shop.[1] Ask the technician to check the inflator marking. The suspect units are etched DTN60DB on the cap face, and the installed module’s connector label carries a barcode reading 666631 or 144415654 666633.[4] Buying used? Demand the repair documentation for any prior airbag work, and walk away from a seller who cannot produce it. A $30 inflator turns the device meant to save your life into the thing that ends it, and no federal ban, however historic, can reach into a steering wheel that was closed up years ago.
