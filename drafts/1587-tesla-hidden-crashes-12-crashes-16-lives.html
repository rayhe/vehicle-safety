The Hidden Death Count Doubled in Nine Weeks. Tesla's Own Filings Say 12 Crashes, 16 Lives.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 25, 2026

On September 10 this site published a file with eight names in it. Eight fatal Tesla crashes, matched one by one from the company's own redacted federal reports to the ordinary accidents the public was told about, and in every case the automation was on and the public record said nothing. Nine weeks later the file holds twelve crashes and sixteen lives. Half the death toll arrived in the update.[1][5]

8 of 16
Lives in Electrek's confirmed list that come from the four cases added since September 10

Start with the worst one, because it is also the oldest. Just before 4 a.m. on May 31, a box truck flipped on southbound I-75 near Naples, Florida, and came to rest blocking two lanes. Jonathan Garcia Arias, 25, a former Marine driving a BMW, pulled over and got out to help the truck driver. A 2023 Model 3 carrying five young men from Naples came up on the scene and drove into the underside of the overturned truck, killing two passengers aged 18 and 19 at the scene. Garcia Arias died later in the hospital. Police and local media covered it as a truck wreck in the dark. Nobody said a word about Autopilot.[3]

Tesla's own report to the federal government says the system was verified engaged at 71 mph. Read that against the trooper's own words. Kenn Watson of the Florida Highway Patrol, on what the Tesla driver could see: "No lights, no reflections, nothing to warn them." Garcia Arias, a human being in a BMW, saw the obstacle and stopped. And this is the third time this exact geometry has killed someone with Autopilot on. Joshua Brown died driving under a truck in 2016. Jeremy Banner died the same way in 2019. Ten years, same crash, same sentence in the police report, same blank space where the software version should be.[3]

Then there is the filing itself. Tesla filed the I-75 crash in the urgent category reserved for fatal crashes, and the report did not land until the September filings, more than three months after the crash. The rule is measured in days. Tesla took months. NHTSA already opened an Audit Query in August 2025 into Tesla's late crash reporting, and Tesla blamed an error in data collection. Some error. It keeps happening to fatal crashes.[3][7]

Second new case. Late on Saturday, September 12, a 2024 Model Y with four people inside left Serrano Parkway in El Dorado Hills, California, hit a tree, and caught fire. Charles Anthony Cameron, 30, was thrown from the car and died at the scene. Alejandra Mendoza, 28, and Kailauna Viola Mills, 28, a bride-to-be visiting from Arizona, were found dead inside after firefighters put out the fire. A fourth woman survived with major injuries. CHP called it a single-car crash for unknown reasons and said nothing about Autopilot. Tesla's report says the system was verified engaged at 74 mph in a residential neighborhood, lists the roadway type as "Unknown" and the object struck as "Unknown," and says Tesla learned of the crash from the media. Police said it was a tree. Tesla's own form says it does not know what the car hit.[4]

Third new case, and the cleanest example of how much Tesla knows and how little it shares. At 11:43 p.m. on September 30, a 2025 Model X left Golden Gate Parkway in Naples, hit a traffic signal pole, and rolled over. A 50-year-old woman from Fort Myers, riding as a passenger, died at the scene. Her driver survived. Tesla flagged the crash itself through its own telematics, and its report confirms it holds the event data recorder, the telematics, and the video. Then it redacted the crash narrative, the software version, and whether the road was inside the system's operating domain, all as confidential business information. Tesla knows whether the system steered the SUV off the road or the driver did. It filed that knowledge under a black bar.[2]

One more filing defect, because the pile is the point. Tesla logged the Naples pole crash at 03:41 UTC on October 1, which is 11:41 p.m. in Florida on September 30. So the federal record lists a September death under October, and anyone checking September's numbers never finds it. A database that cannot place a fatal crash in the right month is a database that cannot be trusted to count.[2]

Fourth case, and the one that should worry you most. A 2021 Model Y crossed the Route 55 median in Vineland, New Jersey, hit a tree, and slid into oncoming traffic, killing 68-year-old Phillip Eastman, a school board member and youth hockey coach, in his SUV. Police said only that the Tesla's driver lost control. Electrek flagged the crash from the news as missing from the federal data, then watched it surface in the next NHTSA release, confirmed fatal, system engaged. A fatal crash with the automation on was absent from the record until a journalist noticed it was missing. Twelve is a floor. It is not a census.[1]

Now the scale, because the count is small and the pattern is not. September 2026 set a record with 264 Tesla driver-assist crashes reported in a single month, beating July's 236. Tesla accounts for roughly 85 percent of the entire industry's reports under the Standing General Order, nearly 4,000 crashes and climbing every year. On 99.9 percent of its reports, Tesla blacks out the narrative, the software version, and the operating domain. GM, Ford, Honda, and Toyota redact essentially none of theirs. Tesla is the only high-volume reporter that hides everything, and it does so while selling a product called Full Self-Driving and promising unsupervised autonomy is months away.[1][6]

Here is the honest counterweight, and it is real. "Verified engaged" means the system was on within 30 seconds of impact. It does not prove the system caused any of these crashes. An overturned truck with no lights in the dark is a hazard a human driver might have hit too. El Dorado Hills is still under investigation, and speed, impairment, and who was driving have not been released. Tesla reports more crashes than anyone because it has more driver-assist miles than anyone, and because its cars phone home. GM's SuperCruise has 55 reports because almost nobody drives a SuperCruise. Redactions are legal under NHTSA's own rules, and Tesla's competitors could claim them too.[5][6]

Fair. Now the other side of that scale. A five-day urgent filing that takes three months is not a paperwork quirk. A report that lists the struck object as "Unknown" when the police report says "tree" is a company that did not read its own file. And "the human is responsible" is a strange defense from a company whose product is named Full Self-Driving, whose chief executive promises unsupervised autonomy is months away, and which withholds the video that would settle whether the human or the machine made the fatal move. When the data helped Tesla, in the fatal Katy, Texas crash, it shared the data. When it does not help, the report is sealed. A company confident in its technology would not need to hide what its cars did when things go wrong while broadcasting it when they go right.[1]

What to do with this. If you drive a Tesla with Autopilot or FSD, remember it is Level 2, whatever the name on the screen says. Hands on the wheel, eyes on the road, and never trust it around a stopped vehicle in a live lane, an overturned truck, or a road departure at night. All three new cases are scenarios where the system was on and the car still left the road or drove into something stopped. If you are investigating a Tesla crash, or you lost someone in one, the police report is not the whole record. Tesla files a separate report with NHTSA, and the engagement status in that report is the fact the public record usually omits. Ask the investigating agency whether it received Tesla's raw telemetry or only Tesla's interpretation of it. And keep the running list bookmarked, because it keeps growing. Eight in September. Twelve in November. Sixteen lives, and the database still has holes in it.[1][6]
