The Deadliest Car in America Is One You Can't Buy Anymore

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 18, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Out of 337 models in the dataset, the deadliest is one the manufacturer already gave up on. I split ten years of federal crash data into two piles, nameplates you can still buy and nameplates you can't, and the dead ones are killing at a median rate 47 percent higher than the living.[1] We are being murdered by ghosts, and the ghosts are mostly sedans.

8.54
Hyundai Veloster deaths per 100M vehicle-miles, worst of 337 models. Hyundai killed the Veloster after 2022.

That roster of the damned reads like a clearance sale. Veloster at 8.54, with 598 deaths on a fleet of just 87,500. Chevrolet Tracker at 7.83. Nissan Maxima at 5.11, Chevrolet Cobalt at 5.10, Chevrolet Impala at 5.00 with 3,774 deaths, the highest body count of any discontinued nameplate. Eight of the ten worst fatality rates in the entire dataset belong to cars nobody sells anymore. Only the Mustang is still breathing on that list, and it earned its spot the honest way.

No nameplate deserves a moment of silence more than the Impala, then some math. GM built the final one in Detroit-Hamtramck on February 27, 2020, because sedans stopped paying rent, not because they stopped killing people.[4] Six years later the Impala fleet is still grinding out miles in the cheapest corners of the used market, and every one of those miles happens in a car whose safety tech froze somewhere around the Obama administration. IIHS found that among drivers 70 and over, vehicles without electronic stability control were associated with 37 percent higher odds of a driver fatality, and vehicles without standard head-protecting side airbags were associated with double those odds.[2] The Institute also found that two-thirds of teenagers killed in crashes were driving cars between 6 and 15 years old, logging more than half their miles in vehicles older than eleven.[3] A discontinued car does not rust faster. It just ends up in the hands least able to fix what rusts.

Now the part where I argue with myself, because someone has to. Discontinuation is not a cause of death; it is a concentration of risk. Most of that rate comes from old ninth-generation fleet and rental examples, not the well-reviewed tenth generation. Small fleets like the Tracker's and Seville's carry canyon-wide uncertainty. And here is the real problem for my thesis: the Dodge Grand Caravan, also dead, posts a 0.23 rate, one of the best in the dataset. Not all zombies are killers. Composition is doing the work here: dead nameplates concentrate aging, high-mileage, deferred-maintenance examples driven by the youngest and poorest drivers, and that concentration is the entire point. You cannot buy your way out of a used-car fleet that skews old. You can only maintain the one you are stuck with.

What I did not prove: the fleet estimates behind these rates are modeled from sales and travel-survey data, not odometer readings, so treat small-fleet rates as approximate. FARS captures fatal crashes only; a kind rate here can hide an unkind injury rate. And the dead-versus-alive line is my judgment call on a handful of borderline models, though moving the border cases around does not change the 47 percent gap by much.

Actionable, since dread without advice is just weather. Shopping used? A dead-nameplate sedan is the statistically worst seat in the used market; cross-shop this site's rate table before you sign. Already driving an Impala, Focus, Cobalt, Maxima, or Veloster? Tires and brakes are the entire game on an aging fleet, and check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Buying a teenager their first car? One IIHS rule survives every dataset: newer and larger beats older and smaller, even when the older car is a bargain. That bargain is priced the way it is for a reason, and now you know the reason.
