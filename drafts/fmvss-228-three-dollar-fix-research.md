# Research Notes: The $3 Fix NHTSA Won't Finalize

## Angle
FMVSS 228 (Pedestrian Head Protection) is the cheapest safety regulation NHTSA has ever proposed: $3/vehicle to save 67 lives/year. The comment period closed December 2024. It's August 2026 — nearly 2 years since the NPRM — and the rule remains a proposal. Original contribution: calculating the regulatory delay body count and the benefit-cost ratio.

## Journalist: Mia Crumplezone (Safety Engineering Editor)

## Primary Sources

### 1. NHTSA FMVSS 228 NPRM (September 19, 2024)
- Federal Register publication: Notice of Proposed Rulemaking
- Adapts UN Global Technical Regulation No. 9 for US market
- Head Injury Criterion (HIC) must not exceed 1,000 over 2/3 of hood surface
- Applies to vehicles under 10,000 lb GVWR
- Child and adult headform impact tests
- Comment period closed ~December 18, 2024
- Cost: ~$3/vehicle
- Projected savings: 67 pedestrian lives/year
- Source: NHTSA, Federal Register (https://www.govinfo.gov)

### 2. NHTSA Value of Statistical Life (VSL)
- NHTSA's current VSL: approximately $13.2 million (2024 update)
- Used for regulatory cost-benefit analysis
- Benefit per year: 67 lives × $13.2M = $884.4M
- Cost per year: ~17M new vehicles × $3 = $51M
- Benefit-cost ratio: 17.3:1

### 3. The Delay Math
- NPRM published: September 19, 2024
- Comment period closed: December 18, 2024
- Current date: August 2, 2026
- Time since NPRM: ~23 months
- Time since comment period closed: ~19 months
- Estimated deaths during delay: 67/year × 1.6 years post-comment = ~107 people
- (Conservative: rule would need lead time even if finalized immediately)

### 4. Hood Height & Blind Zone Data (Carscoops/NYT, June 2026)
- Analysis by Forensic Rock crash testing firm
- Sedan hood: ~30 inches from ground (strikes below waist)
- Pickup hood: ~45 inches from ground (strikes at chest level)
- Average new vehicle hood: ~36 inches (~3 feet) → floors anyone under 5'6"
- Silverado blind zone: nearly doubled over past 2 decades
- Sierra & Tacoma blind zones: ~60% increase
- F-150 blind zone: ~25% increase
- ~3,000 pedestrian deaths linked to taller hoods (2016-2024)
- 200-400 additional pedestrian deaths/year from hood height
- Source: Carscoops (https://www.carscoops.com/2026/06/truck-height-pedestrian-deaths/)

### 5. IIHS Data Points
- 2026 TSP+ awards: only 2 large pickups qualified (Cybertruck, Tundra)
- No minicars, minivans, or small pickups earned awards
- New 2026 requirement: vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention at 31/37/43 mph
- IIHS: "There's no functional benefit to these massive, blocky fronts"
- Pedestrian deaths rose 75% since 2009 (57% increase 2013-2022 per NHTSA)
- Source: IIHS.org

### 6. FARS Data (our dataset, 2014-2023)
- Top 4 pickups by total deaths: Silverado (9,591), F-150 (9,194), Ram (4,407), Sierra (3,337)
- Combined pickup class: 40,122 deaths over 10 years
- Pickup crash lethality: 48.5% (lowest of any class)
- These vehicles protect OCCUPANTS well but our data doesn't capture pedestrians they kill
- Fleet sizes: Silverado 5.7M, F-150 6.6M, Ram 4.2M
- Combined pickup fleet: ~26M vehicles

### 7. Regulatory Context
- Europe/Japan have run pedestrian head-impact testing for years (UN GTR No. 9)
- Euro NCAP has scored pedestrian impact for decades
- US had NO pedestrian crashworthiness standard until this proposal
- The 2009 FMVSS 216 roof crush update (3x vehicle weight) led to thicker A-pillars → wider blind zones → more pedestrian deaths
- NHTSA projected 67 deaths saved/year; independent estimates suggest 200-400/year from hood height alone

### 8. Comparative Regulatory Costs
- ABS mandate (1999): ~$235/vehicle
- ESC mandate (2011): ~$111/vehicle  
- Backup cameras (2018): ~$132-$142/vehicle
- FMVSS 228 pedestrian protection: ~$3/vehicle
- FMVSS 228 is roughly 37x cheaper than the cheapest previous mandate

## Kill Test
Is this genuinely surprising after 670+ articles?
YES — we've covered hood height, pedestrian deaths, blind zones, and pickup safety from many angles, but never the REGULATORY ECONOMICS of FMVSS 228: the $3 cost, the 17:1 benefit-cost ratio, and the ~107 deaths during the regulatory delay. This is an original cost-per-life calculation nobody has published.

## Strongest Counterargument
The rule hasn't been finalized partly because NHTSA must balance comment responses, potential legal challenges from automakers, and implementation timelines. The $3/vehicle cost may be understated — redesigning hood geometry for some trucks could require more significant engineering changes. Additionally, the 67 lives/year estimate may be conservative or optimistic depending on fleet turnover rates.

## Limitations
- The $3/vehicle figure is NHTSA's estimate; actual costs may vary by vehicle type
- 67 lives/year assumes all new vehicles meet the standard simultaneously (fleet turnover takes 15+ years)
- Our FARS data captures occupant deaths, not the pedestrian deaths this rule targets
- We don't know the current status of NHTSA's rulemaking — it could be in final rule drafting
- The "delay body count" is theoretical — it assumes no automaker voluntarily meets the standard
