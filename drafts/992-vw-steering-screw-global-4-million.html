The Screw That Recalled 208,724 Cars in America Just Recalled 4 Million Worldwide

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

On September 18, Volkswagen told NHTSA it was recalling 208,724 American vehicles over a steering rack bolt that could corrode and snap. Eight days later, Germany's motor vehicle authority published a slightly larger number: roughly 4 million vehicles across four brands. That American filing, it turns out, covered about five percent of the problem.

19.2×
The global recall is nineteen times the size of the US NHTSA filing: ~4 million vehicles vs 208,724

Start with the paperwork: AP reports Volkswagen is recalling 2.86 million VW and Audi vehicles as "a precautionary measure over a screw whose failure could cause a loss of steering." Reuters, citing Germany's KBA authority and Handelsblatt, puts the real total near 4 million: 2.16 million VWs, 700,000 Audis, over a million Skodas, and 200,000 Seats. The affected nameplates run the full VW family sedan-to-minivan catalog: Tiguan, Touran, Golf, Golf Variant, Caddy, built from September 2013 to July 2024, plus Audi Q3s from October 2017 to May 2024. In Germany alone, 895,832 VWs and 58,555 Audi Q3s carry the defective screw. That is the largest Volkswagen recall since Dieselgate, announced with a straight face and zero documented casualties.

Zero. Germany's KBA states plainly that there have been no known incidents with damage to persons or property. Now consider the timeline: VW technicians received the first field failure report on October 15, 2024. Internal stress tests on corroded bolts finished on September 10, 2026, and regulatory filings were logged September 24. That is roughly twenty-three months from first broken bolt in the wild to recall, for a defect whose failure chain is almost comically simple: one bolt head fractures under road salt corrosion, the steering rack is left hanging on a single remaining mounting point, continued loading cracks the rack housing, and the driver loses steering. Some of these bolts have been corroding since 2013. A few were already voting-age cars when VW decided to act.

Geography tells its own story here. The US campaign, NHTSA 26V590000, covers 208,724 vehicles with an estimated defect rate around one percent. Do the arithmetic globally and roughly 40,000 bolts worldwide could be on their way to fracture, which is my extrapolation of Dow Jones's US estimate, not anyone's official figure. Germany drove this action through the KBA, under recall code 48VS, and the American filing is a regional footnote: 5.2 percent of the global count. Owner letters go out November 10 in the US; German repairs start December 1; each fix takes under an hour and costs the owner nothing. VW declined to comment on what it will cost the company. You can do that math yourself if you like: four million dealer visits, minimum.

In fairness, the steelman writes itself: a precautionary recall of four million vehicles with zero known incidents is, depending on your lighting, either a scandal or the regulatory system working exactly as designed. VW studied the failure for nearly two years, ran the stress tests, and recalled before anyone got hurt. Nobody at Wolfsburg is accused of hiding bodies. A one percent defect estimate means the overwhelming majority of the replaced bolts were holding fine, which is how corrosion recall math works: you cannot inspect your way to the bad ones without removing all of them. And the "biggest since Dieselgate" framing deserves a footnote. Dieselgate was deliberate deception across eleven million vehicles. This is a screw. The comparison flatters the headline and undersells the difference.

What the data cannot tell you is where the corrosion is worst. Road salt is the stated accelerant, which means a 2014 Golf in Minnesota and a 2014 Golf in Arizona are not the same risk, and neither recall notice distinguishes them. The replacement bolt is described as corrosion-resistant, not redesigned, and nobody has published the test protocol that proves the new one outlasts the old one. Also unknown: whether the one percent estimate holds outside the US fleet, which skews younger and warmer than the global pool that includes eleven model years of European winters.

So check the metal, not the headlines: if you own a VW Tiguan, Touran, Golf, Golf Variant, or Caddy built between 2013 and 2024, or an Audi Q3 from 2017 to 2024, anywhere in the world, this recall now covers your car. American owners: run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and ask the dealer for campaign 48LG or 48VT. German owners: watch for the 48VS notice under KBA reference 17016R. It is free and takes under an hour. And if you are shopping used VWs, understand that no pre-purchase inspection on earth can see this bolt. It lives inside the subframe, under the car, quietly rusting. The recall is the inspection.
