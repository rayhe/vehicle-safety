# Research: #1270 — Orange County parents charged over kids' e-moto crashes

## Topic
Orange County, California District Attorney Todd Spitzer announced October 20-21, 2026 that his office has charged parents with child endangerment for allowing children to illegally ride electric motorcycles (e-motos) on public roads. The office says it has charged **12 parents since January 2026**. This is an enforcement story, not a device story: the intervention point is the purchase, not the machine.

## Novel finding / contribution
- The safety lever moved from the machine to the buyer. Recalls fix hardware; prosecutions aim at the purchase decision, which is the one moment an adult controls. Orange County is running that experiment in public, with a before/after metric already named by the DA (juvenile ER visits).
- The warnings ladder matters: at least one charged parent was warned by deputies on camera a year before charges, and the Garden Grove father was allegedly warned repeatedly and still bought a faster bike. Prosecutors are not starting with prison; they are starting with the parents who were warned and kept going. That distinction is the whole deterrence theory, and it is testable.
- The classification trap: sellers market these machines as e-bikes, but California law classifies by hardware (pedals, watts, speed), not by the listing title. A parent can buy a "bike" online and hand a child a motorcycle under the law.

## Counterargument (full strength)
Prosecuting parents, including parents whose child just died, is cruel and may not work. Dealers and online sellers legally sell these machines to adults, marketing them as e-bikes, while the state has done a poor job of making the pedals-and-watts line legible to a normal shopper. Criminal charges land unevenly: a family that can afford a lawyer reads the same law differently from one that cannot, and Orange County enforcement does not bind any other county. Impoundment, fines, civil liability, and point-of-sale warnings could achieve the same deterrence without putting grieving parents in a courtroom. Until the DA publishes juvenile ER numbers before and after the charges, deterrence is a theory, not a result.

## Limitations
- Charges are allegations, not convictions; no case in this batch has been reported as adjudicated.
- The DA's national figures (125+ deaths) and the "leading cause of juvenile ER visits" claim come from the DA's office via news reports, without a published methodology or dataset in the sources reviewed.
- Secondary reports differ on how the 12 charges break down by announcement wave; this article uses only the DA's cumulative figure (12 since January) and individually sourced case facts, not a wave total.
- Speeds cited (33, 43, 47+ mph) are DA allegations (Life360 data in one case, capability ratings in others), not independently measured crash reconstructions.
- No statewide count exists in the sources for how many children ride e-motos, so no rate (charges per rider) can be computed.
- This article covers Orange County only; no evidence is presented that other California counties are following suit.

## Actionable
- Before buying anything marketed as an e-bike for a child, check three things: does it have working pedals, is the motor at or under 750 watts, and does motor assistance stop at 20 or 28 mph by class. If any answer fails, California treats it as a motorcycle or moped, not a bicycle: it needs a license, registration, and insurance, and a child cannot legally ride it on public roads.
- If one is already in the garage, do not let a child ride it on public roads. Store the key and charger separately, and check the machine's actual top speed rather than trusting the listing.
- Parents who bought one in good faith should check the seller's wattage and speed claims against the hardware plate, not the product title.
