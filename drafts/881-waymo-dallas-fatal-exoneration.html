The Robotaxi Did Everything Right and a Man Still Died in Dallas

Vin Wreckage • Existential Dread Columnist
September 11, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: the most thoroughly exonerated driver in American history doesn't have a driver's license. On August 7, an unoccupied Waymo Jaguar I-Pace on Maple Avenue in Dallas made contact with a pedestrian who had just been struck by an SUV and hurled thirty feet across the road. The pedestrian died at the hospital, and police said no party was at fault. And for the first time in the history of car crashes, we don't have to take anyone's word for it, because the robotaxi kept receipts: detection at 150 feet, speed falling from 24 mph to 5, full stop on contact. The human SUV that struck him first was doing 35 in a 30 zone, and its impact carried 49 times the kinetic energy of the robotaxi's tap.[1][2]

49:1
Kinetic-energy ratio: the SUV's impact versus the Waymo's contact (energy scales with velocity squared).

Thirty-five versus five is not seven times worse. Kinetic energy grows with the square of speed, so one impact was a freight train and the other was a shopping cart. Waymo's claimed braking profile checks out against freshman physics: at 24 mph it needed roughly forty feet of its 150-foot detection margin to slow to a crawl, and the logs say it used exactly that. A human driver needs about 1.5 seconds of perception and reaction before braking even begins, which is 53 feet of travel at 24 mph with your hands on the wheel and your eyes pointed forward, conditions the SUV driver at 35 mph clearly did not meet.[2][3]

But the physics isn't the cosmic joke here; the paperwork is. Within days, the whole world knew the robotaxi's speed, sightline, braking curve, and innocence, all reconstructed from sensor logs with millisecond timestamps. Meanwhile the SUV, the vehicle that actually delivered the lethal energy, remains under investigation, with no charges, no telemetry, and no black box. The machine got tried, acquitted, and buried in public inside a week. The human gets due process and a private file. We're all going to die, but statistically the one who documents it in high definition gets blamed first.

Fairness demands the other reading, so here it is at full strength. That beautiful 150-foot detection and that perfect braking curve come from Waymo's own black box, which is to say from the defendant. We trust the numbers because convenience-store surveillance footage corroborates the timeline, but no independent reconstruction has been published, the police investigation is still open, and nobody outside Waymo has signed those logs. "Not at fault" is also a legal phrase, not a physics one: a system that can see a body 150 feet out and still cannot fully stop before an airborne pedestrian lands in its lane has genuine limits, and plaintiff attorneys will ask whether an earlier, harder stop or a swerve was possible. They will ask it in a courtroom, with money attached.[2][4]

So what do you actually do with this? Pedestrians: pedestrian fatalities overwhelmingly happen after dark, outside crosswalks, on arterial streets that look exactly like Maple Avenue. Cross at the light, wear something reflective, assume every driver is blind.[5] Drivers: five over a 30 limit means 36 percent more kinetic energy delivered to whatever you hit. Slow down on commercial streets at night. And everyone else: when the next crash involves an AV, demand the telemetry, because Dallas is proof that a black box is the difference between a witch hunt and an answer. The tragedy is that only the robot is required to carry one.
