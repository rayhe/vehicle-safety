The Wheels Fell Off an Omoda 5 in 2024. Chery Recalled 600 Cars. On October 6, the Wheels Fell Off Again

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 13, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. On October 6, a Chery Omoda 5 driving through Johor Bahru, Malaysia shed its entire rear suspension assembly (torsion beam, trailing arms, shock absorbers, brake lines, both rear wheels), leaving the hardware sitting in the road behind the car, and remarkably, no one was hurt.[1] Five days later, Malaysia's Road Transport Department suspended the model's type approval, halting new registrations, and Chery recalled 8,878 vehicles.[1][3] Here is the part that broke my spreadsheet: this is the second time the wheels have come off this exact model.

14.8×
How much larger the 2026 recall (8,878 vehicles) is than the 2024 campaign (600 vehicles) for the same failure

The first failure happened on April 28, 2024, in slow traffic. Chery's investigation blamed a welding tip that had been replaced during maintenance without recalibrating the equipment, which produced incomplete weld penetration on the rear axle beams.[2] Engineers traced the bad parts to components made on August 15, 2023. As a precaution, Chery inspected 600 vehicles built with axles from August 14 to 17, and sixty got new axles.[2][5] Regulators closed the recall in June 2024: case closed, welds fixed, everyone home. Twenty-nine months later, a black Omoda 5 left its rear axle on Jalan Pandan.[6]

Now the table I can't stop staring at:

In 2024, Chery scoped the defect to four production days. Two years later, the recall covers 8,878 C and H variants: 14.8 times the earlier population. Running the two candidate explanations against the data: one, the 2024 scope was too narrow, and bad welds escaped the August 14–17 window. Industry analyst Bai Wenxi said exactly that, arguing the earlier recall may have covered only part of the production volume with no review of the entire welding process.[1] Two: this is a different failure wearing an old problem's clothes. Chery gave the Omoda 5 a new rear suspension in September 2025.[3] If the October car wears the new design, the 2024 weld theory doesn't transfer. JPJ's technical investigation will decide which explanation holds, and it hasn't yet.

Zoom out from the single incident and the pattern gets uglier. On October 8, footage showed brake pedal supports snapping on three units of Huawei's $140,000 Maestro V800 during emergency braking tests.[1] On October 10, four Chinese ministries including the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology floated draft rules to tighten development, testing, and verification, explicitly targeting the "quick-build car" practice of compressing development cycles while skipping safety validation.[1] Wheels detaching and brake pedals snapping in the same week is not a coincidence a statistician enjoys.

In Chery's defense, the response time is genuinely fast: five days from incident to an 8,878-vehicle recall, with JPJ's suspension landing the same day. The 2024 campaign did what campaigns are supposed to do. It found a 10 percent defect rate inside its inspection population and replaced every confirmed bad axle under regulatory oversight. And the honest caveat: identical symptoms don't prove identical causes. Root cause for 2026 is still unconfirmed, and if the failed car carries the post-2025 suspension, this may be a new defect that merely rhymes with the old one.

What this article is not: a finding that the 2024 recall failed. JPJ's investigation is open, and the cause of the October failure is officially undetermined.[4] I don't know which suspension generation the October vehicle carried. Remember that this is fundamentally a Malaysia-market story. Chery doesn't sell the Omoda 5 in the United States, so no FARS, IIHS, or NHTSA data exists to cross-check against. And Chery's "welds lack sufficient strength" is the company's characterization, not an independent engineering verdict.

If you own an Omoda 5 C or H variant in Malaysia, get the inspection: the recall covers comprehensive checks and free component replacement.[3] For everyone else, the lesson here is pure arithmetic. A closed recall is a claim about a production window, not proof that a root cause is extinct. When the same failure recurs, the first question is always whether the first scope was right. Watch for JPJ's technical findings: that report decides between "too narrow" and "something new."
