## Story in one line
A court-ordered breathalyzer that 150,000 drivers must blow into before their cars will start is now the subject of a federal defect petition alleging the device itself stalls engines, kills steering and braking assist, and distracts drivers mid-drive — and the company behind it has already been fired by Virginia, censured by Michigan's attorney general, and knocked offline nationwide in March 2026.

## Kill test
- **Genuinely newsworthy?** YES. NHTSA opened Defect Petition DP26014 on November 19, 2026, five days ago. A federal safety agency is now formally deciding whether to investigate a device whose entire purpose is road safety. The petition asks for a safety recall.
- **Novel angle on data?** YES. The story is not "interlock has problems." The story is the inversion: the device courts install to stop drunk driving is accused of creating the exact hazards it exists to prevent — loss of motive power, loss of steering and braking assist, driver distraction during rolling retests. And the petition is not an isolated complaint; it lands on top of a documented pattern: Virginia terminated this vendor's contract in 2023, Michigan's AG issued a cease-and-desist the same year, a federal lawsuit preserves a battery-damage allegation, and a March 2026 outage locked drivers out of their cars in 45 states.

## Verified facts

1. **Defect Petition DP26014, opened November 19, 2026** (NHTSA ODI, via AutoMotor News, Nov 23, 2026). NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened a defect petition into the Intoxalock Model 1001A Ignition Interlock, made by Consumer Safety Technology, LLC. Petition dated August 11, 2026. NHTSA record ID11755577. NHTSA's subject line, verbatim: "Potential Failure in Interlock 1001A Could Lead to a Loss of Motive Power and Distracted Driving." The action is "Open Defect Petition" under 49 CFR Part 552: ODI will review the petitioner's submission and other relevant information to decide whether to grant or deny an investigation. No recall is listed. No closing date as of November 23, 2026.
2. **Population: 150,000, estimated** (NHTSA opening resume). The product is listed as EQUIPMENT, not a vehicle model. There is no model year. The 150,000 figure is NHTSA's estimated population for the listed product.
3. **The allegations** (petitioner's claims, as NHTSA summarizes them): mouthpieces can detach; the device can fail to register breath samples during rolling retests, distracting drivers while the vehicle is in motion; excessive current draw while the vehicle is off can deplete the battery and cause a no-start condition that can strand occupants in extreme temperatures. The petitioner cites consumer accounts alleging engine stalling while driving, loss of steering and braking assistance, and delayed restart, including crashes and near-crashes consumers attribute to engine shutdown or distracted driving during retesting. The petitioner also alleges hardware, software and manufacturing deficiencies and asks for a safety recall.
4. **NHTSA's own failure report summary: 1 incident, 0 crashes, 0 injuries, 0 deaths** (NHTSA ODI). One ODI report reference number: 11755106. The petition's crash and near-crash accounts are consumer allegations cited by the petitioner, not NHTSA findings. The article states this plainly and does not inflate it.
5. **Virginia fired the vendor in 2023** (Commission on VASAP, June 9, 2023 minutes, vasap.virginia.gov). "Intoxalock's contract was terminated at the last Commission meeting giving 90 days to transfer 1,692 clients." Over 70% had transferred by end of May 2023; the remainder were expected done by June 30. New five-year contracts went to LifeSafer, RoadGuard and Smart Start. The Commission's June 2023 meeting materials, as reported, show Intoxalock held about 19.5 percent of Virginia's interlock business while accounting for 96 percent of the state's ignition-interlock complaints received since November 1, 2022. The March 24, 2023 minutes also describe unstaffed service centers, uncertified technicians performing installations, and two instances in which Intoxalock entered an installation date into the state compliance system for a device that had not actually been installed. Intoxalock's CEO appeared before the Commission and stated she was "100% responsible" for the company's performance.
6. **Michigan's attorney general called it "the lowest form of deceit"** (Michigan AG press releases, March 16 and May 5, 2023, michigan.gov). On March 15, 2023, AG Dana Nessel issued a cease-and-desist letter over solicitation mailers that resembled official government documents, sent to people who had entered pleas for operating while intoxicated but had not yet been sentenced. The mailers identified consumers by name and address as having been charged with OWI offenses. Nessel: "Tricking people into making a purchase by using an ad purporting to be from a court of law is the lowest form of deceit." On May 5, 2023, the AG concluded the investigation after the company changed the mailer. The article states the conclusion; the censure is not presented as an open case.
7. **A federal lawsuit preserves the battery-damage allegation** (Mallory v. Consumer Safety Technology, LLC, August 20, 2024). The plaintiff alleged misrepresented costs, overcharges, excessive fees including a lockout fee, and battery damage. The federal court granted the company's motion to compel arbitration under the lease's arbitration clause, so the allegations were never adjudicated. The article says exactly that: allegation preserved, merits never decided.
8. **March 2026: the devices went dark nationwide** (Oklahoma Board of Tests for Alcohol and Drug Influence memorandum, March 17, 2026, oklahoma.gov; consumer reports). The Board reported "nationwide service disruptions affecting certain ignition interlock devices and related systems associated with Intoxalock." Drivers reported being locked out of their vehicles, devices recording violations and placing vehicles into lockout status, service centers closed, and the company's phone and system support down. Local reporting counted impacted devices in 45 states. The company disclosed a cybersecurity event in March 2026; a federal class action, Strahl v. Consumer Safety Technology LLC (Case 4:26-cv-00130, S.D. Iowa), alleges cybercriminals stole customer information. The class action is an allegation, not a finding.
9. **A California class action settled** (Haggerty v. Consumer Safety Technology, LLC). The suit alleged the company recorded customers' telephone calls without the knowledge or consent of all parties. It settled for $1,747,500. The settlement is a payment, not an admission.

## Original contribution: the inversion + the pattern ledger

Two calculations/framings nobody in the coverage ran:

**The inversion.** Every other story in this site's 859-article run is about a car failing its driver. This one is about a safety device failing its driver in the exact ways it is supposed to prevent: loss of motive power (the petition's words), loss of steering and braking assist (consumer accounts cited in the petition), and distraction (rolling retests that fail to register a sample while the car is moving). A breathalyzer that demands your attention at highway speed is a distracted-driving device. The petition's own subject line says so.

**The pattern ledger.** NHTSA's failure summary counts 1 incident. One. But the regulatory record around this single vendor, in three years, reads: a state termination (Virginia, 2023, 1,692 clients moved), an attorney-general cease-and-desist (Michigan, 2023), a federal battery-damage lawsuit sent to arbitration (2024), a nationwide outage plus a cybersecurity disclosure (March 2026), a $1.75 million call-recording settlement (California), and now a federal defect petition (November 2026). The article's original contribution is assembling that ledger in one place and asking the question NHTSA's 1-incident count does not answer: how many chances does a court-mandated safety device get?

**The population math.** 150,000 devices in the petition population. Virginia's 1,692 terminated clients are 1.1% of that. If the 96%-of-complaints-on-19.5%-of-business ratio from Virginia held nationally, it would be the worst complaint concentration in the interlock industry — but the article does NOT extrapolate it nationally, because Virginia's numbers are Virginia's. The ratio is stated, the extrapolation is explicitly refused.

## FARS cross-check
Not applicable in the usual sense: the product is equipment, not a vehicle model, and FARS_BY_MODEL has no interlock row. No fatality-rate claim is made. The article notes the gap explicitly: there is no FARS row for a device that is bolted into cars after sale, which is part of why a petition with 1 ODI incident can still matter — the surveillance system that catches vehicle defects does not count equipment failures the same way.

## Counterargument (full strength)
NHTSA's failure report summary lists one incident, zero crashes, zero injuries, zero deaths. That is the strongest fact in the company's favor and the article leads with it rather than burying it. A defect petition is not an investigation, an investigation is not a defect finding, and a defect finding is not a recall; most petitions are denied. The petition's crash accounts are consumer allegations, self-selected and unverified, and the device's critics include an organized advocacy campaign (IntoxaLockedOut) with an obvious point of view. Interlocks work: they prevent drunk driving, which kills roughly 10,000 Americans a year, and every major study shows they cut recidivism while installed. A rolling retest is a minor inconvenience for a sober driver, and a dead battery is a tow bill, not a funeral. The article states all of that, then states the rebuttal: the petition does not ask NHTSA to decide whether interlocks are good. It asks whether this model, in 150,000 cars, can cut power and steering while moving — and a state that ran the program fired the vendor, which is not a consumer complaint but a regulator's verdict.

## Limitations
- The petition is at the earliest stage: ODI has only agreed to consider whether to investigate. NHTSA may deny it. No defect has been found.
- The crash and near-crash accounts are allegations cited by the petitioner, not NHTSA findings. NHTSA's own count is 1 incident, 0 crashes.
- The Virginia 19.5%/96% figures come from the Commission's June 2023 meeting materials as reported by IntoxaLockedOut; the primary minutes I verified directly confirm the termination and the 1,692-client transfer, not the complaint-share ratio. The ratio is attributed, not asserted as independently verified.
- The Michigan AG investigation was concluded May 5, 2023, after the mailer was changed. It is a closed matter, presented as history.
- The March 2026 cybersecurity event and the Strahl class action are allegations in litigation; no finding is claimed.
- The Haggerty settlement figure ($1,747,500) is reported by the advocacy source; the settlement itself is a matter of public record but the figure is attributed to that reporting.
- No FARS data exists for this product; no fatality-rate claim is made.

## What to do (actionable)
- If you have an Intoxalock 1001A installed: keep a written log of every failed retest, lockout, stall, or dead battery, with dates. The petition exists because someone filed paperwork; NHTSA's Vehicle Owner Questionnaire at nhtsa.gov is the same mechanism, and it accepts complaints about equipment, not just vehicles.
- If a rolling retest fails while you are driving: pull over before retrying. Do not fumble with a mouthpiece at speed. A missed retest is a violation; a crash is worse.
- If the car will not start after sitting: check the battery before assuming the device is at fault, but keep the tow receipt and the service record. Battery damage is the specific allegation in the Mallory lawsuit and in the petition.
- If your state runs an interlock program: ask your monitoring authority whether Intoxalock is a current approved vendor. Virginia is not, as of 2023. Your state's list is public.
- If you are choosing a vendor after a DUI conviction: you usually do not get to choose, the court does. But you can ask the court or your monitoring authority about alternatives, and you can file a complaint with your state AG if a vendor's mailer looks like a court document. Michigan's did, and the AG acted.
