NHTSA Couldn't Investigate Tesla's Door Handles. No Law Requires an Escape.

BY REX DRIVERTON • Clautomotive Desk
October 7, 2026

In November 2025, someone filed a federal petition asking NHTSA to investigate the manual door releases on roughly 179,000 Tesla Model 3s. The claim: the mechanical emergency release wasn't readily accessible or clearly identified. On July 24, 2026, NHTSA said no. Not because the releases are fine. Because the federal standard governing door locks and latches contains, in the agency's own words, "no requirements" about the labeling or location of a manual release. You cannot be found defective under a rule that does not exist.[1]

Read that denial twice, because it is a confession dressed as a verdict. The petition was not defeated by evidence of safety; it was defeated by a regulatory void. As of March 2026, NHTSA had logged exactly one consumer complaint matching the petition's allegation, and it came from the petitioner's own car, while Tesla's owner's manual, regulators noted, already illustrates the release's location in an "In Case of Emergency" section. One complaint is a thin basis for investigating 179,000 vehicles, and the agency said so. Case closed on paper, while the real-world timeline kept running hard in the other direction.

On October 25, 2025, 20-year-old Samuel Tremblett survived a single-vehicle crash into a tree in Easton, Massachusetts, in a 2021 Model Y. Then he couldn't get out. He dialed 911 from inside the burning car and told the operator he couldn't get out, that it was on fire, that he was going to die, according to transcripts filed in the lawsuit his mother brought in February 2026. His body was found in the back seat, which suggests he tried every door before the smoke took him. He died of thermal injuries and smoke inhalation, not crash trauma.[2] Five months before that, on Thanksgiving Eve 2024, four college students in a Cybertruck hit a tree and a wall in Piedmont, California. The electronic handles failed inside and out, and while a bystander smashed a window with a tree branch and dragged one passenger out, the other three died inside.[2]

Now watch two governments react to the same design trade-off. China finalized a ban on Tesla-style electronic door handles earlier this year: any car with electronic actuators must have a mechanical fallback when the power dies, plus sane placement, exterior operation, and markings a passenger can actually read, effective January 1, 2027 for new models.[2] The US position, from the denial document: there is no rule, so there is no defect, plus a separate granted petition for rulemaking that will write an industry-wide emergency-egress standard sometime in the next several years. Rulemaking covers future cars, while the dead were driving current ones.

Congress is moving, slowly, the way Congress does: the SAFE Exit Act, introduced by Rep. Robin Kelly of Illinois on January 6, 2026, would force NHTSA to set performance and labeling standards for electric doors, with clearly marked mechanical releases inside every door and exterior access for first responders. It cleared the House Energy and Commerce Committee on May 21.[3] And Tesla, to its credit, has already begun shipping what the petition wanted: combined electronic-and-manual releases in a single motion, plus a mechanical fix on China-market vehicles.[1] When the company you denied the petition against starts building the thing the petition asked for, the petition was right.

Here's what you can actually do, because Washington's timeline is measured in years. Go sit in your car and find every manual door release. In a Model 3 or Model Y, the rear release hides inside a door pocket behind a panel, a multi-step process no panicked person would invent under smoke, and it is not the button you press every day but a separate mechanical lever you have probably never touched. Practice it now, in a driveway, with the lights on. If you are shopping for a car with electronic latches, check whether each door has a clearly labeled mechanical release, and whether anyone from outside can open it. No US federal standard requires either, and that is the whole story.
