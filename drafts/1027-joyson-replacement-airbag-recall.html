The Airbag That Replaced Your Airbag Has Been Recalled

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a side impact. A curtain airbag is supposed to unfurl from the roofline like a choreographed sail, covering the windows before your head meets glass. Every fold, every roll, every mounting bracket is part of that choreography. Now consider that Toyota just recalled 4,118 of those choreographers, because the replacements themselves were installed wrong at the factory that made them.[1]

1%
The official defect rate in Toyota's filing — which the filing's own footnote admits means "unknown"

Two campaigns, one supplier. NHTSA 26E050 covers 2,381 curtain shield airbag assemblies sold as service replacement parts for 2016-2023 Tacomas, where the cushion may not have been properly secured to its mounting bracket.[1] NHTSA 26E051 covers 1,737 assemblies for 2014-2021 Tundra Double Cabs and CrewMaxes, where the airbag material was improperly rolled during assembly.[4] These parts were never in any truck from the factory. They entered trucks later, at body shops, after the trucks had already crashed once.

Read that again, because the only trucks carrying these airbags are trucks that already survived one collision, paid for a repair, and were told they were whole again. The defect only reveals itself in a second crash, when portions of the curtain fail to reach their intended positions and occupant restraint performance drops.[1] These owners already paid the crash tax once. They may be about to pay it again, through no fault of their own.

And the supplier? Joyson Safety Systems, operating out of Torreón, Mexico, a company assembled from Takata's non-liability assets after the 2017 bankruptcy.[1][6] Yes, that Takata. The company built from the ashes of the largest airbag scandal in automotive history is now the subject of a recall for replacement airbags. The symmetry is almost poetic. Almost.

Credit where due: the system caught this the way it is supposed to. Joyson flagged the condition during a lot acceptance test in December 2025, found ten more bad parts, and spent January through May inspecting recovered units, where it found what the filing delicately calls "a high percent" with the condition. Toyota ran its own engineering analysis through June and July and ordered the recall on July 24.[1] As of late July, there were zero field reports and zero warranty claims from the United States. That is a supplier doing its job and an automaker acting before the injuries.

But then there is the number on the form. Toyota's filing lists the estimated percentage of defective parts as 1%. Scroll to the footnote and it gets better: the NHTSA portal requires an integer, so Toyota entered "1," and Toyota itself clarifies that "1" means unknown.[1] The official defect rate is a placeholder wearing a percentage sign. The supplier found a "high percent" of bad units in its sample. Nobody knows how many of the 4,118 are actually defective, and the federal record says so in code.

Which brings us to the part that should worry you more than the defect. Toyota is notifying only owners it knows had these parts installed. The suspect parts flowed through dealer parts counters into independent body shops and collision repair facilities. Toyota is now asking dealers to reconstruct which trucks received them, shop by shop, invoice by invoice.[1] The owner-notification deadline was September 27, three days ago. If your repair never touched a Toyota dealer's service bay, the letter may never find you.

So here is the actionable part. If you own a 2016-2023 Tacoma and had a curtain airbag replaced after May 2024, or a 2014-2021 Tundra Double Cab or CrewMax with a replacement after November 2021, take the truck to a Toyota dealer for inspection even if no letter arrived.[3] Dig out your collision repair invoice and look for the part numbers or the supplier's name. The recall carries Do Not Drive and Park Outside advisories, Toyota campaign numbers 26TK02 and 26TH02, and Toyota's customer service line is 1-800-331-4331.[2] A VIN search at nhtsa.gov/recalls is worth doing, but equipment recalls do not always surface cleanly under a VIN, so call with your repair records in hand.[5]

What this does not prove: the defect is real-world unconfirmed, and 4,118 units is a rounding error against the millions of Tacomas and Tundras on the road. Toyota acted on supplier test data before a single injury, which deserves more praise than suspicion. And the Do Not Drive advisory on an unknown defect rate is arguably over-cautious boilerplate rather than a measured risk assessment. But an airbag supply chain where the repair parts need their own recall, traced through a paper trail of body-shop invoices, with a defect rate filed as fiction, is a story about how thin the safety net gets one layer down. Check your invoice.
