The Gap

Daylight Saving Ends November 1. Drivers Get Safer. Pedestrians Die.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 13, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. On Sunday, November 1, at 2 a.m., America moves its clocks back an hour, and the 5-to-7 p.m. commute window flips from civil twilight to full dark overnight. Nobody's schedule changes: same crosswalk, same hour, same dog walk. The environment just gets seven times deadlier for the people outside the cars, according to University of Michigan researchers who used the time change as a natural experiment.[3] In the five weeks after the fall-back, fatal pedestrian and cyclist crashes rise 13 percent. Fatal crashes involving vehicle occupants fall 7.1 percent.[1]

+13% / -7.1%
Fatal pedestrian/cyclist crashes after the fall time change vs. fatal vehicle-occupant crashes, per IIHS analysis of 2010-2019 FARS data

Read those two numbers again, because they describe a transfer: one policy lever, one hour of light moved from evening to morning, drivers measurably safer and pedestrians measurably deader. Insurance Institute for Highway Safety researchers ran the FARS data for every time change from 2010 through 2019, isolating only the hours whose light levels actually changed, and found the pattern held in both directions: spring forward, pedestrians win and drivers lose; fall back, the reverse. Net over the decade, both changes together cost 29 extra vehicle-occupant lives and saved 26 pedestrian and cyclist lives.[1] Roughly a wash in total: anything but a wash in who pays.

The mechanism is not mysterious: it is optical, and it is brutal. A driver at 45 mph covers 66 feet every second. Low-beam headlights on a good car reach maybe 150 to 200 feet down the road, which gives you roughly three seconds from "pedestrian becomes visible" to "pedestrian becomes a statistic" once you subtract reaction time and braking distance. On a car with headlights IIHS rates Poor or Marginal, and that is one in three cars on American roads, the reach is shorter and the math gets worse.[5] Your eyes need time to dark-adapt after sunset. A pedestrian in dark clothing at dusk is, to your visual system, approximately a rumor. The 150 milliseconds I opened with? That is roughly how long your brain needs to decide something is there. Darkness does not slow your decision down; it delays the moment the decision starts.

Evening is where the exposure concentrates, which is why the transfer runs the direction it does. Morning gets the light back, but morning pedestrians are mostly commuters on fixed routes in familiar traffic. Evening has the after-school crowd, the dog walkers, the errand runners, the kids whose practice now ends in the dark. IIHS's own president told a Senate hearing last April that less than a quarter of all trips begin at night, yet nearly half of vehicle-occupant deaths and 77 percent of pedestrian deaths happen in the dark.[2] The fall-back does not create the darkness. It relocates it onto the busiest pedestrian hours of the day.

Context matters, because 2024 was actually a good year for pedestrians by the grim standards of the field. NHTSA's finalized numbers show 7,080 pedestrians killed, down 3.9 percent from 2023, and a pedestrian killed every 74 minutes instead of every 70.[4] But injuries rose 5 percent to 71,635, which means more people are getting hit and fewer of them are dying per hit: better crash survival, not safer streets. And 76 percent of those deaths still happened in the dark. The November time change lands on a pedestrian population that is getting hit more often, surviving slightly more often, and doing almost all of it after sunset.

Here is the honest counterweight, and it comes from the same institute that found the effect. IIHS president David Harkey told senators the twice-yearly time change is "not a major factor" in America's overall road safety, and the study's own net numbers back him: 29 and 26 lives over a decade, against 7,080 pedestrian deaths in a single year.[2] Permanent daylight saving would not fix this; it would just move the darkness to the morning school run. Arizona and Hawaii skip the clock change entirely, and Arizona still has one of the worst pedestrian death rates in the country. The clock is a trigger; the disease is year-round: dark roads, under-lit crosswalks, poor headlights, and a vehicle fleet engineered around the survival of the people inside it.

Limitations: The IIHS analysis covers 2010-2019; pedestrian deaths have surged roughly 80 percent since 2009, so the +13% share may not scale linearly to 2026 volumes. This is a five-week-window effect, not an annual apocalypse. Causation is an ambient-light association; the occupant-side drop partly reflects people simply driving less or differently in the dark, and sleep disruption confounds the spring change. Arizona and Hawaii dilute the national signal. None of this changes the direction of the effect, only its size.

So here is the practical engineering, since nobody is moving the sun. Drivers: for the two weeks after November 1, drive the 5-to-7 p.m. window like it is 9 p.m. Slow down on arterials, use high beams wherever legal, and assume every unlit crosswalk is occupied. Check your headlight lenses and aim before November; a hazed-over housing costs you 30 feet of throw you cannot afford to lose. Pedestrians: after November 2, anything reflective or light-colored you wear after 5 p.m. is safety equipment, not fashion. Cross at lit intersections, because nearly three-quarters of pedestrian deaths happen outside them. Parents: your kid's practice schedule did not change, but the light did; walk the route once in the dark before they do. Fleet managers: your last-mile drivers are now doing their final stops in darkness, in the exact conditions where pedestrian crashes spike 13 percent. Brief them.

One hour. Nobody changes their routine: the photons just stop showing up for the evening shift, and the deaths move from the seats with airbags to the sidewalk without them.
