November's Second Half Recalled 898,820 Vehicles. 4,406 of Them Were New Cars.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They did not get better, because the second half of November filed 898,820 vehicles across 11 recall campaigns, and 810,592 of them are 13-year-old Hondas with Takata airbags that can throw metal fragments at their drivers.[5] One campaign is 90.2 percent of the half-month. One day, November 19, filed seven campaigns and 896,048 vehicles, which is 99.7 percent of the entire half-month's total, and the other three filing days combined managed 2,772.[1][2]

0.49%
Share of the 898,820 vehicles filed November 13-20, 2026 that belong to campaigns touching a 2026-model-year vehicle: 4,406 vehicles across 6 campaigns.

Here is the shape of it. Honda's Takata recall, 26V765, covers 810,592 cars from model years 2012 and 2013, and it is 90.2 percent of everything filed in the half-month. Ram's ABS software recall, 26V756, covers 82,843 Ram 1500 Classics from 2022 through 2024, and it is 9.2 percent. Those two campaigns are 99.4 percent of the half-month between them. The other nine campaigns combined cover 5,385 vehicles, which is 0.6 percent, and six of those nine are the only ones that touch a 2026 model at all.[1]

Six campaigns, 4,406 vehicles, and not one of them looks like the others. Altec filed twice on November 13, both times about aerial-device trucks: 1,100 AM and AN Series trucks whose platform stow valve handle was assembled without thread-locking compound, so it can separate and eject hot oil, and 161 AT Series trucks whose rear-suspension U-bolt was not tightened properly after modification work, so the rear axle can loosen or detach. Gillig filed November 18 for 1,323 Low Floor electric buses whose EV resolver cable can drop connectivity and switch the bus from drive to neutral with no warning. Rivian filed November 19 for 1,619 R1S, R1T, and R2 vehicles whose tire placard misstates the wheel, tire, and spare configuration, which is a federal labeling violation with a real consequence: wrong inflation, or the wrong replacement tire after somebody reads the label and trusts it. Genesis filed November 20 for 188 GV80 SUVs whose front-left upper control arm ball joint may detach from the steering knuckle.[1][3][4]

Then there is the Durango. Fifteen of them. Chrysler built 15 model-year 2026 Dodge Durangos with an incorrect powertrain, and the remedy in the federal filing is not a software update or a torque check: dealers will replace the engine and the catalytic converters, free of charge. Somewhere in a factory, the wrong engine went into 15 SUVs, and somebody signed the paperwork, and the fix is a whole new engine. It is the smallest campaign of the half-month and the best story in it, because every other campaign in this census is a spreadsheet and this one is a forklift.[3]

Two more details reward a closer read. Volvo and Mack filed the same defect on the same day, November 19, for the same reason: front steering knuckles that may crack and break because of a manufacturing error, 755 Volvo VN, VNL, and VNR trucks under 26V758 and 141 Mack Anthem, Pioneer, and Pinnacle trucks under 26V759, same inspect-and-replace remedy, same January 18 owner letters. Neither filing names the knuckle supplier, so a shared supplier is an inference, not a fact in the record, but 896 trucks from two makes with one bad part on one day is a pattern whether or not anyone confirms the vendor. And BMW filed for 83 convertibles, 430i, M440i, and M4, because the rearview mirror may be insufficiently attached, and during a crash the front passenger airbag may strike the mirror and knock it loose into the cabin. Dealers will replace the windshield. Read that twice: the fix for a loose mirror is a new windshield.[1]

Now the correction, because this census exists to correct the last one. Our first-half census, published November 16, counted 15 campaigns and 855,544 vehicles filed November 1 through 12, with 8 touching a 2026 model, 68,666 vehicles, 8.0 percent. That count was wrong, and the reason is boring in exactly the way that matters: NHTSA publishes a recall a few days after the manufacturer reports it, so twelve campaigns filed November 2 through 12 were not in the data when we ran the census. They are in the data now, each one verified live against the NHTSA recalls API this run, and they total 471,509 vehicles, including a 314,029-vehicle Toyota Takata recall and a 62,490-truck Toyota Tundra camshaft recall. Corrected first half: 27 campaigns, 1,327,053 vehicles, 14 touching a 2026 model, 197,604 vehicles, 14.9 percent. We undercounted the first half by 35.5 percent, and the 8.0 percent headline nearly doubles on the recount, to 14.9 percent.[1]

Put both halves together and the month through November 20 is 38 campaigns and 2,225,873 vehicles, with 20 campaigns touching a 2026 model, 202,010 vehicles, 9.1 percent. Two campaigns own the month: the Toyota Takata recall from November 12 and the Honda Takata recall from November 19 total 1,124,621 inflators, which is 50.5 percent of everything filed in November, and both are about propellant degrading in airbags that have spent more than a decade baking in dashboards. By campaign count the split is nearly even, 20 of 38 touching a 2026 vehicle, but by vehicles it is 9.1 percent, and the vehicle share is the headline because it is what owners experience.[1]

Fairness demands the other side, stated at full strength. Every share in this article is a snapshot of published data, not a final total, and the correction above proves the point: if the first-half census undercounted by 35.5 percent because of publication lag, this census undercounts by an unknown amount for the same reason, and filings dated November 21 through 26 had not been published by the time we ran the numbers. Campaign populations are full-campaign totals, not 2026-only slices, so 4,406 overstates the number of 2026 vehicles; the 0.49 percent is an upper bound, not the share itself. And the month's biggest number is not the month's biggest risk: a 2012 Civic with a rupturing passenger inflator and a 2026 GV80 with a detaching ball joint are not comparable hazards, and a Takata inflator count of 1.1 million does not make November more dangerous than a month with one small recall of a steering knuckle that can snap at speed. All of that is true. None of it changes the shape of the data, which is that November's recall system keeps fixing cars from a decade ago while this year's models barely appear in it.[1]

What do you do with this? If you own a 2026 Durango, 15 trucks got the wrong powertrain and the fix is a new engine, free, with VINs searchable on NHTSA.gov from December 1 and letters December 15. If you own a 2026 Genesis GV80, 188 cars have a ball joint that can detach; inspection, tightening, replacement, and alignment are free, VINs have been searchable since November 24, and letters go out January 18. If you own a 2022 through 2027 Rivian, the tire placard may be wrong; new labels are free, and letters go out January 18. If you own a 2012 or 2013 Honda or Acura, the month's real story is yours: 810,592 cars with Takata inflators that can rupture and throw metal fragments, interim letters December 18, and the final remedy letter comes later, so check the VIN now instead of waiting for the mail. If you own a 2022 through 2024 Ram 1500 Classic, the ABS software can misread the brake pedal, light the brake lamps wrong, let the truck roll out of Park, and drop stability control and forward collision warning; the free software update comes with letters January 14. And the general lesson, second verse: a recall filed in November is usually about a car you bought years ago, and the census that told you otherwise last week was undercounted by a third, which is why we recount.[1][3][4][5]
