Rivian Blamed the Mechanics for a Failing Rear Suspension. NHTSA Is Looking at the Bolts Instead.

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Desk
October 11, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a rear toe link failure. One small bolt fractures, the rear wheel toes itself out under load, and the back of your 7,000-pound electric truck decides to steer while you are doing nothing at all.[1] That is what NHTSA says happened to two Rivian R1S SUVs on public roads, and it is why the agency opened Preliminary Evaluation PE26004 on May 26, 2026, covering 114,922 R1T and R1S vehicles from model years 2022 through 2025. The drivers never saw it coming. Neither, apparently, did Rivian's service procedure.

114,922
R1T and R1S trucks under federal investigation for rear toe link bolt fracture. Rivian's recall covered 19,641 of them.

Rivian got there first, and that is exactly what makes this case so revealing. On January 5, 2026, the company filed Safety Recall 26V003000 for 19,641 vehicles, blaming an older service procedure that let technicians reassemble the rear toe link joint incorrectly before March 2025. The fix was replacement bolts: M14 class-10.9 fasteners, part SC00002057-A, swapped in at the dealer.[2] Read the fine print and the shape of the recall tells you everything. Only trucks that had been into a service bay for suspension work were included. Rivian's internal line, delivered to reporters, was that its own data showed the toe link joints were operating as intended.[4] In plain language: this is a wrench-turning problem, not a design problem, and if your truck never saw a service bay it could not possibly be affected.

Then the investigators opened the file, and the wrench-turning theory wobbled. NHTSA's preliminary evaluation was triggered by two Vehicle Owner Questionnaires from drivers of 2023 and 2024 R1S SUVs whose left rear toe links separated while driving. Both vehicles crossed multiple lanes; one crashed into another vehicle and a guardrail, and a New Jersey driver's own onboard camera captured the failure in progress.[3] In both cases the bolt holding the toe link had fractured, and both trucks had driven for months and thousands of miles after their last service before the bolts let go. One of the two complaint vehicles had never received the suspension service Rivian blamed. A joint that can separate months after correct-looking assembly, with no warning light and no gradual degradation, is not a procedure problem. It is a sensitivity problem. What NHTSA is now evaluating is whether the rear toe link joint is robust under foreseeable road and service conditions at all, including on the 95,000 trucks Rivian's recall left out.

From an engineering standpoint, the design deserves the skepticism. A single fastener is the entire load path keeping the rear wheel pointed straight, and when it fails there is no graceful degradation: the wheel goes to full toe-out at speed and the driver's corrective steering input only deepens the yaw. A well-designed suspension has margin against a technician's imperfect afternoon. This one, apparently, does not. Owners of 2022 through 2025 R1T and R1S trucks should check their VINs at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 26V003000 today, and if the truck had any rear-suspension service before March 2025, get the bolt replacement scheduled. For the 95,000 trucks outside that recall, ask the service center to check toe link fastener torque at the next visit while PE26004 stays open. There is no dashboard warning for a fractured bolt, and you do not want to meet this failure mode at 70 miles an hour.

Limitations: PE26004 is a preliminary evaluation, not a defect finding; NHTSA has not determined that the toe link joint is defective, and no recall expansion has been ordered. The complaint count (two incidents, one crash) is small relative to the 114,922-vehicle estimate, and the agency's evidence comes from owner questionnaires, not a fleet-wide teardown. Rivian's position that the joints operate as intended remains the official counterpoint. Recall 26V003000's 1%-defect estimate is Rivian's own figure. Fatality figures in ref-5 cover medium/heavy-duty vehicles, a different population than R1T/R1S, and are included only to size the stakes of control-loss failures at highway speed.
