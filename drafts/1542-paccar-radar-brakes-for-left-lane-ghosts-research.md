# Research: #1542 — The radar lost its place in the lane. 6,185 semis brake for the left lane.

**Slug:** 1542-paccar-radar-brakes-for-left-lane-ghosts
**Journalist:** Mia Crumplezone (Safety Engineering Editor — safety tech is her beat; she wrote #830, the heavy-truck AEB phantom-braking investigation this article continues)
**Kicker:** Investigation
**Started:** 2026-11-20

## Proposed angle
PACCAR filed recall **26V744** with NHTSA on **November 11, 2026**: **6,185** model-year 2027 Kenworth T680, T880, W990 and Peterbilt 567, 579, 589 trucks, built February 11 to October 8, 2026. The forward-looking radar can falsely detect stationary or slow-moving objects in the **left lane** as relevant vehicles ahead, causing unintended braking. The root cause is a parameter calculator that writes a software data set into the radar's memory incorrectly: when the radar never learns the truck's real parameters, it defaults to a lateral position too far left of where the truck actually is, so it starts tracking the lane next door. An 80,000-pound truck slamming its brakes for a car that is not in its path. The supplier is Bendix. The remedy is a software update, but owner letters and NHTSA VIN searchability do not arrive until **January 10, 2027** — 67 days after PACCAR's safety committee decided to recall. The article's original contribution: the tie to NHTSA's own open engineering analysis EA25006 (the phantom-braking probe #830 covered), the concentration math (two models are 89% of the recall), the 67-day notification gap, and the fact that this is at least the fifth PACCAR recall on 2027 trucks since July.

Component: FORWARD COLLISION AVOIDANCE: SENSING SYSTEM: RADAR. "The forward-looking radar may falsely detect stationary or slow-moving objects in the left lane as relevant vehicles, which may cause unintended braking." Consequence: "Unintended braking increases the risk of a crash." Remedy: dealers update the radar software, free of charge. Owner letters expected to be mailed January 10, 2026 (see Limitations: the NHTSA record's year is inconsistent with the amended schedule). PACCAR's number: 26PACL. ## Original calculations (for the article)
- **Two models are the recall:** Kenworth T680 (2,790) plus Peterbilt 579 (2,708) = **5,498 of 6,185 trucks, 88.9%**. The other four models (T880, W990, 567, 589) split the remaining 687.
- **Report rate:** 16 reports (8 warranty claims + 8 field reports) across 6,185 trucks = **0.26% reported** as of November 4. But the population is defined by Bendix radar part numbers shipped, not by symptoms: every truck in the population carries the mis-written parameter set, so the defect is present by construction and the trigger is left-lane traffic, not a wear-out. Label the 0.26% as a report rate, not an occurrence rate; most phantom-brake events are never reported.
- **The notification gap:** safety committee decision November 4, 2026 to VIN searchability and owner letters January 10, 2027 = **67 days**. For 67 days, 6,185 trucks drive with a known phantom-brake defect and no owner can confirm by VIN whether their truck is in it. Fleets must go by build date (February 11 to October 8, 2026) and model, not by VIN lookup.
- **The fix was ready before the recall:** corrected software existed October 19, 2026, sixteen days before the recall decision and fifty-three days before owners are notified. Trucks still at the plants got it; trucks already delivered wait for a dealer visit.
- **Fifth 2027 recall since July:** per FreightWaves' count of NHTSA records. The 2027 Kenworth/Peterbilt launch has now produced recalls for the power distribution center (26V485, July 27), seat ventilation harness short (26V544, August 22), bunk restraint webbing (26V674, October 14), driveline snap ring (26V743), and now radar (26V744, November 11). The pattern is the story's second thread: the launch, not the single defect.
- **EA25006 scale check:** 24 induced crashes in the probe data against 19,118 crashes the mandate projects preventing per year at full saturation. The induced harm is small in absolute terms; the point is that NHTSA's benefit math counts zero induced crashes, and now a manufacturer has recalled trucks for the exact failure mode the probe is studying.

## Limitations (must appear in the article)
- No crashes or injuries are attributed to 26V744 in the sources verified: 8 warranty claims, 8 field reports, zero injuries as of November 4, 2026. The crash risk is the stated consequence, not an observed outcome for this campaign.
- The radar supplier is Bendix per FreightWaves' reading of the Part 573 filing. The specific Bendix system name (e.g., which Wingman generation) is not in the verified sources; do not name it.
- NHTSA's acknowledgment record lists owner letters "expected to be mailed January 10, 2026," which is impossible (the recall was filed November 11, 2026). The amended Part 573 schedule lists January 10, 2027 for dealer notification, owner notification, and VIN searchability; use 2027 and note the inconsistency exists in NHTSA's own record.
- EA25006 covers 2017-2022 Daimler trucks, not 2027 PACCAR trucks. The link is the shared failure mode (false AEB activation) and the shared regulatory context (the AEB mandate), not a shared investigation or supplier finding. Do not claim NHTSA is investigating PACCAR's radar.
- FARS 2014-2023 does not cover heavy trucks in the site's per-model data; all truck figures here come from NHTSA recall and investigation records, not FARS.
- The 0.26% report rate is a floor: phantom braking is underreported, and PACCAR's population is parts-defined, so the true occurrence rate is unknown and higher.

## Strongest counterargument (must appear at full strength)
The honest counterargument is that this recall is the system working. Sixteen reports, zero injuries, a software fix that was ready sixteen days before the recall decision, and a manufacturer that held trucks at its plants the day it learned of the problem. A radar that brakes for a perceived threat is doing what automatic emergency braking is designed to do; the error is a calibration parameter, not a failed sensor, and it is fixed with a download. The mandate math still crushes the objection: 19,118 rear-end crashes prevented per year against 24 induced crashes in the probe data, 155 lives saved against one fatality linked to false activation. Phantom braking is a known, rare failure mode of a technology that prevents far more crashes than it causes, the same way early ABS and ESC had false positives before they became background. The article must grant this fully, then answer it: the mandate's benefit number is gross, not net, because NHTSA has never published an induced-harm estimate; and a safety system that cannot tell which lane it is in is not a calibration footnote, it is a localization failure in the exact function the mandate will require. The recall is small. The precedent it sets for the mandate is not.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- Fleet managers running 2027 Kenworth T680, T880, W990 or Peterbilt 567, 579, 589 tractors built February 11 to October 8, 2026: brief drivers that the truck may brake for slow or stopped traffic in the left lane, and keep following distance in mind for the vehicles behind you, because a phantom stop turns your truck into the obstacle. Do not wait for the letter: call Peterbilt at 1-940-591-4220 or Kenworth at 1-425-828-5888, cite campaign 26PACL / NHTSA 26V744, and book the software update.
- Drivers: if your truck brakes with nothing ahead of it, file the event with NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation. The 347 reports in EA25006 are the investigation's data supply; unreported events do not exist to the regulator.
- Buyers of 2027 PACCAR trucks: ask whether the radar software update (26PACL) has been applied before taking delivery, and ask which radar generation the truck ships with.
- Do not use the NHTSA VIN lookup as proof before January 10, 2027: the VINs are not searchable until that date, and a "0 recalls" result before then means the database has not loaded the campaign, not that the truck is clear.
