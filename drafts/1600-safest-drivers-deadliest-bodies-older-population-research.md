## Story in one line
Older drivers are the safest drivers on American roads by every behavioral measure NHTSA tracks — lowest fatal-crash rate per licensed driver, least speeding, least drunk, most belted, daytime, weekday — yet 8,019 people 65 and older died in 2024, one in five of all traffic deaths, and the count is up 29% in a decade. The killer isn't their driving. It's their bodies, their numbers, and the left turn.

## Core numbers (NHTSA DOT HS 813 839, October 2026 — FARS 2024 ARF, unless noted)
- **8,019 people 65+ killed in 2024**, 20% of all traffic fatalities (39,254 total). Up 1% vs 2023 (7,911); **up 29% vs 2015 (6,237)**. Male deaths +33%, female +21% over the decade.
- **61.2 million people 65+** (18% of US population, up from 15% in 2015). Population 65+ grew 28% in a decade vs 6% overall.
- **54.4 million licensed older drivers**, **23% of all licensed drivers** (18% in 2015; +36% in a decade vs +10% overall).
- **Fatality rate per 100,000 population, 65+: 13.11** (down 2% vs 2023's 13.33; flat vs 13.09 in 2015). Under-65 rate: 11.13 (up 4% since 2015). **85+ rate: 17.31, highest of any older subgroup, up 9.3% in a single year** (2023→2024). 80–84: 16.00. 65–69 rate rose 6.3% over the decade (11.30 → 12.01).
- **Fatal-crash involvement per 100,000 licensed drivers, 65+: 15.60 — the LOWEST of any age group.** Male 65+: 23.48. Female 65+: 8.44. (Youngest groups run 2–3.5x higher; figure 4 peaks at 56.72 for young males.) Within 65+: 85+ males 29.69, 85+ females 10.91 — the highest of any older subgroup, still below the young-driver rates.
- **Alcohol: 10% of older drivers in fatal crashes had BAC .08+**, vs 23% for drivers 21–64. By subgroup: 65–69 at 14%, falling steadily to 85+ at 6%. 87% of older drivers in fatal crashes had BAC .00.
- **Speeding: 8% of older drivers in fatal crashes were speeding — lowest of any age group** (overall 19%; young groups ~30%).
- **Restraint: 70% of killed 65+ passenger-vehicle occupants were restrained** (known use), vs 46% for under-65. Survivors: 94% vs 86%. Older women killed: 78% restrained vs 53% under-65. Daytime: 72% vs 51%.
- **Crash signature: 71% daytime, 69% weekday, 66% multi-vehicle** (vs 46%/59%/46% for all fatalities). **33% of older-driver fatal crashes at intersections** vs 24% for 15–64. **Left-side initial impact: 15% vs 10%**; right-side 9% vs 6%; front 59% vs 64%. **52% of older drivers killed on rural roads** vs 48% urban — the reverse of younger drivers (47% rural / 53% urban).
- **8,626 people died in crashes involving older drivers in 2024** (+29% vs 6,668 in 2015; +1.4% vs 2023). Of those: 4,917 were the older drivers themselves, 792 were older passengers, 1,735 were occupants of other vehicles, 963 were nonoccupants.
- **Older pedestrian deaths: 1,535 in 2024, up 49% since 2015** (male +55%, female +40%). 67% at non-intersection locations (vs 84% under-65). 16% of older pedestrians killed had BAC .08+ vs 30% for 15–64.
- **Older pedalcyclist deaths: 237 in 2024, up 130% since 2015**; men +160% (221 deaths). Starting 2022, pedalcyclists include motorized bicycles.
- **States:** Florida had the most older drivers in fatal crashes (696); DC the fewest (5). North Dakota had the highest SHARE of older drivers in fatal crashes (23.8%); DC lowest (8.2%), then Vermont (10.6%), Delaware (10.8%).

## Original contribution (for the article)
1. **Safest drivers, deadliest bodies.** The same fact sheet proves both halves: lowest fatal-crash involvement per licensed driver (15.60) AND a higher per-capita death rate than the under-65 population (13.11 vs 11.13). The contradiction resolves only through fragility: older drivers crash less, but die more when they do. No prior article has put the two rates side by side.
2. **The left-turn signature.** The 15%-vs-10% left-side impact gap and 33%-vs-24% intersection share quantify the gap-judgment failure mode in one place — the specific crash older drivers die in is the unprotected left across oncoming traffic, not the highway pileup.
3. **The 85+ cliff.** The 85+ fatality rate (17.31) jumped 9.3% in one year while every other older subgroup fell — the cliff is getting steeper, not flatter, even as the overall 65+ rate ticked down.
4. **The sex gap that never closes.** Older men crash at nearly 3x the rate of older women per licensed driver (23.48 vs 8.44) — the male risk premium survives into the 80s.
5. **The pedestrian/cyclist blind spot.** Older pedestrian deaths (+49%) and older male cyclist deaths (+160%) grew far faster than older driver deaths (+26%) — the danger to older people isn't only behind the wheel.

## Limitations (must appear in article)
- 2024 figures are from the FARS Annual Report File (ARF), not the Final File — counts may be revised slightly when finalized.
- Per-licensed-driver rates use FHWA licensed-driver counts; they don't adjust for miles driven per driver. Older drivers drive fewer miles, so per-mile involvement rates are higher than per-driver rates suggest (IIHS per-mile data shows the rise from 70–74 onward).
- The fact sheet's restraint percentages exclude unknown restraint use; "when restraint use was known" is doing real work in those numbers.
- State shares (e.g., North Dakota 23.8%) reflect small denominators and older state populations, not necessarily more dangerous older driving.

## Strongest counterargument (must appear in article)
The honest case against the "safest drivers" framing: per-driver rates flatter older drivers because they drive fewer miles and self-restrict to daytime, weekday, familiar roads. Per MILE, IIHS shows fatal-crash involvement climbing from age 70–74 and peaking at 85+. An 85-year-old driving 3,000 miles a year to the grocery store and back is not safer per mile than a 40-year-old commuting 15,000 — the exposure is just smaller. The article's claim is about behavior and per-driver risk, not per-mile risk, and the per-mile picture is worse. Say that plainly.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- If you have a parent 75+ who drives: the risk isn't speeding or drinking — it's the unprotected left turn and the intersection. Route planning (right-turn-only routes, avoiding unprotected lefts) matters more than another lecture about speed.
- Vehicle choice for older drivers: side-impact protection and AEB with intersection support matter more than horsepower; the left-side impact overrepresentation is the spec sheet to shop against.
- Families: 85+ is the cliff — the 9.3% one-year rate jump says the conversation about driving should happen before 85, not after.
- Older pedestrians and cyclists: 1,535 older pedestrian deaths, up 49% in a decade — reflective gear and intersection timing matter as much for the walker as the driver.
