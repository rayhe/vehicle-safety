NHTSA Filed a Federal Recall for Exactly One Range Rover Sport. The Paperwork Outweighs the Repair.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 29, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better, because the number is one. On September 23, 2026, NHTSA logged campaign 26V607000: Jaguar Land Rover North America is recalling one 2026 Range Rover Sport, not one thousand, just one car.[1] Somewhere in America, a single SUV is driving around with a circuit board inside its powertrain control module that was misaligned during production and may now be deformed, and that single board was enough to set the entire federal recall machine in motion.

1
Vehicles covered by federal recall campaign 26V607000, the smallest possible recall population

Consider what n=1 buys you in the federal system: a campaign number, a Part 573 defect report on file with the Office of Defects Investigation, and an interim owner notification letter expected to be mailed November 20, 2026, addressed to a single household, warning of a safety risk in a vehicle the owner presumably drives every day.[1] Then quarterly status reports, because the law requires them for every campaign no matter how small the denominator.[3] The administrative tail is longer than the recall itself.

What failed is specific and physical. The powertrain control module is the computer that manages the engine and transmission, and its circuit board went into the housing crooked at the factory. A deformed board can interrupt the signals that keep the drivetrain engaged, which means the SUV can lose drive power at speed with no warning.[1] JLR's remedy is refreshingly simple: a dealer will replace the whole module, free of charge, under internal campaign number D164.

Zoom out and the single car sits inside a crowded year. Counting the September 23 filing, the 2026 Range Rover Sport now carries five distinct federal campaigns in 2026: a misprinted certification label (26V005), detaching panoramic sunroof trim (26V097), a seatbelt warning chime that fails FMVSS 208 (26V297), this one-car PCM filing (26V607), and rear subframe cracks filed exactly one day later (26V613).[1][2] Five campaigns. One model year. The smallest and the scariest arrived within 24 hours of each other.

Now the defense, because the numbers deserve it. Federal law requires manufacturers to report every safety-related defect regardless of population size, and a campaign of one is arguably the system at its best: JLR traced a single deformed board to a single VIN instead of shrugging at the whole production run.[3] That traceability is genuinely hard. It means the supplier records, the build sequencing, and the board-level quality data all lined up well enough to isolate one car out of thousands. Mock the paperwork all you want; that paperwork is what found the car.

What to do with this: if you own a 2026 Range Rover Sport, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today.[3] The odds that you own the one car in 26V607 are effectively zero, but the other four campaigns are not a joke, and the subframe one has no final remedy yet. Recalls are free, and that same federal apparatus will happily process your VIN too, even if you are merely one in thousands instead of one in one.

What this article did not prove: NHTSA's public record for 26V607 lists no crashes, injuries, or fires, which means none are listed, not that none happened. How JLR discovered the misaligned board (supplier self-report, dealer finding, or field incident) is not in the public summary, and this piece does not guess. The five-campaign count for the 2026 Range Rover Sport comes from NHTSA's recalls-by-vehicle API response on September 29, 2026; campaigns can be superseded or expanded after publication. Interim letter dates are manufacturer estimates, not guarantees.
