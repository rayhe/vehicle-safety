Tesla’s Driver-Assist Was On in Three More Fatal Crashes. The Public Was Told Nothing.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

A 2025 Tesla Model X left Golden Gate Parkway in Naples, Florida, at 11:43 p.m. on September 30, hit a traffic signal pole, and rolled over. A 50-year-old woman from Fort Myers, riding as a passenger, died at the scene. Her driver survived with minor injuries. Florida Highway Patrol called it a car that veered off the road. Tesla’s own report to the federal government says Autopilot or Full Self-Driving was verified engaged.[1]

264
Tesla driver-assist crashes reported to NHTSA in September 2026, a new monthly record

September set a record, with Tesla reporting 264 driver-assist crashes to NHTSA that month, beating July’s 236 by 28, which means the monthly record for the company’s federally reported crashes keeps climbing even as the fleet ages and the software matures. Electrek’s Fred Lambert, matching redacted federal filings against local crash reports since September, has now identified 12 fatal crashes claiming 16 lives, a toll that includes drivers, passengers, a man who stopped to help at a crash scene, and a motorcyclist. Three more surfaced on November 16 alone.[2]

Tesla knows more about the Naples crash than anyone. It detected the crash through its own telematics before anyone called it in, and its filing checks every box for evidence held: event data recorder, telematics, video, and other, a completeness almost no other crash in this investigation can claim. It knows whether the system steered the SUV off the road, whether the car warned the driver, and how fast it was going when it hit the pole. It filed all of that as confidential business information. The narrative and the software version are redacted, and so is whether the road was even inside the system’s operating domain. Lambert notes Tesla gives 99.9 percent of its reports the same treatment, and that it is the only high-volume reporter withholding all the details he seeks.[1]

Naples appears twice in the same NHTSA release. In May, a Model 3 with the system engaged at 71 mph hit an overturned box truck on I-75 at Pine Ridge Road, killing three people, two passengers and a man who had stopped to help. Troopers investigated both crashes, and neither was ever publicly tied to Autopilot or FSD. A third crash in the November 16 batch, a Model Y in El Dorado Hills, California, left a parkway, hit a tree, and caught fire, killing three of the four people inside, and local media reported it without mentioning a driver-assist system, just as it had the other two.[2]

Then there is the timestamp. Tesla logged the Naples crash at 03:41 UTC on October 1, which is 11:41 p.m. Florida time on September 30, two minutes off the patrol’s 11:43, a bookkeeping choice that quietly moves a September crash into October’s column. Because the UTC stamp crosses midnight, Tesla’s record files the crash under October. Anyone reconciling monthly counts will undercount September and never know it. September’s record 264 may itself be an undercount.[1]

Now the case for the defense, because it is real. Tesla complied with the law, filing every crash under the Standing General Order, on time, with engagement verified by its own telemetry, which is exactly what the order asks for and exactly what a regulator can demand. Redactions are lawful confidential-business-information claims, and NHTSA’s public data carries the same exclusions for every manufacturer. Engagement within 30 seconds of impact is not causation, and the patrol, not a spreadsheet, determines what caused the Naples crash, which means the honest headline is narrower than the alarming one and every reader should know that before drawing conclusions. Fair enough, but compliance that hides the ball is still hiding the ball, and Tesla publishes its data eagerly when it flatters the company, as it did after the Katy crash. When a carmaker holds the video of a fatal crash and calls it a trade secret, people will assume the worst, and Tesla only has itself to blame for that.

If you drive a Tesla with Autopilot or FSD, the system being on does not make the car self-driving, and in a fatal crash the public record may never mention it was engaged. Treat every mile as yours, because legally and practically, it is. If you investigate or report a Tesla crash, check NHTSA’s Standing General Order data by VIN and remember Tesla logs in UTC, so a late-night crash can land in the next day and the next month. Seven more dead, and the only public sentence about the Naples crash was that a car veered off the road, which is true in the way a weather report is true and useless in exactly the same way.
