Traffic Deaths Fell by 425. The Two Official Counters Disagree by 1,710.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 16, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better. NHTSA says 16,750 people died on American roads in the first half of 2026. The National Safety Council says 18,460. Same six months, same country, and two official answers 1,710 lives apart.[1][2]

1,710
Deaths separating the two official counts of the same six months, January through June 2026

Run the gap against the good news and the good news shrinks. NHTSA's headline improvement is 425 fewer deaths than the first half of 2025, a 2.5 percent decline that the agency calls the second-lowest first-half fatality rate on record. Divide 1,710 by 425 and the disagreement between the counters is 4.02 times the decline being celebrated. Per day, the gap is 9.4 deaths that one counter counts and the other does not, against an improvement of 2.3 a day. Four phantom funerals a day for every one life actually saved, on the counters' own arithmetic.[1][2]

Both counters publish their definitions openly, which is how you know the gap is structural and not a typo. NHTSA's FARS counts traffic deaths, meaning crashes on public roads, in which someone died within 30 days. NSC's preliminary estimate counts deaths within 100 days of the crash, on public and private roadways alike, parking lots and driveways included, built from National Center for Health Statistics data. Its final count stretches the window to a full year. NSC's own comparison page itemizes the difference: FARS omits 800 to 1,000 deaths a year that occur more than 30 days after a crash, plus 900 to 1,900 nontraffic deaths a year. Add the two ranges and you get 1,700 to 2,900 a year.[4]

Annualize the half-year gap and it overshoots that explanation. 1,710 times two is 3,420 a year, about 520 above the top of NSC's own 1,700-to-2,900 range. And remember the preliminary estimate uses a 100-day window, narrower than the one-year final window the decomposition describes, which should make the late-death component smaller, not larger. Either the preliminary estimate runs hot, or a third difference nobody has itemized is hiding in the mix. I checked the arithmetic three times, and it is not a rounding error. It is an open question, and neither agency's press release mentions it.[3][4]

Check the gap across halves and it barely moves, which is the tell that this is a fixed feature of the system and not noise. NCHS final data put 20,278 deaths in the first half of 2024, against NHTSA's 18,681, a gap of 1,597. First half of 2025 gave a gap of 1,671, NSC's revised 18,846 against NHTSA's 17,175, and the first half of 2026 gave 1,710. Three halves, three gaps, all within 113 of each other, while both counters fell by hundreds in between. A discrepancy that stable is not measurement error. It is a definition, wearing a trench coat. The Center for Auto Safety has documented the same pattern for fifteen years: NSC historically counts about 2,000 more motor-vehicle deaths a year than FARS, mostly non-traffic deaths and deaths past thirty days.[3][6]

Credit where it is due: both counters agree on direction, and direction is the part that matters most. NSC says deaths fell 2 percent; NHTSA says 2.5 percent. Both put the increase in miles driven at 0.8 percent. Rates differ, 1.12 per hundred million miles for NSC against 1.02 for NHTSA, but both rates fell, and both fell by roughly the same share. If you need a trend line, either counter will do. If you need a body count, pick one and say which, because the level is contested even when the slope is not. One more wrinkle from NHTSA's own tables: May and June were the only two months that rose year over year, so the improvement is already nine months old and getting older.[1][2]

Now the case for the defense, at full strength, because it is a good one. Nobody is lying: both definitions are published, both figures are labeled estimates, and both will be revised when the final files land. NHTSA's 30-day rule exists so all fifty states report the same way, and the agency estimates 98 percent of crash deaths happen inside thirty days anyway. A policymaker who needs a consistent trend across decades is right to use NHTSA. A public-health researcher who wants every death is right to use NSC. But that defense has a limit, and the limit is arithmetic: when the celebrated improvement is a quarter of the definitional gap, quoting either number to the digit is false precision, and leading with the decline without naming the fog is incomplete reporting.[5]

What to do with this: never quote a single traffic-death toll as gospel. If a politician, a press release, or a news story says 16,750 people died, ask which counter, because the other official answer is 18,460. If you work in safety policy, pick your counter on purpose, NHTSA for trend consistency across years and states, NSC or NCHS for the fullest count of lives lost. And know what the 30-day rule costs in human terms. A crash victim who survives more than thirty days in a hospital and then dies is not in the federal count at all. Watch the nine-month projections due in late December. If the third quarter repeats the second quarter's near-flat 0.2 percent decline, the second-lowest-rate-ever headline will be resting on a first quarter that is already a year old.[1]
