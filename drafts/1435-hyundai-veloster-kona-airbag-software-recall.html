The Deadliest Car in America and One of the Safest Just Got the Same Recall

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Editor
November 8, 2026

Hyundai filed recall 26V728 with NHTSA on November 2, covering 37,901 cars.[1] A software error in the airbag control unit can corrupt the unit’s own memory when the system reboots or loses power mid-write. Corrupted memory lights the airbag warning lamp and deactivates the airbags and the seat-belt pretensioners, no crash required and no warning beyond a small amber icon most drivers learn to ignore.

71x
Fatality-rate gap between the Veloster (8.54) and the Kona (0.12) per 100M VMT in FARS 2014–2023 — same manufacturer, same airbag defect

Start with the two cars sharing this bug, because they could not be further apart. FARS puts the Hyundai Veloster at 8.54 deaths per 100 million miles, the highest rate of any model in our 337-model dataset, on 598 deaths across 884 fatal crashes.[3] Its drivers are not unusually impaired: 17.4 percent tested positive for alcohol or drugs, below the fleet average. This is not a drunk-driving story; it is a car story.

Now the Kona. Same manufacturer, same recall, same airbag computer. FARS puts the Kona at 0.12 deaths per 100 million miles, on 73 deaths across 136 fatal crashes, among the lowest rates in the dataset.[3] Divide 8.54 by 0.12 and you get 71. One defect now spans a 71-fold spread in fatality rate, which means the bug does not care how safe your car was before it arrived. It deletes the airbag either way.

Inside the box, the failure is almost elegant. An airbag control unit writes data to its own memory constantly, and a reboot or power dip in the middle of a write can scramble that memory.[2] Scrambled memory trips the malfunction light and the system stands down. Hyundai noticed the pattern in August 2024, when airbag warning-light warranty claims started climbing, and by October 2026 it had 331 reports. Zero crashes, zero injuries, and zero deaths tied to the glitch, which is the good news and also the reason nobody noticed for two years.

Zoom out one week and the pattern sharpens. GM recalled 67,380 electric Cadillacs and Chevrolets the same week for brake software that can cut braking, and Toyota recalled 228,367 Siennas for a seat-belt software fix.[1] Three recalls, three software patches, zero wrenches, and cars that used to fail at welds and hoses now fail at writes and reboots, with the fix arriving as a download at a dealership.

Give Hyundai its due, because the system worked here. A warranty-claim trend surfaced the defect, the company investigated for two years, and it recalled 37,901 discontinued cars before a single injury. That is the recall system doing its job. But a single point of failure for every restraint in the car, a failure mode that hides behind a dashboard light, and owner letters that do not go out until January 1 add up to a thin margin. A corrupted airbag computer needs exactly one crash to matter.

Read the fine print before you panic or relax. FARS counts whole nameplates, so the Veloster’s 8.54 covers every Veloster trim and the Kona’s 0.12 covers every Kona, gas and electric; the recall covers only the Veloster N and the Kona Electric. Hyundai has not said how many of the 37,901 units are actually corrupted. FARS ends in 2023, so none of this measures the last three years of driving.

Check your VIN today at nhtsa.gov/recalls if you own a 2019–2022 Veloster N or a 2019–2023 Kona Electric. Do not wait for the January letter.[5] If your airbag light is on in any car, get it diagnosed now: in this defect, that light means the system has switched itself off. Dealers reflash the control unit free, no parts, no hardware, one visit. Both models are discontinued, so every one left on the road is a used car, and a VIN check is the only way to know whether the computer inside it still remembers how to save you.
