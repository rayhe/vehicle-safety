Four Stars Up Front, Marginal in the Back: The 2026 GMC Canyon's IIHS Report Card

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. In IIHS's updated moderate-overlap test, a 2026 GMC Canyon AT4 hits a deformable barrier at 40 mph with 40 percent of its width, and two dummies ride along: one driving, one in the back seat. The driver dummy came through beautifully, with head, neck, and chest all rated Good. Structure held with zero A-pillar movement.[1] Then the cameras swung to the rear dummy, whose head pitched forward toward the front seatback hard enough to drag the whole rating down to Marginal, the second-worst score IIHS gives.

Crash dummies NHTSA has ever seated in a back seat for a frontal test

That zero is the whole story. When GM was asked about the result, spokesman Mark Lubin told the Detroit Free Press the company was "confident in the safety of 2026 GMC Canyon that achieved a four-star rating in NHTSA's New Car Assessment Program."[2] Sounds reassuring. There is just one problem: NHTSA's frontal test seats dummies in the driver and front-passenger positions only. No federal frontal crash test has ever put a dummy in the rear.[8] GM defended a back-seat failure by citing a test that cannot see the back seat.

IIHS added the rear dummy to this test in 2022 for exactly this reason. Front-seat protection had improved for decades while rear-seat engineering quietly stagnated, and the updated protocol finally made automakers answer for the second row. Where the industry's effort went is written all over the Canyon's scorecard: small overlap front Good, updated side test Good, structure and safety cage Good. Rear passenger head and neck, Marginal, with a chest index of 40 and shoulder-belt tension hitting 5.7 kN.[4] Belts stayed positioned over the pelvis, yet the head did not stay where it belonged.

Worse than the Marginal is the Poor: the Canyon's standard front crash prevention system earned IIHS's lowest rating in the updated vehicle-to-vehicle test, which runs at higher speeds than the old protocol, because the system "failed to consistently mitigate collisions."[3] A Marginal rear-seat score is about injury risk once the crash is already happening. A Poor prevention score means the truck's standard automatic emergency braking couldn't reliably stop the crash from happening at all, on a 2026 model year vehicle, when this technology is supposed to be mature. Pedestrian prevention managed an Acceptable; the car-to-car system, the one that prevents the highway pileup, did not.

Some honest context from our own data: the Canyon nameplate's FARS record for 2014 through 2023 shows 209 occupant deaths across 385 fatal crash involvements, a rate of 0.59 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles.[6] That is unremarkable. Its mechanical twin, the Chevrolet Colorado, sits at 0.28; the Nissan Frontier at 1.45; the Ford Ranger at 2.91. But nearly all of that FARS history belongs to the previous-generation truck, and FARS cannot split deaths by seating position anyway. The failure mode IIHS just found is invisible to both the federal star rating and the federal fatality database, and that invisibility is the finding.

Fairness requires the other side at full strength, because GM's statement isn't empty. In most crash modes this is a genuinely protective truck: Good structure, Good driver measures, Good side protection, and a 4-star NCAP that reflects real sled testing rather than vibes. That Marginal is narrow, confined to rear-dummy head excursion in one specific offset scenario, and crew-cab rear seats disproportionately carry children in car seats, cargo, or nobody, which shrinks the real-world exposure. That Poor comes from a closed-course test of the standard system; optioned trims differ, sensors get dirty, software gets updated. IIHS's updated protocols are deliberately brutal, and most of the industry is still catching up. Five of the seven vehicles in this test batch failed to earn an award, including a Lexus that flunked the same test on the same rear-seat vulnerability.[3]

All of which misses the structural point. Understand this: the window sticker's stars and the IIHS report card are grading different vehicles. One of them has never looked at the back seat. If you regularly carry passengers back there, kids in boosters, the carpool, actual adult humans, the rating you need is the one GM just dismissed: the updated moderate overlap rear-seat subscores, model by model, at iihs.org/ratings.[7] Check those before you check the stars. And if you're cross-shopping the Canyon against its twin, note IIHS's fine print: these ratings do not apply to the Chevrolet Colorado, so look it up separately. Never assume the badge on the tailgate tells you what's behind the back seat.
