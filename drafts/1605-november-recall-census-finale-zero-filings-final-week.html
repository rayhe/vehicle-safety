By The Numbers

November Filed Nothing in Its Final Week. The Census Closes at 2,225,873.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. This time they came back the same, which is the story. Zero recall campaigns were filed November 21 through 27, 2026, covering zero vehicles, and the month closes exactly where last week’s census left it: 38 campaigns, 2,225,873 vehicles, with 20 of them touching a 2026 model, 202,010 vehicles, 9.1 percent.[1][2]

0
Recall campaigns filed November 21–27, 2026: seven days, zero filings, zero vehicles.

November filed in bursts, then stopped. Week one, November 2 through 6, filed 15 campaigns and 918,098 vehicles, 41.2 percent of the month. Week two, November 9 through 13, filed 14 campaigns and 410,216 vehicles. Week three, November 16 through 20, filed 9 campaigns and 897,559 vehicles, and week four filed nothing. Two days own the month: November 4, when BMW filed 720,796 cars with a water-pump fire risk, and November 19, when seven campaigns filed 896,048 vehicles, 40.3 percent of the month in a single day. Together those two days are 1,621,044 vehicles, 72.8 percent of everything November filed.[1][2]

New cars kept disappearing from the census as the month went on. Week one’s 2026-touching campaigns covered 128,288 vehicles, 14.0 percent of the week’s filings. Week two covered 70,577, 17.2 percent. Week three covered 3,145, which is 0.35 percent, and week four covered none. Across the whole month, 20 campaigns touch a 2026 vehicle, 202,010 vehicles, 9.1 percent. By campaign count the split is nearly even, 20 of 38, but by vehicles it is 9.1 percent, and the vehicle share is the headline because it is what owners experience. Half the month is still two Takata campaigns: Toyota’s 314,029 inflators from November 12 and Honda’s 810,592 from November 19 total 1,124,621, which is 50.5 percent of everything filed, and both are about propellant degrading in airbags that have spent more than a decade baking in dashboards.[1]

Last week’s census corrected the one before it by 55 percent. Twelve campaigns filed November 2 through 12 were not in the data when the first census ran, and they added 471,509 vehicles to the count. This week’s recount, run from scratch on November 27 across 78 makes and 3,978 queries, added nothing: no campaign, no vehicle, no correction. Zero is the first correction of zero in this series, and it is worth saying plainly what it does and does not prove. It proves the November 1 through 20 totals held for 24 hours. It does not prove they will hold for ten days, because the last correction took ten days to materialize, and a filing dated November 24 that gets published November 28 is invisible to this census by construction.[1]

Meanwhile the week’s recall headlines were all reruns. Vehicle Service Pros published its November 23 through 27 roundup on November 27, and all five campaigns in it were filed in earlier weeks: the Honda Takata recall from November 19, the Ram ABS recall from November 19, the Explorer map-light recall from November 2, the Rivian placard recall from November 19, and the BMW mirror recall from November 19. MotorSafety announced a Toyota RAV4 plug-in hybrid recall on November 24, 30,978 vehicles that may lose power at speed, and the federal filing date on that campaign is October 7, which makes it 48 days old as a filing and an October campaign by this census’s definition. Autoblog’s Genesis GV80 story ran November 27 for a recall filed November 20. Not one recall headline in the final week was about a filing from the final week, because there were no filings in the final week.[3][4]

November 21 through 27 is Thanksgiving week. Thanksgiving fell on Thursday, November 26, and the filing-free week lines up with the holiday week, which is a fact worth reporting and a cause worth not claiming. NHTSA publishes a recall a few days after the manufacturer reports it, so a filing drought and a publication drought look identical in this data, and the mirror’s newest campaign was still 26V766 as of November 26 at 9:50 PM UTC. Seven straight days with no filings is the longest dry stretch of the month; the previous longest was four days, November 14 through 17, which included a weekend. Whether the holiday caused the gap, or the gap just happened to land on the holiday, the record cannot say.[2]

Fairness demands the other side, stated at full strength. Every share in this article is a snapshot of published data, not a final total, and the correction history proves the point: the first census undercounted by 35.5 percent because of publication lag, and this census undercounts by an unknown amount for the same reason. A campaign filed November 24 and published November 28 would make the zero wrong, and the correction would belong in a December piece. Campaign populations are full-campaign totals, not 2026-only slices, so 202,010 overstates the number of 2026 vehicles; the 9.1 percent is an upper bound, not the share itself. And the month’s biggest number is not the month’s biggest risk: a 2012 Civic with a rupturing passenger inflator and a 2026 GV80 with a detaching ball joint are not comparable hazards, and a Takata count of 1.1 million does not make November more dangerous than a month with one small recall of a steering knuckle that can snap at speed. All of that is true, and none of it changes the shape of the data, which is that November’s recall system spent the month fixing cars from a decade ago, then filed nothing at all in its final week.[1]

What do you do with this? If you own a 2026 model, the month filed 20 campaigns that touch one, all of them by November 20, and the full list runs from a 330-Bronco wiper nut to a 62,490-truck Tundra camshaft; check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because every remedy is free. If you own a 2012 through 2018 BMW, the park-outside water-pump recall has no remedy yet, so park it outside, away from buildings, until the fix exists. If you own a 2012 or 2013 Honda or Acura, 810,592 cars carry Takata inflators that can rupture and throw metal fragments, interim letters go out December 18, and the final remedy letter comes later, so check the VIN now instead of waiting for the mail. If you own a 2026 RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid, the power-loss recall was filed in October, not November, but the fix is a free dealer software update and owner letters started November 22. And the general lesson, third verse: a recall announced in November is usually about a car you bought years ago, and a recall announced in the last week of November is usually about a filing from the first three weeks.[1][5]
