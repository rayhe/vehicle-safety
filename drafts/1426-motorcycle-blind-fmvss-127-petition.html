The Federal Brake Test Has Never Seen a Motorcycle. Riders Just Filed to Change That.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

Somewhere in Washington, a federal test is teaching cars to brake for everything except the vehicle most likely to die in front of them. On October 29, the Motorcycle Riders Foundation and a coalition of national rider groups sent NHTSA and Congress a formal list of priorities, and among them was a request so basic it reads like a typo: when the government writes the test for automatic emergency braking, include a motorcycle.[1] Not a new sensor or a new law, just a target, something for the car to aim its brakes at.

Here is what the federal mandate actually requires. FMVSS 127, finalized in May 2024, says an automatic emergency braking system must detect and react to an imminent crash with a lead vehicle or a pedestrian.[3] Its compliance tests use a lead car, at speeds up to 62 mph, and pedestrian dummies in daylight and darkness. Two targets, a car and a person on foot, and that is the whole cast. A motorcycle, a two-wheeled vehicle with a rider sitting on top of it in plain view, appears nowhere in the standard. Not in the test procedures, not in the scenarios, not as a footnote. NHTSA projects the rule will save at least 360 lives a year once every new car complies.[3] Six thousand two hundred twenty-eight motorcyclists died in 2024.[4] Do the division and the annual motorcycle body count is seventeen times the rule's entire projected benefit, and none of those riders are in the benefit case, because the test never sees them.

Maybe the test is right to skip them, and maybe cars already see bikes fine while the paperwork just never caught up, so IIHS ran that experiment. Its updated vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention evaluation, the one that decides whether a car earns a safety award, includes a motorcycle target alongside the passenger car target and the semitrailer.[6] When IIHS published its first commercial-vehicle ratings on October 8, the motorcycle runs were the embarrassment of the session. Several systems did not issue warnings or slow at all in most of the motorcycle trials. One van, the Rivian delivery van, avoided the car target in four of six runs and warned in every car and semitrailer run, then recorded zero speed reduction and zero timely warnings in all six motorcycle runs.[6] Same sensors. Same van. Same afternoon. Four out of six for a sedan, zero out of six for a bike, because it does not fail at braking, it fails at seeing.

Riders are three percent of registered vehicles and sixteen percent of traffic deaths.[4] Their fatality rate per mile, 28.00 per 100 million miles, sits next to the passenger car occupant rate of 1.05, and the gap is the whole story of American road risk compressed into one ratio. Now consider what a compliance test does, because it does not merely measure a car. It teaches a manufacturer what to optimize, what to validate, what to put in the training data and what to leave out. A federal test with two targets tells every automaker in the country that two targets are the job. A motorcycle is not a third target, it is a rounding error with a rider on it.

Fairness requires the strongest case against this story, stated straight, because it is a good case. Most fatal motorcycle crashes are not a car rear-ending a bike. The dominant pattern is a car turning left across an oncoming motorcycle, about 26 percent of fatal two-vehicle motorcycle crashes from 2017 to 2021,[9] a geometry no forward-facing brake test would catch, motorcycle target or no motorcycle target. And the 360 lives a year is the incremental benefit of mandating brakes on the cars that do not have them yet, not the total value of automatic braking, since most new cars already ship with it. Comparing 6,228 rider deaths to 360 incremental lives overstates the gap, and this article will not pretend otherwise. Here is the honest version of the claim, narrower and harder to dismiss: a compliance test that never shows a motorcycle teaches manufacturers that motorcycles are optional in sensor validation, and the voluntary test that does show one keeps catching systems that cannot see it, and that is the argument. Left-turn crashes are the limit of it, not the refutation.

What this piece did not prove, stated plainly. The October 29 filing arrived as a formal list of priorities sent to NHTSA and Congress, and the available reporting does not specify its exact form, so no docket number is claimed here. The 2024 motorcycle figures come from the FARS Annual Report File and may be revised in the Final File. The Rivian result comes from the commercial-vehicle ratings, vans and Class 3 pickups, not light passenger cars, and the article names the vehicle rather than generalizing to every car on the road. And the rider groups' ask is not new: the Motorcycle Riders Foundation put it in its 2026 legislative priorities a year ago, calling for rulemaking on standards "specific to seeing, detecting, and properly reacting to" motorcycles.[2] What is new is the delivery. NHTSA published its automated-vehicle safety and lighting frameworks in late October, and the riders filed their answer within days, while the comment window is still open.

What you can actually do with this starts with your bike: if you ride, check its VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. NHTSA ran a Fall 2026 recall campaign for riders from October 26 through November 1, and the check is free year-round, because on a bike there is no steel cage and no seat belt to buy you a second chance when a known defect lets go. If you buy cars, look at the motorcycle line in IIHS's vehicle-to-vehicle ratings, not just the overall score. A system rated Good overall can still be blind to a two-wheeler, and the rating page will tell you if you scroll far enough. And if you comment on federal rulemaking, the FMVSS 108 docket, NHTSA-2026-1519,[5] stays open until about November 25. The riders already filed, so the rest of us can read what they wrote and decide whether a brake test with two targets is a safety standard or a suggestion.
