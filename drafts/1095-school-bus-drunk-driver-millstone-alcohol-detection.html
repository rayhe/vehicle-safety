The School Bus Driver Blew a 0.161. Nobody Made Him Blow Into Anything First.

Dale Impactor III • Toxicology Desk
July 10, 2027

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, school buses are supposed to be the safest vehicles on the road. On March 4, 2024, one rolled over on State Highway 16 near Millstone, West Virginia, with nineteen students aboard, and its driver tested at 0.161, more than twice the legal limit.[1] Three students were seriously injured; sixteen more were hurt. Nobody asked that driver to blow into anything before turning the key, because no federal rule requires it.

118
School bus drivers cited or arrested for operating impaired, 2015 to 2019, in a Stateline study cited by the NTSB

In April 2026 the National Transportation Safety Board published its findings, and the cause line reads the way the number reads: the driver's alcohol impairment caused the crash.[1] Then the board went further than assigning cause. It recommended that NHTSA require vehicle-integrated alcohol detection systems on every new school bus in America.[1]

This is not vaporware: active systems that measure breath and passive systems that detect impairment already exist, and they are already riding on school buses in parts of Europe as a preventive measure.[1] Europe puts a sobriety check between the driver and the ignition of the bus carrying its children. America does not, and NTSB investigators noticed the gap even though they cannot close it. That decision belongs to NHTSA, which has yet to act.

Meanwhile, the American system checks your breath only after it catches you. Ignition interlocks have prevented 5.7 million drunk driving attempts since 2006, about one every 95 seconds, and MADD wants one on the car of every convicted drunk driver in the country, first offense included.[2] Call it conviction-gated: get caught, then prove you are sober every morning. What the NTSB wants is the job-gated version, because Millstone's driver was never convicted of anything. That is exactly why nothing stood between him and the ignition.

Here is the honest objection, and it is arithmetic: a Stateline census found 118 school bus drivers cited or arrested for impaired operation across five years.[1] That averages roughly two dozen a year against a workforce of around half a million drivers moving billions of student-miles. Rare event, vast fleet, and detection hardware costs money that budget-squeezed school districts do not have. Fair, but it is also the same arithmetic that classified Millstone as a low-probability event, which is a strange comfort when the bus is already on its side on Highway 16.

That same report renewed the NTSB's call for lap-and-shoulder belts on school buses, citing a similar crash in Dale, Texas.[1] Compartmentalization, the tall padded seats that let school buses skip belts, works when the crash is frontal. In a rollover, the kids become projectiles and the padding becomes scenery. Unbelted students were thrown around the cabin in both crashes.[1] For perspective, the federal government just finished requiring belt reminders for the rear seats of passenger vehicles, and school buses were written out of that rule.[3] So the safest vehicle on the road remains the one where nothing buckles and nobody checks.

What to do with this: ask your school district whether its buses have lap-shoulder belts and how it screens drivers, because neither answer is a federal requirement, though NTSB can recommend even if it cannot command. Somewhere out there is a bus driver who has never been convicted of anything, which under current law means he never has to prove a thing.
