## 1. General Editor — 8.8/10

Lede does its job in three sentences: hook (ran the numbers twice), news (18,460, −2%), thesis (Illinois did a third). The pull stat is the single most shareable number in the piece. Structure is clean: headline number, gross-vs-net decomposition, California reversal, denominator trap, methodology wrinkle, honest close. Deductions: the original headline ("Illinois Did Most of the Work") overstated 34.7% as a majority — fixed to "a Third of the Work" in revision. At ~600 words of prose it runs long for the 300–500 guide, but in line with recent flagship drafts. No filler paragraphs.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0/10

Axle's catchphrase opener is deployed verbatim ("I ran the numbers... then I ran them again") and the voice stays in register throughout: spreadsheet-as-character, contempt for press releases, the "always read the absolute count" sermon. Distinct from Rex (no noir), Mia (no engineering), Dale (no bar jokes). No AI tells found: no "here's the thing," no "delve," no "it's not about X, it's about Y." Rhythm script passes all three metrics. The one soft spot: "The spreadsheet says something ruder" flirts with anthropomorphism, but it lands as personality, not slop.

## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.2/10

No individuals are named, shamed, or blamed. State-level criticism targets aggregate data, not people. The piece explicitly refuses to turn DC's +133% into a crisis narrative (it is 12 deaths) and refuses to explain Illinois's decline causally, which is the honest choice. The California framing ("Illinois did the work; California un-did it") is punchy but stays on the numbers. Limitations are disclosed in the disclaimer and the research notes. No self-congratulation, no moral grandstanding.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.6/10

The 34.7% pull stat is built for screenshots. "California erased Illinois" is the argument people will quote in replies. The denominator-trap section (DC +133% = 12 deaths) is the kind of media-literacy beat that travels. Deduction: statistical roundups are inherently less viral than outrage or paradox pieces, and the NSC-vs-NHTSA scope paragraph, while rigorous, is the least shareable stretch. The kicker "By The Numbers" correctly sets expectations.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10

Every external figure is attributed inline to NSC, NHTSA, or Reuters with a references section carrying five verbatim URLs. No defamation exposure: claims are about published government/NGO statistics. The characterization of the NSC–NHTSA gap is hedged as scope difference ("count different things"), which matches both organizations' published methodologies. The NHTSA Q1 2026 citation uses the press-release page that carries those figures (odd slug, verified from search results). No legal advice, no accusations of wrongdoing against any party.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.8/10

Original contribution: six computations nobody else ran (net-vs-gross decomposition, Illinois's 34.7% share, the CA-erases-IL arithmetic, the small-denominator trap, the NSC–NHTSA 1,706-death scope gap, implied 2024 rate). All arithmetic independently verified. Limitations stated: preliminary data, revision risk in both directions, no causal attribution, scope-gap decomposition not published. Counterargument given at full strength (rate improvement is real, June monthly volatility, preliminary revisions can go down). Deductions: causal explanation of Illinois's decline is absent (acknowledged, not fixable from available data); the "partly a function of who is holding the clipboard" line is suggestive but the exact scope decomposition is unknown (flagged as such).

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0/10

Every percentage is paired with its absolute count (134, 178, 12, 4, 4). The pull stat states its own denominator. The small-denominator trap is called out explicitly rather than exploited. NSC vs NHTSA definitional difference disclosed before the reader can be misled by the two series. One nit: the article says "down 10.4 percent from the first half of 2024" where the source says "from 2024" — in context of an H1 report this is the correct reading, but a pedant could ask; acceptable.
