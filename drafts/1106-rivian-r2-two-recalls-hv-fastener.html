The Rivian R2 Is Four Months Old. It Already Has Two Recalls.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
July 21, 2027

Rivian started handing R2 keys to customers on June 9. Four months later, the make-or-break SUV is on its second federal safety recall.[1][3] The first one was the biggest in company history: 98,828 vehicles, fixed mostly before the letters went out. The second one covers fourteen cars. A human being caught it, not a machine.[1]

14
Vehicles in the R2's second recall, NHTSA 26V625. The first recall, 26V597, covered 98,828. The two campaigns are four months apart, and they tell opposite stories about how cars get fixed.[1][2]

Recall one, NHTSA 26V597, filed September 23. In rare cases, a stability-control prompt could park itself over the rearview camera image when the driver shifted into reverse, which violates the federal rear-visibility standard, FMVSS 111.[2] The fix was software, update 2026.31.40, delivered over the air. By the time Rivian filed the paperwork, 96 percent of affected R2s had already taken it.[2] Your car fixed itself overnight and then the company sent you a letter about it, which is either the future of automotive safety or a bureaucracy cosplaying as one. Both, probably.

Recall two, NHTSA 26V625, is the old kind. Fourteen 2027 R2s, built May 18 through August 27, carry an improperly torqued fastener on the high-voltage battery, the kind of defect that can loosen over time and kill motive power with no warning.[1] Nobody crashed. Nobody got hurt. What makes this one interesting is who found it: a manufacturing operator, on September 17, with their eyes. The automated torque station had been under-tightening the fastener, and the robot needed a human to notice. Rivian investigated for a week, filed a voluntary recall, and reprogrammed the station to reject badly torqued fasteners. All fourteen cars are reportedly already back at service centers, weeks before the legally required owner letters go out on November 29.[1]

The honest read is that both of these are the system working. A line worker spotted a defect, the company spent a week confirming it, filed voluntarily, fixed the station, and retrieved every car before the deadline, with zero injuries anywhere in either campaign. The first recall was remedied to 96 percent of R2s before the envelopes were even sealed, which is the fastest recall fix in automotive history and a genuine feature of the software-defined car. Four months into a brand-new assembly line, early-production teething is normal, and next to the multi-year, multi-million-unit repeat campaigns that keep embarrassing Ford, this is a footnote.[1]

All true. Here is the part worth your attention anyway. Fourteen cars at a 100 percent defect rate means the automated station was not occasionally wrong; it was systematically wrong for three months, and the only sensor that caught it had a pulse. Meanwhile the notification system keeps trailing the fix by weeks in both campaigns, which is fine when the fix is software and the cars are already patched, and less fine when it is a torque wrench and the cars need a service visit. Rivian's quality story is two stories now: the software works, and the wrench needs watching.

What to do: If you drive a 2027 Rivian R2, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and install software update 2026.31.40 or newer for the camera fix; if yours is one of the fourteen fastener-recall cars, confirm the torque repair is logged before the November 29 letter arrives. Shopping? Early VINs of a brand-new platform carry teething risk, four months of data now says so. And treat every over-the-air update the way you treat a phone update: the recall you ignore is the one that was already supposed to be done.[1][5]
