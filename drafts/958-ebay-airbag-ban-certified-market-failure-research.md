# Research: 958-ebay-airbag-ban-certified-market-failure

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
Effective September 24, 2026, eBay bans all airbag, inflator, and steering-wheel-cover sales worldwide. This is not a crackdown on shady sellers: it kills eBay's own certified-recycler program — the most regulated, VIN-tracked, recall-verified channel for used airbags in America — which means the industry has concluded nobody can reliably tell a real airbag from a fake one at listing time.

## Original contribution (required)
1. **The ban-as-confession.** eBay's airbag category was not a free-for-all: it ran a restricted, certified-recycler program with ARA-built safety, training, certification, recall verification, and VIN tracking. The ban kills that program. A regulator banning its own compliance regime is a confession that the regime failed — nobody, not even with certification and VIN data, can reliably distinguish a good module from a fake at listing time.
2. **The Michigan enforcement ratio.** One prosecuted seller, $600K seized, nine felonies, versus ~81,000 Michigan vehicles that may carry fakes. Michigan built a dedicated Auto Fraud Task Force and its first case touched one man. The denominator is ~81,000 in one state; the enforcement pipeline processes cases in the single digits. State prosecution cannot scale to this problem; that is precisely why a private marketplace moved first.
3. **Two failure modes, one blind spot.** The DTN60DB inflators rupture and fire shrapnel (11 deaths). The generic counterfeits the Michigan AG describes mostly do nothing: over 80% fail to deploy at all. Buyers imagine the explosion risk; the statistically likelier fake is an airbag that simply will not fire. Both arrive through the same uninspectable channel, and a VIN lookup at nhtsa.gov catches neither.

## Counterargument (full strength)
- The ban may make things worse. ARA's certified recyclers with VIN tracking and recall verification were the SAFEST way to buy a legitimate used airbag at a non-dealer price. eBay killed the safest channel and left buyers with two options: pay dealer prices, or go to unregulated channels (Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, Car-Part.com) where there is no certification at all. The counterfeit problem was never concentrated in the certified program; the ban punishes the compliant sellers who were doing verification and leaves the parts flowing through channels nobody monitors.
- Compliance theater risk: the DTN60DB inflators were illegally imported and installed by repair shops, not sold through eBay's certified program. Banning eBay listings polices a storefront while the actual death vector — direct import plus shady installer — is untouched. A2C2's "only verifiable mechanism" claim is true of eBay's own storefront and says nothing about the parts that never touch it.
- Cost externalization: the ban raises the price of legitimate used airbags (fewer legal channels), which widens the price gap between genuine and counterfeit parts and strengthens the economic motive the Michigan case exposed.

## Limitations (required)
- The MI AG's ~81,000 figure is an estimate of vehicles that MAY carry counterfeit restraints, not confirmed installations; it sits on top of deployment-count data, not forensic part inspections.
- CARFAX's ~2 million is the opportunity pool (vehicles back on the road after a deployment), not the number with counterfeits installed. The true installed base is unknowable (NHTSA, 2024).
- The Kamaitis prosecution's connection to DTN60DB specifically is unestablished. The 80%-non-deployment failure mode the AG cites is distinct from DTN60DB's rupture-shrapnel mode; do not merge them into one statistic.
- eBay's ban covers its own marketplace only; international rules for non-pyrotechnic parts are looser (any seller outside the US), and nothing stops migration to other platforms.

## Actionable insights (required)
- Buying used: pull a vehicle history report and look specifically for airbag deployment records; ask the seller for the repair order and invoice naming the supplier and part number of any replacement airbag; have a trusted mechanic or the brand's dealer inspect SRS components before you buy.
- If your car was repaired after a deployment: demand genuine OE part numbers on the invoice; if the paperwork is unclear, get a dealer SRS inspection and pay for the verification.
- Do not inspect an airbag module yourself and never buy one from a marketplace listing; after September 24, 2026, eBay will not sell them at all, which does not make listings on other sites safe.
- A clean VIN search at nhtsa.gov/recalls catches manufacturer recalls but cannot catch aftermarket or counterfeit installs: absence of open recalls is not an all-clear.
- Watch the airbag warning light: NICB notes unusual SRS light behavior can hint at a tampered or fake module.
