Nissan Called It the “4-Door Sports Car.” FARS Calls It the Deadliest Sedan in America.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, Nissan Maxima drivers are not drunker than Camry drivers. They are dying at 2.5 times the Camry rate anyway. The Maxima went out of production in 2023 after 42 years and eight generations, and its obituary is already written in federal data: 1,544 deaths from 2014 through 2023, a fatality rate of 5.11 per 100 million miles traveled, the highest of any sedan in America.[1]

5.11
Maxima deaths per 100M miles of travel. Camry: 2.03. Altima: 2.88.

Rank all 122 sedans in FARS by death rate and the Maxima sits alone at the top. Behind it: the Chevy Cobalt at 5.1, the Impala at 5.0, and then daylight. Accord 3.07. Altima 2.88. Camry 2.03. For decades Nissan marketed this car as the “4-Door Sports Car,” a full-size sedan with a 300-horsepower V6 and the attitude to match.[3] It turns out the four-door sports car was also the four-door with the most dying.

I went to the toxicology desk expecting the usual story: fast car, cheap on the used market, young drivers, the whole sad spreadsheet, and the numbers refused to cooperate. Of 2,570 Maxima drivers in fatal crashes, 20.9 percent showed alcohol or drugs in their systems; Camry drivers came in at 19.2 percent. Altima: 20.0. Accord: 20.0. That is not a gap, it is a rounding error. I run this desk, I love blaming things on impairment, and my own data just took away the alibi.

The real story is age, and not the driver’s age. The car’s. Of the 1,544 Maxima deaths, 800 came in cars built between 2000 and 2009, which is 52.1 percent. Fifteen-to-twenty-five-year-old sedans packing 255 to 290 horsepower, trading for the price of a used Corolla. As a new car, the Maxima died in 2023. It is thriving as a cheap fast car, and a cheap fast car is the most dangerous object a used lot can sell you.

Start with the rate itself: its denominator is estimated miles, not odometer readings, and for a shrinking fleet of old beaters the estimate could be off by 15 percent. FARS does not record driver age or income, so the “aggressive driver in a cheap fast car” story is my inference, not their data. A tenth behind sits the Cobalt, which makes this crown narrower than the headline suggests. And FARS only counts the dead; a car with a low fatality rate can still maim plenty of people. All true. None of it moves the Maxima off the top of the list.

What this means for you

Shopping the used lot? A 2000-to-2009 Maxima is the exact car this data is warning you about. Newer is better here: the 2019-to-2023 final generation has modern safety tech and far more reliable transmission programming, and the 2009-through-2014 cars deserve extra suspicion for their genuinely failure-prone CVTs.[4] Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before you hand anyone money.[2] Already own one? No curse involved. It is simply the fastest cheap thing on the lot, and it gets driven like it. Fresh tires, working stability control, and not treating the on-ramp like a qualifying lap will do more for you than any recall.
