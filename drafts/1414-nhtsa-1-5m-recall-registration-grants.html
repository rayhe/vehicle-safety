NHTSA Will Pay States $1.5 Million to Tell You About Your Open Recall. Last Time, Only One State Showed Up.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

Your state is about to get paid to tell you something your car has been trying to tell you for years. NHTSA announced November 5 that it will hand states $1.5 million to flag open safety recalls when drivers register or renew their vehicles.[1] Applications close February 4, 2027. Six states can split the money, and last time Washington offered this same deal, only one state bothered to apply.[2]

1 in 10
Maryland vehicles renewing registration that carried an open safety recall during the state's two-year pilot

Maryland was the one. It took a $222,300 federal grant in 2017 and spent two years checking every registration renewal against the recall database.[2] From April 2018 to January 2020, the state renewed 4.6 million registrations and found 456,000 vehicles carrying 943,000 open recalls.[3] Roughly one car in ten at the counter had a known defect. Of those recalls, 371,000 got repaired, and more than 32% of the fixes were air bags.[3]

Run the pilot's own numbers and the picture gets complicated. Maryland repaired 39.3% of the recalls it found, which means six in ten stayed broken after the state told the owner, in writing, at the counter. Now run the new grant's numbers. Split six ways, $1.5 million is $250,000 per state, barely more than Maryland's single pilot. Spread across the roughly 14 million vehicles still unrepaired from 2025 recalls, it comes to about eleven cents per car.[5]

That math cuts both ways. If registration notices lifted the 2025 cohort's completion rate from 50.3% to 60%, roughly 2.7 million more vehicles would get fixed.[5] State letters have moved the needle before: when 18 states mailed Takata notices on state DMV letterhead, incremental repairs jumped 219% on average.[4] A letter with a government seal on it works. A letter from a manufacturer, sent to an address three owners old, mostly does not.

Knowing is still not fixing. Forty percent of Takata owners told surveyors that inconvenience was the main reason they skipped the repair.[4] Others assume the fix costs money. It does not: recall repairs are free for vehicles under 15 years old.[4] Others have no loaner, no time off work, and a car that still runs fine. California shows the other failure mode. An open recall does not block registration renewal there, and the state's only recall notice rides along on the Smog Check report, which cars in their first eight model years never receive.[6]

None of this makes the grant a bad idea, only a small one. Most recalled vehicles never actually had the defect, with manufacturing recalls routinely carrying defect rates of one or two percent, so owners who ignore the letter are usually right. Until they are catastrophically wrong, which is exactly what happened with 67 million Takata inflators, more than two dozen deaths, and at least 400 injuries.[4] Maryland's pilot proved notification finds defects. It also proved notification alone fixes fewer than four in ten of them. Five of six slots went empty last time, and nothing in the new announcement explains what changes this time.

So do not wait for your state to apply. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls right now, then check it again twice a year, which is what NHTSA itself recommends.[1] If you are buying used, run the VIN before you sign: one in ten cars at a Maryland renewal counter had an open recall, and the odds yours is one of them are not small. Your DMV might start telling you in 2027. Your car has been telling you all along.
