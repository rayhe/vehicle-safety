# Research: The Left-Turn Blind Zone — How Vehicle Geometry Kills Pedestrians

## Angle
The structural geometry of modern pickups and SUVs — thick A-pillars, tall hoods, narrow windshield angles — creates blind zones that erase pedestrians from the driver's field of view during left turns. New IIHS data quantifies this for the first time. Cross-referenced with FARS aggressivity data, this reveals that occupant protection and pedestrian danger are structurally coupled: the same design features that save you in a rollover kill the person in the crosswalk.

## Self-Critique Gate

## Primary Sources

### IIHS Blind Zone Study (November 2025)
- Source: https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/vehicles-with-big-blind-zones-spell-danger-to-pedestrians-during-left-turns
- 168 vehicles measured using camera-based technique
- Large driver-side blind zone (>30% blocked) → **70% increase** in left-turn pedestrian crash risk
- Medium blind zone (20-30% blocked) → **59% increase**
- Narrow field of view (≤85°) → **51% increase** in left-turn crash risk
- Near-ground visibility >30 ft → **37% increase**
- Average driver-side blind zone blocks 27% of view for 5'9" driver, 33% for 4'11" driver
- Average windshield: 88° field of view
- Nearest visible point: 26 ft (5'9" driver) vs 30 ft (4'11" driver)
- Lead author: Wen Hu, IIHS Senior Research Transportation Engineer

### IIHS Turning Study (2022)
- Source: https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/suvs-other-large-vehicles-often-hit-pedestrians-while-turning
- 14,000 fatal pedestrian crashes analyzed
- Pickups: **4x more likely** to hit crossing pedestrians in left turns vs cars
- SUVs: **2x more likely** in left turns
- Vans/minivans: **3x more likely** in left turns
- Right turns: pickups 89% more likely, SUVs 63% more likely
- 900 of ~5,800 fatal intersection pedestrian crashes involved turning vehicles (2014-2018)
- Non-turn crashes: pickups 51% more likely, SUVs 25% more likely to hit walking pedestrians

### IIHS Pedestrian Trend Data
- Pedestrian deaths up **78% since 2009**
- **7,300+ pedestrian crash fatalities per year** currently

## Original Finding
The FARS data reveals a structural coupling between occupant protection and pedestrian danger. The same vehicle design features that lower lethality for truck occupants (thick A-pillars for rollover protection, heavy frames, tall hoods) simultaneously create the blind zones that IIHS measured as increasing left-turn pedestrian crash risk by 70%. This is not an unfortunate coincidence — it is a physics tradeoff. NHTSA's roof crush standard (FMVSS 216) requires vehicles to support 3x their curb weight on the roof. For a 6,000-lb pickup, that's 18,000 lbs, demanding thick A-pillars. Those thick pillars block >30% of the driver's left-side view. The result: your truck is safe in a rollover, and the pedestrian in the crosswalk is invisible.

## Limitations
- FARS "excess crashes" is a rough proxy — not all excess crashes are pedestrian fatalities (includes multi-vehicle crashes where other vehicle occupants died)
- Cannot determine what fraction of excess crashes specifically occurred during turns
- The blind zone measurements cover 168 vehicles; not all FARS models are represented
- FARS data is 2014-2023; turning study used 2014-2018; blind zone study used 7 states
- Correlation between vehicle size and turning crash risk doesn't prove A-pillars are the sole cause — higher ride height, longer hoods, wider turning radii, and driver demographics all contribute

## Strongest Counterargument
Automakers would argue that rollover protection saves far more lives than blind zones cost. NHTSA's Enhanced Roof Crush Standard (FMVSS 216a) was projected to save 135 lives and prevent 1,065 serious injuries annually. If thicker A-pillars are a consequence of that standard, the math might favor occupant protection. But the IIHS data suggests this framing is false — there are engineering solutions (stronger materials, side-view cameras, redesigned cross-sections) that could preserve roof strength without blocking the view. The tradeoff exists only because manufacturers haven't been forced to solve both problems simultaneously.
