The Gap

The CT5 Earned Five Stars and Two Poors. Both Are True.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 4, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, because the first pass looked like a typo. The 2026 Cadillac CT5 carries a five-star overall rating from NHTSA and two Poor ratings from the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, earned in the same summer, and neither organization made an arithmetic error.[1][2] One car, two verdicts, and the distance between them is the story.

82%
More crash energy in IIHS’s updated side test than in the protocol it replaced

In July, IIHS published ratings for nine 2026 models. Four swept the board to Top Safety Pick+: the Audi A6, BMW X1, Mazda CX-5, and Subaru Crosstrek Hybrid.[3] Five missed, and the CT5 was, in Autoblog’s words, “by far the worst performer,” going Poor in the updated moderate overlap front test and Poor in the updated side test, a double that very few modern vehicles manage.[4]

The damage is specific, which is what makes it interesting. In the moderate overlap test the structure rated Good and the driver dummy rated Good everywhere; the rear passenger came back head-and-neck Poor, chest Marginal, the dummy’s head approaching the front seatback.[5] In the side test the structure rated Marginal while the driver dummy’s head slid down past the side curtain airbag and contacted the window sill hard.[5] Add Poor vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention and Marginal-or-Poor headlights depending on trim, and the CT5 missed every award on the table.[5]

GM’s answer was the five-star NHTSA rating, and spokesman Kevin Kelly said the company stood behind the car.[2] But the two systems are no longer grading the same crash. IIHS replaced its side test with a 4,200-pound barrier striking at 37 mph, up from 3,300 pounds at 31 mph, and the institute’s own math puts the new configuration at 82 percent more energy.[6] I checked it: kinetic energy scales with mass times velocity squared, so (4,200 × 37²) / (3,300 × 31²) = 1.813, which is 81.3 percent and rounds to their 82, so their number holds. A five-star rating earned under gentler physics and a Poor earned under these are not contradictory; they are answers to different questions.

4 of 5
July batch misses whose failure traces to the updated rear-occupant criteria

Zoom out to the batch and a pattern surfaces: four of the five July misses, the CT5, Lexus IS, Nissan Kicks, and Tacoma crew cab, carry Marginal-or-worse moderate overlap scores driven by rear-occupant protection, with only the Audi A3 missing on the side test instead.[3][4] IIHS added the rear-seat dummy to the moderate overlap criteria in 2022, and per the institute’s Jessica Jermakian, vehicles are still struggling with it.[2] Nine cars, one common failure point, and it is the back seat, where front-seat engineering is a solved game and rear-seat engineering is apparently still catching up.

Does any of this cash out in bodies? Side impacts account for roughly 23 percent of passenger vehicle occupant deaths,[6] and an IIHS study of ten years of crash data found the driver of a Good-rated vehicle 70 percent less likely to die in a left-side crash than the driver of a Poor-rated one.[6] Inverted, the CT5’s Poor side rating sits in the band historically associated with about triple the left-side fatality risk of a Good-rated car, an association from the study population rather than a prediction about any single crash.

The other side deserves its full strength. The CT5 meets every federal standard and earned its five stars under a real government test. IIHS ratings are voluntary, insurer-funded, and deliberately ratcheted upward to flunk cars that cleared the old bar, and this CT5 rides a platform introduced in late 2019 with a successor already in development.[4] Grading a seven-year-old design on a 2026 curve produces exactly this headline, and none of it makes the CT5 a deathtrap.

What you do with this: cross-shopping midsize luxury sedans, read the moderate overlap and side scores line by line at iihs.org/ratings, since the window sticker’s stars no longer answer the T-bone question alone.[1] In the same batch where the CT5 went Poor/Poor, the redesigned 2026 Audi A6 swept to Top Safety Pick+, so the curve is clearable.[3] Carry rear passengers regularly and the moderate-overlap rear-occupant sub-score is the one to filter on, because it is doing the flunking across brands right now.

Honest limits: lab ratings model injury risk, not crash outcomes; these scores describe the 2026 CT5 as tested, trim-dependent; the 70-percent figure comes from a 2011 study of the original side-test era, so the ordering transfers even if the exact magnitude does not; nine cars is suggestive, not proof of an industry-wide rear-seat lag; and the five-star-versus-Poor “contradiction” is partly framing, since the two systems measure different crashes by design.
