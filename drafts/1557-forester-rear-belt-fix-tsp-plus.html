Subaru Fixed the Back Seat With a Seat Belt. The CR-V Still Hasn’t

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

Subaru changed a seat belt, and that is the whole story. Beginning with 2026 models, the Forester got revised rear seat belts, and the car went from Acceptable to Good in the IIHS test that puts a dummy in the back seat, on the same platform, with the same structure, in the same crash, with only the belt changed.[1] In the same year IIHS made a Good grade in that test mandatory for any safety award, the Forester met the new bar and collected its twentieth consecutive Top Safety Pick honor, its tenth Top Safety Pick+.[2]

0.26
Forester deaths per 100 million miles driven, 2014-2023. The CR-V’s rate is 0.53, almost exactly double

Start with what the test actually measures, because the grade only makes sense once you see the dummy. IIHS updated its moderate overlap front test in 2022 to add a second dummy behind the driver, sized like a small woman or a 12-year-old child, then runs the car into a deformable barrier at 40 mph with 40 percent overlap.[9] In the Forester’s test, every rear passenger measure came back Good: head and neck, chest, thigh, restraints, kinematics, all of it, with shoulder belt tension at 4.5 kN.[1] IIHS says so plainly on the ratings page: beginning with 2026 models, Subaru made changes to the rear seat belts to improve rear occupant safety in moderate overlap front crashes. A 2025 Forester, same redesigned generation, same body, is Acceptable in the same test. One model year apart. One belt apart.

Put the Forester next to its classmates and the grade stops looking like a participation trophy. In the same November round of ratings, the 2026 Honda CR-V scored Poor in this test, the 2026 Toyota RAV4 Hybrid scored Acceptable, the RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid scored Marginal, the Audi Q3 scored Poor, and the Chevrolet Trailblazer fell short of Good.[5][6][8] Two cars passed it: the Forester, and the 2026-2027 Nissan Kicks, which earned its Good only after Nissan revised the rear seat belts on vehicles built after June 2026.[7] Both fixes were belt revisions. Neither maker touched the platform. Toyota could do the same thing tomorrow, and the part already exists, because Nissan and Subaru just proved it works twice in one year.

Now the part that keeps this honest. Road data from FARS, 2014 through 2023, agrees with the lab grade on the headline pair and then refuses to cooperate with it everywhere else.[3] Forester deaths run 0.26 per 100 million miles against the CR-V’s 0.53, so the car that passed the rear-seat test kills at half the rate of the car that failed it. But the RAV4, which won no award and scored Acceptable at best, has the lowest rate in the group at 0.19, and the Kicks, which now holds a Good grade and a Top Safety Pick award, sits at 0.69, higher than both the Forester and the CR-V. If the rear-seat grade predicted road deaths, those two facts would be reversed. Impairment does not rescue the pattern either: any-impaired shares across the group run from 17.6 percent for CR-V drivers to 22.1 percent for Trailblazer drivers, a 4.5-point spread, while death rates span 0.14 to 2.83, a twentyfold spread. Lab grades measure belt geometry in one crash configuration, while road rates measure who buys the car, where they drive it, and how, so both numbers are real without being the same number.

Fairness demands the skeptical case at full strength, because it has teeth. A test that is four years old, built around one dummy size, rewarding one belt behavior, may or may not map to real rear-seat injuries at real crash angles, and the test-maker keeps rewriting the criteria, which makes a twenty-year streak partly a streak of passing a moving target. FARS covers 2014 to 2023, so the generations in the death table are not the 2026 models in the lab, and no honest article claims a grade caused a rate, yet all of that is true alongside the fact that a belt is the cheapest safety fix in the car, that the failure mode it prevents is a lap belt sliding off a pelvis and onto a child’s abdomen, and that two manufacturers fixed it in a single model year while four competitors in the same segment have not. A cheap, proven, targeted fix that your competitors have not copied is a choice, not a lab artifact.

If you are shopping for a compact SUV and you carry people in the back seat, check the IIHS updated moderate overlap front rating for the exact model year, not the overall award and not the NHTSA stars, because NHTSA runs no rear-seat offset-frontal test of this kind. If you are buying a Kicks, only 2026 and 2027 models built after June 2026 carry the revised belts and the Good grade, so read the build date on the door jamb sticker. If you are buying a Forester, the Good grade applies to 2026 models only, while a 2025 is Acceptable on the same platform with different belts. Already driving a CR-V, a RAV4, a Q3, or a Trailblazer? That grade is rear-occupant-specific, so belt fit matters more than it used to, keep kids in the right restraint for their size, and check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because any future belt revision would arrive as a service action, not a headline.
