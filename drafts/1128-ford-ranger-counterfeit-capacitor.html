A Counterfeit Capacitor Killed the Backup Camera on 11,405 Brand-New Rangers

By Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

The cheapest part in a forty-thousand-dollar truck is a capacitor the size of a grain of rice. On 11,405 of the newest Ford Rangers, Ford says that part was probably fake. When it cracked, it shorted, blew a fuse, and blacked out the 10.1-inch SYNC 4 screen. The federally mandated backup camera went dark with it.

11,405
2026 Rangers recalled under NHTSA campaign 26V605

This is a supply-chain crime story wearing a recall notice. Ford's Part 573 filing names the display supplier: Suzhou Dongshan Display Inc., a Tier 2 outfit in China. Its engineers tore down six dead screens and found the same culprit every time, a multilayer ceramic capacitor, part number GRM32ER71H106KA12L, with a dissipation factor that did not match the drawing. In plain English, the part did not behave like the part it claimed to be. Ford's official phrase is "suspected counterfeit."[1]

Credit where it is due: Ford's warranty analytics caught this fast. The Michigan Assembly Plant team flagged black screens showing up at low time-in-service and low mileage. On August 14, Ford's Pre-Investigation Review opened a case. Six days later, the Critical Concern Review Group had the root cause. The recall landed September 22 with zero crashes reported. Nobody had to die for this one, which is rarer than it should be.[1]

Now the part that should bother you. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 111 requires a working rearview image, which makes the infotainment screen the compliance device. When the screen dies, the truck is not merely annoying. It is out of compliance with federal law. Against that, the honest counterweight: the camera was always a supplement. Every one of these Rangers still has mirrors, a rear window, and a driver with a neck that turns. Back-over crashes kill more than 200 Americans a year, and the camera exists to shave that number down, not to replace looking.[2]

If you drive a 2026 Ranger, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. VINs have been searchable since September 25, and interim letters started mailing October 5. There is no fix yet, so back up like it is 1995: mirrors, windows, turn your head.[3] Everyone else gets a different lesson. A modern truck carries thousands of chips and capacitors, and the safety-critical one might be the ten-cent part nobody bothers to authenticate.
