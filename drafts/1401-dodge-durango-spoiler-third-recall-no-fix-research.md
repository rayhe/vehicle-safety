# Research: #1401 — Dodge recalled the Durango's spoiler twice. It fell off anyway. Now it's recalled a third time, and there is still no fix.

**Slug:** 1401-dodge-durango-spoiler-third-recall-no-fix
**Journalist:** Clara Rollover (Consumer Safety Advocate — the "your dealer already fixed this twice" angle is her beat; owners need to know what to do when the fix itself failed)
**Kicker:** Investigation
**Started:** 2026-11-05

## Proposed angle
Stellantis recalled 74,578 Dodge Durangos (2021-2023) in August 2026 because the rear spoiler can detach at speed and become road debris. This is the third recall for the same defect on the same cars: 21V842 in October 2021, 23V115 in February 2023, and now 26V528. Cars that were already "repaired" under the first two recalls have to come back for a third repair, and at filing the remedy was still under development. The story is not a spoiler. It is a recall system that issued the same fix twice, watched it fail, and then mailed owners a letter saying the real fix is coming later.

## Challenge (kill test)
Is this genuinely new after 1,400 articles? PASS, four ways. (1) Fresh: NHTSA received the report August 13, 2026; interim owner letters mailed September 10, 2026; press coverage (CarBuzz, Carscoops, MotorSafety) ran August-October 2026. The third recall is less than three months old. (2) Not a duplicate: full queue grep, no truncation — zero slugs contain "spoiler"; the only Durango queue entry is #1329 (tail-light failure). The Jeep coil-spring third recall (#865/#882/#891) and the GM Express/Savana recall-of-recall (#1391) are the same *pattern* on different vehicles, not this vehicle or this defect. (3) Novel angle: the chronology arithmetic is original — three campaigns, 218,434 recall notices, one part, zero published permanent fixes at filing. Nobody on this site has totaled the three Durango spoiler campaigns or framed the 161 post-repair failure reports as the evidence that killed the first two fixes. (4) Newsworthy: a body part detaching at highway speed is a road-debris hazard for everyone behind the truck, not just its owner, and the owners most affected are the ones who already did everything right and brought the car in twice.

## The recall (26V528)
1. **Campaign:** NHTSA 26V528000; FCA US recall number 61D. Report received by NHTSA August 13, 2026. Population: certain 2021-2023 Dodge Durango (WD), built July 13, 2020 to March 28, 2023. Potentially affected: **74,578**. Component: STRUCTURE: BODY: HATCHBACK/LIFTGATE — liftgate spoiler.
2. **Defect:** some vehicles were built with rear spoilers over-flush to the roof, allowing contact with the roof when cycling the liftgate. Repeated contact damages the spoiler, which may detach from the vehicle while driving. A detached spoiler becomes road debris and increases the risk of a crash.
3. **Warning signs (owner-detectable):** the spoiler hitting or scraping the roof when opening or closing the liftgate; a rattling sound while driving may indicate the spoiler has loosened. (CarBuzz, August 21, 2026.)
4. **Estimated defect rate:** FCA estimates less than 1% of the recalled population actually has the defect (CarBuzz); BizzyCar reports the filed estimate as 0.8%. At 0.8%, about **597** of the 74,578 trucks carry the defect. The other ~74,000 get inspected anyway because the population was defined by build date, not by measurement.
5. **Remedy status (verify again at DRAFT):** FCA's Part 573 filing states "the remedy is under development." The NHTSA record as summarized by OEMDTC (updated October 13, 2026) describes the eventual remedy as dealers inspecting and replacing or repairing the spoiler and aligning the liftgate as necessary, free of charge, with a second owner notice to follow once the final remedy is available. Interim owner letters mailed September 10, 2026. VINs searchable on NHTSA.gov since August 20, 2026. Stop sale in effect for dealer inventory; federal law bars retail delivery before the repair, with civil penalties up to $27,874 per vehicle. Chrysler customer service: 1-800-853-1403, reference 61D.
6. **Supplier and parts:** upper standard liftgate spoiler part 7DY53TZZAB and upper special liftgate spoiler part 7DY56TZZAB, supplied by Nascote Industries of Nashville, Illinois. (BizzyCar.)
7. **What the record does NOT state:** no crash or injury count tied to this defect — FCA states it is not aware of any accidents or injuries potentially related to this issue as of July 29, 2026. That is absence of a published count, not a claim of zero incidents. No trim-level split of the 74,578 is published; any Durango with a roof spoiler, from base SXT to SRT Hellcat, may be affected.

## The chronology (original contribution — the three-strike count)
8. **Recall 1 — 21V842 (FCA Y73), October 28, 2021:** 2021 Dodge Durango, rear spoilers that may detach. Population **4,837**.
9. **Recall 2 — 23V115 (FCA 20A), February 23, 2023:** 2021-2023 Dodge Durango, same defect. Population **139,019**.
10. **The failure of the fixes:** on March 19, 2026, FCA's Technical Safety and Regulatory Compliance organization opened an investigation because field data indicated potential rear spoiler detachments *after* the Y73 or 20A recall remedies were completed. In other words, the repaired cars were still shedding spoilers.
11. **The evidence that killed the fixes:** as of July 29, 2026, FCA was aware of 8 customer assistance records, 116 warranty claims, and 37 other service records potentially relating to this issue, with receipt dates from May 10, 2022 to June 30, 2026. Total: **161 records**, all after at least one recall remedy existed.
12. **Recall 3 — 26V528 (FCA 61D), August 13, 2026:** defect determination August 6, 2026. Population **74,578**. Vehicles repaired under 21V842 or 23V115 still need the new repair completed. The defect is an assembly issue (spoiler installed over-flush), not a part defect — the same part, installed the same way, three times.
13. **The arithmetic:** 4,837 + 139,019 + 74,578 = **218,434** recall notices issued for one spoiler on one SUV line. First recall to third recall: October 28, 2021 to August 13, 2026 — **4 years 10 months**. Investigation to defect determination: March 19 to August 6, 2026 — about **4.5 months**. Interim letters September 10, 2026; as of this writing (November 5, 2026) no final remedy notice has surfaced in press or NHTSA summaries — roughly **two months** of owners driving with a known detachment risk and a letter that says the fix is coming.
14. **The Durango's other appearance:** #1329 covered a Durango tail-light failure. Different defect, different campaign. Do not conflate.

## Secondary context
15. **The pattern on this site:** Jeep Grand Cherokee coil springs were recalled three times for the same detachment (#865/#882/#891); the 2014 F-150 downshift recall was repeated in August 2026 because the 2024 software fix was never installed on some trucks; GM's Express/Savana got a recall-of-recall (#1391). The Durango is the third member of a small, ugly club: recalls whose remedies did not remedy. The article should name the club, not pretend the Durango is unique.
16. **Road-debris framing:** NHTSA's own language is that a detached spoiler "may create road debris and increase the risk of a vehicle crash." The hazard is not only to the Durango driver; it is to whoever is behind them at 70 mph. That is the strongest sentence in the piece and it belongs in the lede.
17. **Why a spoiler matters:** a roof spoiler is a cosmetic aero trim piece, not a structural component. The story's bite is that a *cosmetic* part required three federal safety recalls because the assembly process, not the part, was wrong — and the process was never fixed between recalls 1 and 2.

## Original calculations (for the article)
- Three campaigns, one part: 4,837 + 139,019 + 74,578 = 218,434 recall notices.
- Defect rate 0.8% of 74,578 = ~597 trucks actually defective; ~73,981 inspected unnecessarily because the population is build-date defined.
- 161 post-repair failure records (8 + 116 + 37) — the count that forced recall 3.
- 4 years 10 months from first to third recall; ~2 months from interim letters (Sep 10) to today (Nov 5) with no final remedy published.
- Stop-sale penalty: up to $27,874 per vehicle for delivering an unrepaired truck.

## Limitations (must appear in the article)
- No crash or injury count is published for this defect; FCA reports none known as of July 29, 2026. The article must not invent one.
- The 0.8% defect estimate is FCA's filed estimate, not a measured rate; the article must attribute it.
- The remedy description is ambiguous across sources: FCA's filing says under development; the NHTSA summary describes inspect/replace/realign with a second notice to follow. The article must state the remedy status as of its publication date and point readers at the VIN lookup rather than asserting a fix is available.
- This is a defect story, not a fatality-rate story; FARS cannot quantify the risk of a detached spoiler, and the article must not pretend it can.
- Trim-level exposure is unknown; do not claim Hellcats (or any trim) are disproportionately affected.

## Strongest counterargument (must appear at full strength)
This is a cosmetic trim piece on a three-year-old SUV, with zero known crashes or injuries, an estimated defect rate under 1%, and a manufacturer that caught the pattern itself, investigated it, and recalled the cars voluntarily — twice, then a third time when the data said the fix had not held. That is the recall system working as designed: a company monitoring field data, escalating, and re-recalling rather than hiding the failure. A spoiler is not an airbag, a brake, or a fuel tank, and three recalls for a part that has hurt nobody is evidence of diligence, not negligence. Say that, then answer it: the diligence framing collapses on the remedy. After five years and three recalls, owners were mailed a letter in September 2026 that says, in effect, "we know, we don't have the fix yet, we'll write again." A system that re-recalls without a repair is a notification system, not a safety system — and every mile driven between the letter and the fix is a mile with a loose part over someone else's windshield.

## What NOT to claim
- Any crash, injury, or fatality count for the spoiler defect.
- That a final remedy is available (verify at DRAFT; at research time it was under development).
- That any specific trim (SXT, Hellcat) is more affected than another.
- That the Durango is the only vehicle with a triple recall for the same defect (Jeep coil springs, F-150 downshift).
- That NHTSA forced the recall — FCA conducted it voluntarily after its own investigation.

## Actionable insights (required)
- If you own a 2021-2023 Durango: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 26V528 (FCA 61D). Do not assume a 2021 or 2023 spoiler repair means you are done — cars repaired under 21V842 or 23V115 need the new repair too.
- Listen for it: a scraping sound when the liftgate cycles, or a rattle while driving, means the spoiler is loosening. Do not keep driving it; call the dealer.
- If you are behind a Durango and see the roof spoiler fluttering or hanging, drop back. It is a part that detaches, and it detaches at speed.
- If your dealer says no fix is available yet: that is consistent with the filing. Get the interim notice on file, keep the paperwork, and check the VIN lookup monthly until the remedy notice arrives.
