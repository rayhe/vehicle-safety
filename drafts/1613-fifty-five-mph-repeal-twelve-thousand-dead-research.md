# Research — #1613: The 55 MPH Limit Repeal (31st Anniversary)

## Angle
On November 28, 1995, Congress passed the National Highway System Designation Act, repealing the National Maximum Speed Limit. Exactly 31 years later, the peer-reviewed body count for that single vote is 12,545 dead in its first decade alone. This is the one federal safety law whose repeal has a published death toll — and nobody on this site has ever covered the 55 mph limit.

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? Yes: exact 31st anniversary of the repeal vote (Nov 28, 1995 → Nov 28, 2026). Timely hook, zero prior coverage on the site (0 hits for "55 mph", "autobahn", "national maximum speed limit" in queue/stories).
- Novel angle? Yes: not "speeding kills" (covered in #1448) but the policy experiment itself — the only time America ran a controlled national speed experiment, twice (1974 enactment, 1995 repeal), with published, peer-reviewed casualty counts both ways.
- Original contribution: kinetic-energy calculation (55→75 mph = 86% more crash energy; 55→85 mph = 139% more) plus the anniversary framing and the Farmer 2017 extension (33,000 excess deaths 1995–2013).

## Verified facts (3+ primary sources)
1. **1974 enactment effect:** Fatalities fell 16.4% in one year, from 54,052 (1973) to 45,196 (1974). NHTSA credits the 55 mph limit with saving 3,000–5,000 lives in 1974 alone (TRB, 1984). Source: Friedman et al., AJPH 2009 (PMC2724439); NHTSA Countermeasures That Work.
2. **Repeal effect, short term:** Farmer, Retting & Lund (IIHS, Accident Analysis & Prevention, 1999): in the 24 states that raised interstate limits after repeal, interstate fatalities rose 15% and fatality rates rose 17% vs. states that did not raise limits. Patterson et al. (2002): states that raised rural interstate limits to 70/75 mph saw 35%/38% higher rural interstate fatalities than expected.
3. **Repeal effect, long term:** Friedman, Hedeker & Richter, American Journal of Public Health (2009): 3.2% increase in road fatalities attributable to raised limits on all road types; 12,545 deaths (95% CI 8,739–16,352) and 36,583 injuries in fatal crashes attributable 1995–2005. Highest increases on rural interstates (9.1%) and urban interstates (4.0%).
4. **Extension:** Farmer (IIHS, 2017): each 5 mph increase in state maximum speed limits associated with an 8% increase in fatality rates on interstates/freeways and 4% on other roads; an estimated 33,000 more traffic fatalities 1995–2013 than if limits had not risen since 1993. Source: NHTSA Countermeasures That Work.
5. **Physics (original calculation):** Kinetic energy scales with v². 55→75 mph: (75²−55²)/55² = 2,600/3,025 = +86% crash energy. 55→85 mph (Texas, the current US maximum): (85²−55²)/55² = 4,200/3,025 = +139% crash energy. Same car, same crash, nearly two and a half times the energy to dissipate.
6. **Dates:** NMSL signed by Nixon Jan 2, 1974, effective March 3, 1974; relaxed to 65 mph on rural interstates April 1987 (41 states raised limits); repeal passed Nov 28, 1995, signed by Clinton Dec 8, 1995. Today at least 25 states have raised maximums since 2001, some to 80–85 mph (TRB 2019).
7. **Present-day context:** Speeding-related deaths remain ~11,000+/year (11,288 in 2024 per site's #1448). The 1974 one-year drop (8,856 fewer deaths) is larger than a full year of modern speeding deaths.

## Counterargument (strongest)
- The 1974 drop is confounded: VMT fell ~3% during the oil embargo, fuel shortages kept cars off the road, and the economy was in recession. The TRB's 3,000–5,000 lives-saved estimate already nets those out, but the raw 16.4% drop overstates the speed effect alone.
- Higher limits also have real benefits: less travel time, and the 1987 and 1995 studies disagree on magnitude (one 1987-era study found no fatality-rate increase on raised rural interstates). The 12,545 figure is a model estimate with a wide confidence interval (8,739–16,352), not a body count from death certificates.
- Modern cars are vastly safer than 1974 cars; a 75 mph crash in a 2026 car is more survivable than a 55 mph crash in a 1974 car. The limit debate is not the same debate it was in 1974.

## Limitations
- The 12,545 figure covers 1995–2005 only; no peer-reviewed update extends it to 2026, so the full 31-year toll is unknown (Farmer 2017 suggests ~33,000 through 2013 alone).
- FARS data cannot isolate speed-limit changes from concurrent changes (vehicle safety tech, enforcement, drunk driving trends).
- The 1974 estimate (3,000–5,000 lives) is TRB's modeled attribution, not a direct count.

## Actionable takeaway
- The speed limit is the cheapest safety device ever deployed: it cost nothing, required no new technology, and saved thousands of lives a year. Drivers choosing 75 over 65 are accepting ~33% more crash energy ((75²−65²)/65² = +33%) for minutes saved.
- Practical: on rural interstates, the difference between 65 and 75 mph over a 100-mile trip is 12 minutes. The energy difference is a third more crash force.
