The Drunk Pedestrian Is Fading Away. The Young Drunk Driver Is Not.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 11, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, the drunk pedestrian is becoming a historical artifact. For decades, the impaired figure in a pedestrian fatality was the person on foot, staggering into traffic on an arterial at midnight. The Governors Highway Safety Association just published its full-year 2026 report, built on NHTSA's 2024 FARS data, and the archetype is expiring.[1][2] People walking got more sober. Drivers got more sober too, in every age bracket except one. The youngest drivers went the other way, and sharply.

15.6%
Share of fatal pedestrian crashes where a driver aged 16-24 was at or above .08 BAC in 2024, up from 7.2% in 2019. More than doubled.

In 2019, when a 16-to-24-year-old driver killed a pedestrian, the driver was legally drunk about one time in fourteen. In 2024, it was about one in six.[1] No other driver age group moved. Not one. Older brackets sat flat for five years while the youngest cohort roughly doubled. Meanwhile, alcohol involvement among the fatally injured pedestrians themselves declined in nearly every age group.[1] Pedestrian deaths fell for the third consecutive year, deservedly, with one footnote that deserved a bigger font: the single impairment cell trending worse in America is the kid behind the wheel.

Now the disclaimer desk gets its innings: FARS toxicology data is patchy across states, and young drivers get tested more aggressively: a 19-year-old smells like zero tolerance, a 45-year-old gets the benefit of the doubt. If testing of the youngest cohort intensified between 2019 and 2024, some of this doubling is measurement, not drinking.[2] And there is a gap nobody can fill: 23.8 percent of these deaths were hit-and-runs, and a fleeing driver never blows into a tube.[1] The true impaired share is higher than reported in every age bracket. That means 15.6 percent is a floor, not a ceiling. Comforting, right.

Association is not causation: impaired young drivers also skew toward night, speed, and the arterials where 62 percent of these deaths happen with no sidewalk noted.[2] Alcohol rides along with other risk; it rarely drives alone, so to speak. The latest data year is 2024, the 2025 numbers are state-reported preliminary, and the 16-24 cell is a small numerator, so year-to-year wobble is real. But the five-year direction is not wobble: down for walkers, flat for every driver age but one, and up, sharply, for the youngest.

So what do you do with a toxicology trend? If you are a parent, understand that the one impaired-driving category getting worse in America is the one your teenager belongs to. Zero tolerance is already the law under 21, and the problem is compliance, not statute. A rideshare credit is cheaper than everything else on this page. If you walk, the drunk-in-the-road stereotype is dated, but the defenses are not: reflective gear, lit arterials, actual sidewalks. The danger moved from the mirror to the windshield. And if you make policy, start by testing more drivers, because you cannot fix what you refuse to measure.[3]

Limitations: BAC data are incomplete across states and ages; 23.8% of pedestrian deaths were hit-and-runs, and fleeing drivers are rarely tested, so reported impaired-driver shares understate the true figure. Alcohol involvement is associated, not proven causal, and co-occurs with night driving, speed, and arterial roads. Figures are from 2024 FARS (released April 2026); 2025 numbers are state-reported preliminary estimates. The 16-24 driver cell is a small numerator, so percentages can shift on modest count changes. The article cannot attribute the rise to specific causes (testing practices, enforcement, post-pandemic behavior); those are hypotheses.
