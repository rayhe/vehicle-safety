Existential Dread

The Safest Driver in the Crash Drives the Deadliest Vehicle. Physics Does the Rest.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

Picture two vehicles meeting at highway speed, one weighing 80,000 pounds and the other 4,000, and guess who dies. In 2024, when a passenger car and a large truck crashed into each other and somebody in one of those two vehicles was killed, 96 percent of the dead were in the car: 2,511 people against 95 in the truck.[2] Twenty-six to one, which is not a crash statistic but a law of nature wearing a hi-vis vest.

26 to 1
The odds that the person killed in a two-vehicle car-vs-truck crash was in the car, not the truck: 2,511 dead car occupants against 95 dead truck occupants in 2024.

Here is the part that should bother you. Truck drivers are, by every behavioral measure the federal government collects, the best drivers on the road. Four percent of truck drivers in fatal crashes had a blood alcohol level of .08 or higher, against 25 percent for car drivers, the same 25 percent for motorcyclists, and 19 percent for light truck drivers.[1] Prior license suspensions tell the same story: 7.2 percent for truck drivers, 11.8 percent for car drivers, 15.4 percent for motorcyclists. Sober, licensed, and professional, and still the vehicle they drive kills the other guy 26 times out of 27.

Blame the math, not the man, because a loaded tractor-trailer needs 20 to 40 percent more distance than a car to stop, and the gap gets worse on wet roads or with tired brakes.[2] Trucks on the heavy end weigh 20 to 30 times what a passenger car weighs, and they sit tall enough that a smaller vehicle slides underneath them instead of bouncing off. You can drive perfectly and still lose an argument with 40 tons, because the argument was never about driving. It was about mass, and mass does not negotiate.

Zoom out and the toll is 5,340 people killed in crashes involving large trucks in 2024, down 2.5 percent from 5,478 in 2023, which sounds like progress until you notice the rest of the sentence: an estimated 161,201 people were injured in those same crashes, up 5 percent, and 548,521 large trucks were in police-reported crashes of any severity.[1] Fewer people died, more people got hurt, and more trucks crashed, so pick which of those three numbers you want to celebrate, because they do not agree with each other.

Seventy percent of the dead were occupants of other vehicles, 3,753 people; seventeen percent, 920, were truck occupants; and twelve percent, 667, were pedestrians, cyclists, and other people outside any vehicle.[1] Fourteen percent of every traffic death in America in 2024 happened in a crash that involved a large truck, and seven in ten of those dead were never inside one.

Truck drivers die differently than the people they hit. Most truck occupant deaths happen alone: 560 of the 920 were single-vehicle crashes, against 360 in multivehicle crashes, and 47 percent of truck occupant deaths happened in rollovers, compared with 20 percent for car occupants.[1][2] When a truck driver dies in a crash with another vehicle, six times in ten the other vehicle is another large truck. So the pattern is almost symmetrical in its cruelty: car drivers die when they meet a truck, truck drivers die when they meet the road, or each other.

Now the number that kept me up is in the IIHS per-mile table, where the risk of a truck crash killing someone has gotten worse since 2009, not better. Passenger-vehicle occupant deaths per 100 million truck miles rose from 0.82 in 2009 to 1.00 in 2024, up 22 percent. Truck occupant deaths per 100 million truck miles rose from 0.17 to 0.28, up 65 percent. Truck miles traveled grew only 14 percent over the same span.[2] Meanwhile the national fatality rate fell to 1.19 deaths per 100 million miles, down from 1.26 the year before, so American roads got safer while truck crashes did not get the memo.

Most of this killing happens in daylight, on a weekday, on a road you drive every day. Seventy-six percent of fatal truck crashes happened on weekdays. Forty-seven percent of the deaths happened between 6 a.m. and 3 p.m., against 28 percent for crashes without a truck.[1][2] Fifty-one percent of the deaths happened on major roads that are not interstates, the two-lane highways and arterials where trucks and cars share one lane each and a head-on collision is one bad decision away; thirty percent happened on interstates, and only 19 percent of the deaths happened on a weekend, against 35 percent for other crashes, because freight runs on a work schedule and so does dying in it.

Somebody in Washington noticed, years ago, and ordered a fix. Congress told NHTSA and the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration in 2021 to require automatic emergency braking on heavy trucks. Their 2023 proposal estimated the standard would prevent 19,118 crashes, save 155 lives, and reduce 8,814 nonfatal injuries every year, aimed mostly at the roughly 60,000 rear-end crashes a year in which the heavy vehicle is the striking vehicle.[3] Cars and light trucks got their AEB mandate, FMVSS 127, effective 2029. Heavy trucks got a proposal that was never finalized, shelved for a supplemental proposal, with a final rule likely years away. As of September 2026, there is no federal requirement for automatic emergency braking on a commercial truck, and the only thing standing between your sedan and 80,000 pounds is a human foot on a brake pedal that needs 20 to 40 percent more road than yours does.

Fairness demands the other side of this, stated at full strength, because the 96 percent figure is easy to misread, and FARS counts involvement, not fault. It records every death in a crash that had a large truck in it, whoever caused it, and it does not say who caused it. Plenty of these crashes start with the car: a driver cuts in front of a truck and brakes, lingers in a blind spot the size of a studio apartment, misjudges a turn across a trailer. A clean BAC and a valid license do not prove the truck driver was blameless in any particular crash, and 96 percent does not mean trucks cause 96 percent of these deaths. It means that when physics settles the argument, the car loses, regardless of who started it. And the AEB fix has a real defect of its own: NHTSA opened an investigation into phantom braking on Freightliner Cascadia and Western Star trucks in 2023 and upgraded it to a formal engineering analysis in October 2025, because a system that brakes for nothing is a hazard too.[3]

What this count cannot do is explain itself. FARS is a census of the dead, not an assignment of blame, and the 2024 numbers come from the Annual Report File, which NHTSA revises into a Final File about a year later. "Large truck" lumps together a 10,001-pound delivery van and an 80,000-pound tractor-trailer, and their fatality profiles are nothing alike. Per-mile rates rest on FHWA mileage estimates, and the estimation method changed in 2007, so anything before that year sits on a different ruler. None of that changes the shape of the finding. It only limits how precisely you can draw it.

So what do you do with a number like 26 to 1? If you drive a car, respect the stopping distance, because it is the one variable in this story you control. Do not merge in front of a truck and slow down, and do not sit alongside one. A loaded rig needs a fifth to two-fifths more road to stop than you do, and it cannot stop for a car that appears in front of it and brakes.[2] If you shop for a car and commute on freight corridors, understand that no crash-test star rating measures how your car does against 80,000 pounds, and the only thing that moves the physics is more mass of your own, which helps only at the margins. If you drive a truck, the rollover number is talking to you: 560 of your colleagues died alone, most of them upside down, and speed, load shift, and curve entry are the levers in your hands. And if you want a policy to ask for, ask for the one Congress already ordered: heavy-truck automatic emergency braking, docket NHTSA-2023-0023, modeled at 155 lives a year, still waiting for a rule five years after it was ordered.
