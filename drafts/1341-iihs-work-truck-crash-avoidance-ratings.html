The Gap

America's Work Trucks Can't See Motorcycles. IIHS Finally Graded the Blind Spot.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 31, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. That's the window where a forward-collision system decides whether the shape ahead is a motorcycle or road noise. For several of America's best-selling work trucks, IIHS now has an answer, and it's silence: no warning, no braking, the truck just keeps coming. On October 8, the Institute published its first crash-avoidance ratings for cargo vans and Class 3 pickups.[1] Detroit's heavy hitters, the Silverado 3500HD, F-350, and Sierra 3500, all earned Poor at avoiding the vehicle ahead. In one 43-mph trial, the big Ford never shed a single mph.[5]

Only one vehicle earned Good: the Mercedes Sprinter 2500, a German van, while Chevrolet's own BrightDrop 400 managed Acceptable and everything else the Big Three sell for the job site flunked. Two vans, the Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500, flunked both tests for the simplest reason in the book: they ship with no automatic emergency braking at all, and optional forward collision warning is the best money buys on a new 2026 work van.[1]

Motorcycles broke them; IIHS reports several systems “did not issue warnings or slow at all in most of the motorcycle trials.”[1] A pickup's radar sits high, tuned for the broad flat rear of another truck, while a motorcycle is a narrow sliver of metal and denim. Its sensor sees something, calls it ambiguous, and the 150 milliseconds expire undecided. Some braked in time yet got downgraded for staying silent.

Then came the child dummy, and the pattern repeated at smaller scale: several trucks, including the acceptable-rated F-350 and Ram 3500, spotted a child in daylight worse than an adult at night. In some runs the child registered at distance, then “disappeared” as the truck closed in. The reason is packaging: sensors live up by the rearview mirror, and these trucks have hood heights like studio apartments, so a small shape drops out of the sensor cone exactly when it counts. That's a design choice, not an act of God.[1]

Federal safety standards that bind passenger cars, airbag rules included, mostly don't cover these vehicles.[1][2] AEB mandates rolling out for passenger cars stop at the Class 3 line. IIHS counts 6,215 people killed in 2024 in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans: 16 percent of every roadway death in America.[1] Do the math and it holds: 6,215 is 16 percent of roughly 38,800, in line with NHTSA's preliminary 2024 estimate.[3][4] So the deadliest vehicles to share a road with are the least required to avoid you, and that should comfort no one.

What do you actually do with this? For fleet buyers, the verdict is blunt: only the Sprinter 2500 rates Good at not hitting the vehicle ahead. Cross-shopping an Express or Savana? Ask why a 2026 van has less crash tech than a base economy car. Motorcyclists: ride like these trucks can't see you, because on the test track several couldn't. Parents where delivery vans idle: the trucks struggled most with small pedestrians in daylight. Hold the hand, because the data says the sensor might lose them.

Counterargument, at full strength: these are track tests, not traffic. The trucks ran unloaded against foam targets with nobody aboard. A loaded truck brakes nothing like an empty one, and false-positive braking at fleet scale has costs the ratings don't price. What IIHS measured was protocol performance, not crashes per mile, on eleven vehicles, and all of that is true. It's also how passenger-car AEB got good: IIHS graded it, buyers noticed, manufacturers fixed it. As president David Harkey put it, these are “a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers.”[1] Someone should pick up the phone.
