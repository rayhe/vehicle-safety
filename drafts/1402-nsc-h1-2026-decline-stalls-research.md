# Research: #1402 — Traffic deaths fell 2% in the first half of 2026. Last year they fell 13%. The 16-quarter safety streak is running out of road.

**Slug:** 1402-nsc-h1-2026-decline-stalls
**Journalist:** Axle McScatter (Data Visualization Editor — statistical roundups and national trends are his beat; the story is a trend deceleration plus a state-level flip table, pure Axle territory)
**Kicker:** Trend Watch
**Started:** 2026-11-05

## Proposed angle
The National Safety Council's preliminary estimate for the first half of 2026: **18,460 people died in motor vehicle crashes, down 2%** from the revised first-half 2025 count of 18,846, while Americans drove 0.8% more miles. The mileage death rate fell to **1.12 per 100M VMT, down 3.4%** from 1.16. Sounds like more good news. It is not, once you put it next to the same release a year ago: H1 2025 deaths fell **13%**, and the rate fell **13.5%**. The improvement rate collapsed by roughly three-quarters in twelve months. The 16-quarter decline NHTSA has been tracking since Q2 2022 is flattening, and the state table underneath it is chaos: California went from -43% (second-best in the nation) to +14%; D.C. went from -67% to +133%; Hawaii went from +46% to -30%. Six states flipped from the worst list to the best list or vice versa in a single year. The article's original contribution is the deceleration arithmetic plus the flip table, which together say: the national trend is stalling, and the state numbers are too noisy to support any single-state victory lap.

## The numbers (primary sources)
1. **NSC H1 2026 (Sept 9, 2026):** 18,460 estimated deaths, down 2% from revised H1 2025 count of 18,846. Miles driven +0.8% Jan-June. Rate 1.12 per 100M VMT, down 3.4% from 1.16 in 2025 and down 10.4% from 2024. Source: NSC press release Sept 9, 2026 (PRNewswire) and NSC Injury Facts preliminary estimates page.
2. **NSC H1 2025 (Sept 10, 2025):** 18,720 estimated deaths, down 13% vs H1 2024. Rate 1.15, down 13.5% vs 2024 and 2023. Miles +0.8%. Source: NSC press release Sept 10, 2025.
3. **NHTSA H1 2025 (DOT HS 813 756, Sept 2025):** 17,140 deaths, down 8.2% from 18,680 (H1 2024), rate 1.06, "largest first-half decline since 2008." All 10 NHTSA regions down. Source: crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov.
4. **NHTSA Q1 2026 (DOT HS 813 833, July 2026):** 7,770 deaths, down 4.3% vs Q1 2025 (8,120). Rate 0.99, down from 1.05. "16th consecutive quarterly decline beginning with Q2 2022." 8 of 10 regions down. Report states H1 2026 projections "released in late September." Source: crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov.
5. **NHTSA full-year 2025 (DOT HS 813 800, April 2026):** 36,640 deaths, down 6.7% from 39,254 in 2024. Rate 1.10, second lowest ever. Source: NHTSA.
6. **NCHS cross-check (via NSC Injury Facts):** NCHS final estimate for H1 2024 is 20,278 deaths vs NSC's initial estimate of 20,900 (3.1% high). NCHS provisional H1 2025 is 18,846 vs NSC initial 18,720 (0.7% low). NSC's estimates have run within ~3% of final counts. Source: injuryfacts.nsc.org.

## Original calculations (for the article)
- Deceleration, deaths: NSC -13% (H1 2025) to -2% (H1 2026). The year-over-year improvement rate fell by roughly 85% ((13-2)/13).
- Deceleration, rate: NSC rate improvement -13.5% (H1 2025) to -3.4% (H1 2026). Roughly three-quarters of the rate improvement evaporated.
- Lives: 18,846 to 18,460 = **386 fewer deaths** in six months. Had H1 2025's 13% pace held, H1 2026 would have come in near 16,396 (18,846 x 0.87) — about **2,064 more deaths** than that pace implied. Label this explicitly as a rough counterfactual, not a forecast: the 13% was measured against a different base year.
- NHTSA cross-check: NHTSA's H1 2025 fell 8.2% and its Q1 2026 fell 4.3%. Whichever series you use, the improvement is roughly halving each release. Q1 2026 rate 0.99 vs H1 2026 NSC rate 1.12 — the two series are not comparable (see limitations), but both point the same direction: slower.
- The streak: 16 consecutive quarterly declines since Q2 2022 (NHTSA Q1 2026 report). At the H1 2026 pace the streak survives on paper but the margin is 386 deaths out of 18,846 — about 2%.

## Limitations (must appear in the article)
- NSC and NHTSA count different things. NSC uses National Center for Health Statistics data, counts deaths within 100 days of the crash, and includes private roadways (parking lots, driveways). NHTSA's FARS counts deaths within 30 days on public roads only. That is why NSC's H1 2025 (18,720) is ~1,600 higher than NHTSA's (17,140). Never mix the two series in a single sentence without saying which is which. The deceleration claim is built within the NSC series (13% then 2%), which is methodologically consistent.
- All H1 2026 numbers are preliminary estimates, subject to revision as data matures. NSC's own track record: initial H1 2024 estimate was 3.1% above the NCHS final; initial H1 2025 was 0.7% below the NCHS provisional. Treat 18,460 as accurate within a few hundred deaths, not to the person.
- State-level H1 estimates carry far more noise than the national number — the six-state flip table is the evidence. Do not write a "California is getting deadlier" article; the same data said California was the second-most-improved state twelve months ago. The honest read is that state H1 swings of 10-40% are mostly noise plus small counts.
- NHTSA's H1 2026 early estimate had not appeared as of November 5, 2026, despite the Q1 report's "late September" language. That is an observed absence from CrashStats search results on that date, not proof NHTSA will never publish it. The article must date the claim.
- FARS 2014-2023 cannot speak to 2026; the article's baseline comparisons use the published early estimates, not FARS model-year data.

## Strongest counterargument (must appear at full strength)
The honest counterargument is that a 2% decline is still a decline, the 16-quarter streak is intact, the rate (1.12) is still 10.4% below 2024, and preliminary H1 estimates have a track record of revising in both directions — NHTSA's H1 2025 number will itself be revised when the FARS Annual Reporting File lands. A single half-year of slower improvement after the largest first-half drop since 2008 could be regression to the mean, not a stall: you cannot fall 13% every year forever, because the denominator keeps shrinking and the easy wins (fleet turnover, post-pandemic normalization) get used up. The deceleration may be arithmetic destiny, not policy failure. The article must grant this fully — then answer it with the rate math: even if some slowdown was inevitable, losing three-quarters of the improvement rate in one year, while miles driven kept rising, is a materially different regime than the 2022-2025 run, and the state table shows the improvement is no longer broad-based.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- The national trend no longer does your safety thinking for you. In 2025, 38 states improved; in H1 2026, 11 jurisdictions got worse by double digits. Check your own state's trend, not the national headline.
- If you live in California, the +14% is preliminary and noisy — but it is the second consecutive signal (Q1 2026 NHTSA release had 20+ states up) that the broad improvement is narrowing. Drive like the streak is over, because in your state it may be.
- The cheapest life-saver in the data is unchanged: the 2025 subcategory breakdown showed unrestrained occupants down 13% and speeding-related deaths down 11%. Belt use and speed are still the two levers an individual controls.
- Do not buy a car on a single IIHS or NHTSA headline from a single quarter. The same state table that crowns a winner in September crowns it a loser the next September.
