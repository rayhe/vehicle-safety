The Biggest Vehicles on Your Street Are the Worst at Avoiding Crashes

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

Eleven work trucks and cargo vans went into the IIHS test facility this year. Seven came out rated Poor at avoiding the car in front of them. Nearly every system tested failed the motorcycle target: several never issued a warning, never braked, never even slowed down. These are the first-ever crash-prevention ratings for commercial vehicles, released October 8, and the headline writes itself in the data.[1]

Work trucks and cargo vans rated Poor in vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention in IIHS's first commercial-vehicle ratings.

The Poor list reads like a roll call of American job sites: the 2025-2027 Chevrolet Express 2500, the 2025 Ford Transit T250, the 2025-2027 GMC Savana 2500, the 2025-2027 Ram ProMaster 2500, the 2025-2026 Chevrolet Silverado 3500 HD, the 2025 Ford F-350, and the 2025-2026 GMC Sierra 3500. Not one Class 3 pickup managed a Good rating in either the vehicle-to-vehicle or pedestrian test. One data point captures the pickups: the F-350's system could only avoid a passenger-car target at 31 mph; at 43 mph it did not reduce speed at all, which is a vehicle weighing well over three tons arriving at full speed.[1][3]

Two of the Poor vans have a simpler problem: they don't have automatic emergency braking at all. The Express 2500 and Savana 2500 carry optional forward-collision warning instead, a beep instead of a brake pedal, and they scored Poor against pedestrians too. This is the platform GM has been selling, with a facelift here and a cupholder there, since 1996. Thirty years of sheet metal. Zero automated braking.[1]

Then there's the child dummy. Both the F-350 and Ram 3500 earned Acceptable pedestrian ratings, which sounds fine until you read the test notes: they could avoid the adult-size dummy at night but failed to detect and stop for the child-size dummy in broad daylight. At close distances, children simply disappear from the driver's and the sensors' lines of sight behind those tall front ends.[1] AEB, IIHS argues, is precisely the safety net for this scenario. It is the equipment these vehicles most lack.

But the good scores went to the newcomers. First among them: the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500, the only Good in vehicle-to-vehicle prevention. For pedestrians, the two Good ratings went to the discontinued Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 electric van and the Rivian RCV-Delivery 500, the platform under Amazon's custom delivery vans. New architectures with new sensors beat legacy platforms with old ones. That's the whole story in one comparison.[2]

Which brings the asymmetry into focus. David Kidd, IIHS's vice president of vehicle research, put it plainly: "When there are large crashes, those vehicles are usually doling out the harm." Crashes involving medium- or heavy-duty vehicles or light vans killed 6,215 people in 2024, sixteen percent of every road death in America.[5] Yet the vehicles that dole out the harm face fewer federal standards than the cars they hit: AEB that detects vehicles and pedestrians is on its way for passenger cars, Kidd noted, but for commercial vehicles it is not coming. NHTSA's own June 2023 math said a heavy-vehicle AEB requirement would save about 155 lives, prevent 8,814 injuries and more than 19,000 crashes a year, worth close to $2 billion annually. Three years later, the rule still hasn't been finalized.[1]

The defense deserves its full paragraph. Commercial vehicles are driven by professionals who log enormous mileage, and per-mile crash rates may still compare favorably to distracted commuters; fleet buyers optimize for total cost, not test scores, and per-unit economics are real money. Also, the motorcycle test target is brand new methodology, meaning no manufacturer had a chance to tune for it, and Poor ratings partly grade an exam nobody studied for. And driver inattention causes most of these crashes regardless of equipment: AEB is a backstop, not a solution. All fair. None of it explains a 1996 platform shipping in 2026 without automated braking.

If you buy or dispatch fleet vehicles, these ratings are leverage: specify AEB-equipped vans and refuse the beep-instead-of-brake vans on new orders. If you're a parent, the child-dummy result is the actionable part: never assume the driver or the sensors of a tall work truck can see a small child near the front bumper, so hold hands in any parking lot where work vehicles move. If you ride a motorcycle, assume the box truck behind you cannot detect you, because in several test scenarios it literally did not. And if you're driving behind one of these trucks, leave the gap you wish its sensors would.[6]
