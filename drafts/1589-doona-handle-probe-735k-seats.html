Your $550 Car Seat-Stroller Is Under Federal Investigation. The Handle Can Let Go.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

NHTSA opened a federal investigation into the Doona on July 6, and the file reads like a riddle. Zero crashes, zero injuries, zero deaths, and 735,734 car seats under review. Federal investigators say they have field reports alleging the carry handle can separate from the seat, and if it lets go while you are lifting your baby out of the car, the seat can drop. That is the whole case so far: a handle that might fail, on a product whose entire pitch is the handle.[1]

735,734
Doona+ seats, model years 2020 to 2024, under preliminary evaluation PE26005. About 147,000 per model year, roughly one for every 25 babies born in the US in a given year.

Start with what the handle actually does, because the Doona is not a normal car seat with a handle bolted on. Push a button and the wheels fold out, turning the seat into a stroller in about three seconds. Carry it by the handle up the stairs. Click it into the base, swing the handle against the seatback, and it becomes the anti-rebound bar in a crash. One part carries the baby, steers the stroller, and holds the seat steady in a frontal impact. No other infant seat asks a single component to do all three jobs, which is why a handle investigation on this seat is not a trim complaint.[1][6]

A second report sits in the same file, and it describes a different failure in the same family of moving parts. A parent in Cape Coral, Florida, told NHTSA that converting the seat from car mode to stroller mode on December 19, 2025 made the harness tighten around the infant by itself. The baby screamed, and bruising showed up later where the straps had compressed. One injury, no crash, medical attention required. Read the complaint all the way through before you get angry, because the seat was second-hand and the padding may have been reinstalled wrong by a prior owner, letting the webbing ride over a moving part instead of under it. That is the complainant's own theory, and it is fair to note it. It is also the complainant's point: taking padding off to wash it is something every parent does, and a seat should not punish a foreseeable reassembly mistake by squeezing a baby.[2]

Notice one line in the opening resume that most people will skip. ODI says it will inform the Consumer Product Safety Commission because the product is of shared interest. A car seat belongs to NHTSA, and a stroller belongs to CPSC. The Doona is both, which means two agencies with different recall powers are now circling one product, and neither one owns the whole thing. If the probe ever becomes a recall, watch which agency moves first.[1]

Timing matters here. On December 5, nine days from now, the federal rulebook for child seats flips: FMVSS 213b becomes mandatory for newly made restraints, and the side-impact standard FMVSS 213a reaches its compliance date after a delay that stretched back to a 2012 congressional mandate. Seats under investigation were certified to the outgoing FMVSS 213. Its replacement, the Doona X, launched in July at $850 against the original's $550, and its manual says it is certified to the new standards. Parents shopping this week are choosing between the investigated seat and its successor at the exact moment the rules change. Ask the retailer which standard the box claims. It is the one question that separates the two.[3][4]

So what do you do if one is already in your hallway? Tug the handle before every carry and confirm it locks. Check the harness routing against the manual after you wash the padding. Register the seat with Doona so a recall notice can find you, and check your model and manufacture date at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Buying used? Get the serial number first and run it before you pay. And if your handle has ever felt loose, file a vehicle owner questionnaire. Field reports are what opened this probe, and yours could be the one that moves it.[5]

Fairness requires saying plainly what this is not. It is not a recall, and it is not a defect finding. It is a preliminary evaluation, the first rung on NHTSA's ladder, and plenty of them close with no action. The file counts zero injuries from the handle, and the one injury in the record came from a second-hand seat with possibly misrouted webbing. Doona has not been ordered to fix anything, and the new model already meets the incoming standards. Treat the probe as a question NHTSA is asking, not an answer it has given, and treat the five-second handle check as cheap insurance while it asks.
