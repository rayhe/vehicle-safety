# Research: Young Drunk Drivers Doubled While Pedestrians Sobered Up (#1238)

## Angle
Inside an improving headline (pedestrian deaths falling for a third straight year, 6,732 projected for 2025), one subgroup is deteriorating fast: drivers aged 16-24. In fatal pedestrian crashes involving a young driver, the driver had a BAC above .08 in **15.6%** of 2024 cases — more than double the **7.2%** in 2019. Meanwhile alcohol impairment among *pedestrians* has fallen across nearly all age groups. The drink moved from the sidewalk to the driver's seat. The impairment story flipped sides.

## Kill test
Genuinely newsworthy? Yes: the headline is "pedestrian deaths are falling," but the fastest-growing risk pocket is the one nobody is talking about — young drivers getting drunker behind the wheel at night. It inverts the "drunk pedestrian" stereotype (22% of fatal pedestrian crashes involve an impaired pedestrian only, vs 11% driver-only) and lands a week after Utah's .05 story (#826) proved lower limits change behavior. Novel angle on fresh 2024 FARS data via the July-2026 GHSA spotlight report.

## Primary sources (5)
1. **GHSA, "U.S. Pedestrian Deaths Fall for Third Straight Year" + 2025 Pedestrian Spotlight Report deep dive into 2024 FARS data (July 2026)** — young-driver BAC>.08 in fatal pedestrian crashes: 15.6% (2024) vs 7.2% (2019); pedestrian alcohol impairment falling across nearly all age groups; 1,669 hit-and-run pedestrian deaths (23.8% of all pedestrian fatalities, up from 19.9% low in 2019); 76.5% of deaths after dark; 62.2% where no sidewalk; light trucks 50.4% of striking vehicles. https://www.ghsa.org/news/us-pedestrian-deaths-fall-third-straight-year

## Original contribution
The cross-cut nobody has drawn on this site: pairing the young-driver impairment surge (+2.17x) against the *falling* pedestrian-impairment trend in the same dataset, and setting both inside the third-straight-year decline. The usual story is "drunk pedestrians." The 2024 data say the drunk one is increasingly in the driver's seat — and younger. Also: at 15.6%, young drivers are approaching the share of crashes where the *pedestrian* is the impaired party.

## Strongest counterargument (must be in the article)
The doubling may partly reflect measurement, not behavior. FARS BAC reporting relies on tested drivers; young-driver toxicology testing and reporting completeness improved post-2019, and more young drivers on night roads post-pandemic shifts the denominator. Also, the trend comes off a low base (7.2%) — a doubling is easier than a doubling of the 22% pedestrian-impaired share. And overall pedestrian deaths are *falling*, which means other forces (speeding enforcement, vehicle tech, infrastructure) are swamping this pocket — it is a worsening pocket inside an improving whole, not the whole story.

## Limitations
- FARS BAC values include imputed estimates for untested/unreported cases; the imputation model's error is larger for small subgroups like "drivers 16-24 in pedestrian fatal crashes."
- GHSA's 2025 figures are projections from state preliminary data, not final FARS.
- "BAC above .08" captures legal impairment, not necessarily crash causation; a sober-at-fault pedestrian and a .09 BAC driver in a crash where the driver is not at fault still counts.
- No exposure normalization: we don't know miles young drivers drove at night vs daytime, so the rise could partially reflect exposure shifts.

## Actionable takeaways
- Parents of 16-24-year-olds: the conversation isn't just "don't drive drunk" — it's "don't drive drunk *at night near people on foot*." The lethal overlap is young drivers, darkness, and pedestrians on roads without sidewalks.
- Utah's .05 law (#826) actually moved heavy drinkers off the road — lower BAC limits are the one policy with an evidence base against this exact pocket.
- If you walk at night: the drunk-pedestrian stereotype is wrong; assume the impairment is coming at you in a car, and that car is disproportionately a light truck.
- 15% of pedestrian deaths happen on freeways/interstates — if you break down on one, stay in the vehicle if safe; if you must exit, get over the barrier. Every state has a Move Over law; drivers frequently ignore it.
