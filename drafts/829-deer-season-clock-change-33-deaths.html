The Clocks Fall Back November 1. Deer Collisions Are About to Triple.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 6, 2026

Somewhere in West Virginia tonight, a driver will meet a 150-pound herbivore at 55 miles an hour, and the herbivore will win the argument. Researchers at the University of Washington estimate 2.1 million deer-vehicle collisions happen in the United States every year, killing about 440 people, injuring 59,000, and costing upwards of $10 billion.[1] That makes the white-tailed deer the deadliest animal on American roads by a margin so wide nothing else qualifies for second place. And the calendar is conspiring against you: collision claims more than double in October, November, and December, with November running roughly triple the summer rate.[2] Eight weeks from today, we will make it worse on purpose.

How much more frequent deer-vehicle collisions are two hours after sunset compared with two hours before

That 14-to-1 ratio is the whole story in one number.[1] Deer are crepuscular, most active around dawn and dusk, and the danger is not the dark itself but the overlap: your commute and their commute sharing the same road at the same hour. On November 1, at 2 a.m., we end daylight saving time. Sunset lurches an hour earlier, straight into the 5-to-7 p.m. rush, at the exact peak of the rut, when bucks are distracted, does are moving, and nobody in the herd is checking both ways. The same Washington study modeled the alternative and found that permanent daylight saving time would likely prevent 33 human deaths, 2,054 injuries, and $1.19 billion in costs every single year, mostly because the evening stays bright.[1] Thirty-three funerals, scheduled by the clock on your dashboard.

Now the part the official statistics would rather you not notice. Ask the federal government how many Americans deer kill and you get about 200 a year.[3] Ask the researchers with a million insurance claims and state crash records and you get 440.[1] The gap is not a rounding error; it is a filing system. Fatality databases code crashes by the first harmful event and the manner of collision, which means the driver who swerves to miss a deer and rolls into a ditch gets recorded as a rollover, not as an animal strike. The deer was the cause; the ditch got the credit. Somewhere between the two numbers sits the true body count, and it is bigger than the one on the official letterhead.

Which brings us to the swerve, the deadliest instinct in the American driver's repertoire. State Farm and AAA both say the same thing, and they say it the way people say things that have been ignored for decades: brake, do not swerve.[5] Swerving trades a damaged hood for a rollover, a tree, or oncoming traffic. A Forest Service biologist who studies how deer actually see explains why the standoff feels so unfair: a deer's eyes are stationary, built to catch the motion of predators, so your car reads to it as a shape getting larger, detail-free, and your headlights, gathered by night vision evolved for starlight, are simply blinding.[3] The animal cannot perceive what is happening. You are the only one in the encounter with a prefrontal cortex, so act like it.

Fairness requires giving the counterargument its full weight, and it has some: permanent daylight saving would darken the mornings, shifting some risk to the dawn commute, and the 33-death figure is a modeled estimate, not an experiment anyone has run. The deeper drivers are not on any clock: there are more white-tailed deer in North America now than at European settlement, around 30 million, and sprawl keeps paving subdivisions into their living room.[3] No legislature can vote the herd away. But the clock is the one variable we set deliberately, twice a year, knowing the cost. In West Virginia the odds of hitting a deer are 1 in 40; in Pennsylvania, 148,000 claims a year, the most in the nation.[2] If you drive those roads after dark this fall, use your high beams, watch the 6-to-9 p.m. window, and remember that deer travel in herds: where there is one, there are more. Check your policy, too, because animal strikes fall under comprehensive coverage, not collision, and the average claim now runs about $5,600.[4] The deer will not be checking theirs.
