# Research: #909 — Suspended Licenses Don't Stop Speeders. Oregon Wants to Govern the Car Instead.

**Journalist:** Vin Wreckage (Existential Dread Columnist — paradoxes, counterintuitive findings, the big picture)
**Kicker:** Investigation
**Status:** RESEARCH → DRAFT

## Thesis
License suspension is America's primary punishment for dangerous speeding, and it
does not work: roughly 75% of suspended drivers keep driving anyway. Fines do not
work either — the Brooklyn driver who killed a mother and two children on Ocean
Parkway had collected nearly 100 violations in two years, 18 of them for speeding
in a school zone, and was driving on a suspended license at more than double the
posted limit. Oregon's answer, presented to its Senate Judiciary Committee on
September 8, 2026: stop punishing the driver and start governing the car. The
"Stop Super Speeders" bill would require the worst offenders to install
Intelligent Speed Assistance devices that physically cap the accelerator. We have
already run this experiment. It was called the ignition interlock, it is now
mandated for repeat drunk drivers in all 50 states, and it works for exactly the
same reason: the device does not care about your intentions. Families for Safe
Streets is explicitly running the MADD playbook — start with the worst offenders,
prove the device works, expand. The only honest objection is the one nobody says
out loud: we accept governing the car for alcohol, but speed still feels like
freedom.

## Data and arithmetic
- **The 75% problem:** approximately 75% of drivers with suspended licenses
  continue to drive (NCHRP research, cited by Oregon advocates; independently
  cited via finesandfeesjusticecenter.org). Suspension is a paperwork punishment
  for a physics problem.
- **Oregon toll:** 587 people died in Oregon crashes in 2023; 32% involved
  speeding drivers (Families for Safe Streets) = ~188 speeding-involved deaths
  in one state in one year.
- **National toll:** 11,258 speeding-related deaths in 2023, 29% of all US
  traffic fatalities (NHTSA).
- **The Brooklyn case:** ~100 traffic violations in two years, 18 school-zone
  speeding infractions, suspended license, killed a mother and two children on
  Ocean Parkway at more than double the posted speed limit. Every conventional
  punishment had already been applied. None of them touched the accelerator.
- **ISA mechanics:** interacts only with the accelerator, not the brakes
  (Zachary Lauritzen, Oregon Walks). Three types exist: hard max-speed cap
  (already in wide fleet use), GPS warning (EU-mandated on all new cars since
  2024), and active prevention (Virginia delegate test-drove a +9 mph cap with
  temporary override for merging).
- **Cost:** ~$150 install, $100 uninstall, $150/month ongoing (Oregon proposal);
  driver pays; Oregon State Police would monitor (they already run the ignition
  interlock program). DC and Virginia subsidize impoverished drivers.
- **Efficacy signal:** NYC municipal fleet ISA pilot: 64% reduction in speeding
  (NYC DCAS + USDOT Volpe Center, via AAMVA model act).
- **Physics:** 100 mph vs 70 mph = 2.04x kinetic energy (Crash Report #823
  arithmetic: KE scales with v^2). A governor that holds a super speeder to the
  limit does not just reduce speed; it more than halves the energy of the crash
  that would otherwise happen.
- **The interlock precedent:** ignition interlocks (breathalyzer-to-start) are
  now mandated for multiple-DUI offenders nationwide. The regulatory theory is
  identical: when the driver cannot be trusted, govern the machine.

## Legislative landscape (2025-2026)
- Washington, D.C.: STEER Act, effective September 2025 — first US jurisdiction
  with an active ISA program for suspension-bound drivers; District pays for
  impoverished drivers.
- Virginia: first STATE to enact active ISA (bipartisan: Del. Patrick Hope (D),
  signed by Gov. Glenn Youngkin (R)); judges' discretion as alternative to
  suspension/jail; 2-6 month enrollment; effective July 1, 2026. Youngkin
  stripped a provision mandating devices for 100+ mph tickets before signing.
- Washington state: passed similar legislation.
- Georgia: passed a bill days after Virginia's enactment; awaiting governor's
  signature.
- Filed/introduced: Maryland, Arizona, California, New York, Rhode Island
  (H 8098, "Super Speeder Accountability Act" — ISA as license-reinstatement
  condition).
- Oregon: concept presented Sept 8, 2026; 2027 session target.
- GHSA: 16 states introduced active ISA legislation in 2026; AAMVA published a
  Model Intelligent Speed Assistance Act (Jan 2026) for interstate uniformity.

## Novel contribution
1. **The interlock-precedent frame.** No Crash Report story (and no #823/#841)
   has made the explicit regulatory-theory argument: ignition interlocks for
   DUIs and ISA for speeders are the SAME intervention (govern the car when the
   driver is ungovernable), and the interlock's 50-state adoption is the proof
   that device-mandates survive every objection now being raised against ISA
   (cost, freedom, tampering). #823 covered Florida's criminalization; #841
   covered the GHSA guidebook. Neither connected the dots to the interlock.
2. **The 75% problem as the load-bearing number.** The entire ISA wave is a
   confession that suspension-based enforcement has a 75% failure rate. That
   single statistic reframes every "tough on speeders" law as theater.
3. **The Brooklyn case as the kill shot for fines.** ~100 violations in 24
   months is roughly one ticket per week. The driver was, by every
   administrative measure, maximally punished before the crash. The system did
   not fail to identify her; it failed to touch the accelerator.

## Counterarguments and limitations (for the ethics/legal critics)
- The Oregon bill is a CONCEPT for the 2027 session, not enacted law. Frame
  honestly: "proposed," "would require." The news is the wave, not the statute.
- 75% figure: advocates' citation of NCHRP research; treat as "research cited
  by advocates" not as NHTSA gospel. Two independent citations (Oregon hearing,
  Governing) corroborate the number.
- 587/32% are Families for Safe Streets' figures presented at the hearing;
  attribute accordingly.
- Equity: $150/month falls on the driver; DC/VA subsidize the poor, Oregon's
  proposal is silent on subsidy — flag it. A device mandate priced as a
  regressive fee deserves the criticism.
- Tamper-resistance: RI bill requires tamper-resistant, circumvention-reporting
  devices; real-world defeat rates are unproven at scale. Note it.
- Youngkin stripped the mandatory 100+ mph provision from Virginia's bill —
  the "super speeder" trigger is exactly what got negotiated away first. That
  undercuts the "targeting the worst" framing; include it.
- Override: active ISA systems allow temporary override (merging); a determined
  driver can still speed in bursts. The device reduces, not eliminates.
