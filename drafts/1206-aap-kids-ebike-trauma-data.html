Pediatricians Studied 136,000 Kid Bike Crashes. E-Bikes Came Out Deadliest.

By Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 31, 2027

Before you buy the e-bike on your kid's holiday list, look at what pediatric surgeons just reported. At the American Academy of Pediatrics conference in San Diego this month, researchers presented 136,452 bike crashes involving patients 17 and younger, and electric bikes had the highest share of trauma deaths of any category studied, beating pedal bikes and gas-powered bikes.[1]

Surgery resident Seth Weir led the study: twenty years of emergency-department records, 134,426 pedal-bike cases, 305 e-bike cases, 1,721 gas-powered cases.[1] Pedal bikes produced scrapes; e-bikes produced fractures. Head injuries led every category, worst on e-bikes at 20 percent versus 15.8 percent for pedal bikes.[1]

A second study from Children's Hospital Los Angeles landed the same week and settled the argument parents actually have: e-bikes versus e-scooters. Kids on e-bikes needed surgery at nearly three times the rate of the e-scooter kids, and were nearly three times as likely to be admitted to the hospital. Concussions and fractures, same story.[2]

This breaks the ladder most parents carry in their heads. Pedal bikes feel safe, e-bikes feel like a small step up, gas-powered bikes feel like the real danger. That ranking inverts it: e-bikes sit at the top of the trauma-death share, and nothing about the reason is mysterious. A Class 3 e-bike does 28 miles per hour, which is motorcycle speed, with bicycle brakes, bicycle tires, and a rider whose judgment is still under factory warranty,[3] which is how you get higher-energy crashes: fractures instead of road rash, heads hitting pavement at speeds heads were not designed for. The pattern is not local, either. Germany's first-half 2026 figures showed e-scooter deaths climbing to 28, the only vehicle category going the wrong way, while New York City logged 738 e-bike injuries and 13 rider deaths in the same span, up from 601 and 10 a year earlier.[4][5]

To be fair: emergency-department records are not a death census, 305 e-bike cases is a thin sample from a category that barely existed before 2017, and these are conference abstracts, not peer-reviewed papers. The denominator problem is real, but severity per crash is less denominator-sensitive than raw counts, and both studies point the same way. Strongest defense of the e-bike: it replaces something worse. A teenager on an e-bike is a teenager not driving a 4,000-pound SUV, and not riding with a 16-year-old driver, which remains the deadliest thing adolescents do. That defense is real, but it does not make the trauma data go away.

Here is what you actually do about it: start with helmets, every ride, no exceptions. Head injuries led every category and peaked at 20 percent for e-bikes. Do not buy a child a Class 3 e-bike; 20-mph pedal-assist is the sane ceiling for under-16s. Check your state's age and helmet laws, because plenty of kids in the data were already violating them.[2] Buy from a reputable brand. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's parallel file is battery fires, and cheap no-name bikes fail on two axes at once.[3] Give a new rider an empty parking lot before you give them a road. Sixty-five percent of parents already told a national poll that e-bikes are more dangerous for teens than regular bikes.[2] Now the pediatricians have shown them the receipts.
