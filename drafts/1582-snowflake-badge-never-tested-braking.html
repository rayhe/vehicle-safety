The Gap

The Snowflake on Your Tire Never Tested Braking

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Editor
November 25, 2026

Look at the sidewall of your tires, where somewhere in the raised lettering sits a little mountain with a snowflake inside it, and that symbol is supposed to mean winter-ready. Tire Rack just spent its 2026 test season measuring what the symbol actually guarantees, and the answer should change how you shop for tires. Ten tires, one 2025 Camry, one size, tested on snow, ice, wet, and dry in near-freezing weather. On snow, the best winter tire stopped from 25 mph in 56.1 feet. The best all-season needed 81.1. Twenty-five feet, at neighborhood speed, is the whole crash.[1]

15.1 feet
The snow-braking gap between two tires wearing the identical three-peak mountain snowflake badge

That little mountain is called the three-peak mountain snowflake, and it dates to 1999. A tire earns it by accelerating on medium-packed snow at least 10 percent better than a reference tire, and that is the entire exam: braking is not tested, cornering is not tested, and ice is not tested. In this test, a Michelin CrossClimate2, an all-weather tire, needed 72.4 feet to stop on snow, while a Bridgestone Blizzak WS90, a dedicated winter tire, needed 57.3, the same badge and fifteen feet apart. One of them is a winter tire, and the other one passed a test about going, not stopping.[2][4]

One all-weather tire nearly erased the gap. Bridgestone's WeatherPeak stopped on snow in 58.6 feet, within 2.5 feet of the best winter tire in the test, and beat three of the five dedicated winters outright, including the Continental VikingContact 8. Before you call it a fluke, Tyre Reviews checked: the WeatherPeak finished second of eight for snow braking in its own all-weather test and second of eleven in Tire Rack's 2025 test. Some all-weather tires are genuinely good in snow. A badge cannot tell you which ones, because a badge does not measure stopping. Only test data does.[2]

Ice is where winter tires earn their money. From just 12 mph, the Blizzak stopped in 38.7 feet. The Primacy Tour A/S needed 57.7. Nineteen feet, at parking-lot speed, and four of the top four ice spots went to dedicated winters. Ice produced the widest margins in the whole test: a 49 percent spread from best to worst. Tyre Reviews ran the residual-speed math, and it is ugly: at highway speed, the all-season car would still be doing 75 km/h when the Blizzak had stopped. On black ice, at 12 mph, the difference between the best and worst tire here is the difference between stopping at the line and sliding straight through it.[2]

Now the part no tire shop puts on the poster. The tire that won snow and ice lost the rain. Michelin's X-Ice Snow, first on snow at 56.1 feet, stopped from 50 mph on cold wet pavement in 129 feet, dead last and thirty-six feet behind the Vredestein Quatrac's 93. In the dry it finished last too, 31.39 metres against the CrossClimate2's 25.33. Winter rubber is soft by design, and soft rubber that grips ice gives up grip on wet tarmac. No tire in this test wins everywhere, and anyone selling you one is selling a slogan, not a tire.[1][2]

Even inside the winter category, labels mislead. Vredestein's Wintrac Pro+, a dedicated performance winter tire, got beaten on ice braking by the Vredestein Quatrac, an all-weather tire from the same manufacturer, and lost the ice acceleration run too. Performance winter means the tire trades some cold-weather traction for speed rating and dry-road manners. A fair trade if you know you made it. A nasty surprise if you bought the word winter and assumed it meant best on ice.[2]

All-wheel drive will not save you from any of this. It helps you go, and does nothing for stopping. Traffic Injury Research Foundation data puts numbers on it: a car on winter tires stopped from 30 mph in about 59 feet, while the same car on all-seasons needed about 30 feet more. A front-drive car on winter tires out-brakes an all-wheel-drive SUV on all-seasons. Every October, the pitch is that AWD is the winter safety feature. This test says the safety feature is four patches of rubber, and the drivetrain is a footnote.[3]

Give the other side its full due, because it is real. For most of America, winter is cold rain, not snow and ice, and on cold wet pavement the winter tire is the worst tire in this test. An all-weather tire wins the wet chart, handles snow respectably, and never asks for a seasonal swap, a second set of wheels, or a corner of the garage. A dedicated winter set costs hundreds of dollars plus two swaps a year, and pays for itself only in the conditions many drivers see twice a winter. If your winter is 40-degree rain with two snow days, buy the all-weather tire, because this test proves it.

What to do, concretely. If you see real snow and ice every winter, fit four dedicated winter tires. Never two: two on the front lets the rear slide out, and two on the rear leaves the front unable to steer.[5] Check the snow braking number, not the badge, because two tires with the same snowflake stopped 15 feet apart. If your winter is mostly cold rain, buy a good all-weather: the WeatherPeak if snow matters, the Quatrac if rain does. If you run all-seasons through winter, know the gap, 25 extra feet to stop from 25 mph on snow and 19 extra feet from 12 mph on ice, and add following distance to match. Remember the temperature line, too: all-season rubber starts losing grip below about 45 degrees. Winter is not a season on the calendar. It is a temperature, and your tires know it before you do.[3]
