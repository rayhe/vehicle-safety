The Gap

The 2029 Auto-Brake Mandate Is Quietly Sliding to 2031. That Delay Costs 720 Lives.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

In May 2024, the federal government ordered every new car sold in America to brake itself by September 2029. This March, in a court filing almost nobody read, the government admitted it probably will not happen until 2031. That two-year slip costs roughly 720 lives and 48,000 injuries, by the government's own math. Before you sign that lease on a 2029 model, you might want to see this.

720
lives of forgone steady-state benefit from a two-year delay, per NHTSA's own projections

The rule is FMVSS 127, and Congress did not ask nicely: the 2021 infrastructure law ordered NHTSA to write it, and what came out is the toughest auto-brake standard in the world, requiring no contact with the car ahead at up to 62 mph, pedestrian detection in daylight and in darkness, and automatic braking up to 90 mph when a crash is imminent. NHTSA projected at least 360 lives and 24,000 injuries prevented every year, at a cost of about $82 per vehicle, less than a tank of gas in a Suburban.

The industry sued anyway: in June 2024 the Alliance for Automotive Innovation, the trade group for nearly every major automaker, petitioned for reconsideration alongside Toyota, Volkswagen, and boutique supercar maker Scuderia Cameron Glickenhaus. Their objections read like a wish list: the no-contact requirement is impracticable, one test run is not enough, the malfunction lamp rules are too strict, drivers should get an off switch. NHTSA denied nearly all of it in November 2024. Alliance president John Bozzella called the decision "disastrous," predicting it would "endlessly and unnecessarily frustrate drivers" and "won't really improve driver or pedestrian safety."

Then came the freeze. On January 17, 2025, the Alliance sued in the D.C. Circuit. Three days later the new administration's regulatory freeze landed. By February the case was held in abeyance, which is lawyer for frozen, not dead, and then, on March 23, 2026, the Department of Transportation filed a status report with the court stating that it is "currently preparing a notice of proposed rulemaking" that would extend the compliance lead time by two years, to give automakers "more time to achieve challenging aspects of the standard." That proposed rule still has not been published. On paper, the September 2029 deadline is still the law. Nobody in the industry is planning around it.

The "challenging aspects" deserve a fair hearing, because the engineering complaint is not pure theater. Our own census of IIHS ratings found 22 percent of current systems score Poor or Marginal on the harder front crash prevention test, and Jeep fails at 61 percent. Teaching a car to slam its own brakes at 62 mph with zero contact, in the dark, for a pedestrian, while never phantom-braking at a highway overpass shadow, is genuinely one of the hardest perception problems in production cars. False positives at highway speed cause rear-end crashes too, which is exactly what Bozzella warned Congress about. NHTSA has now conceded the point in the only language that matters. A mandate the regulator admits needs more time is a mandate whose deadline was, at least in part, aspirational.

None of that changes the arithmetic: two years times 360 lives is 720 lives. Two years times 24,000 injuries is 48,000 injuries. One honest caveat: those are steady-state projections, and safety benefits phase in over roughly a dozen years of fleet turnover, so the delay shifts the whole benefit curve right by about two years rather than billing 720 deaths to a calendar. The cumulative loss lands in the same neighborhood. It just gets collected slowly, in emergency rooms, over the following decade.

If you are buying new in the 2029 to 2031 window, do not assume the sticker's AEB meets the federal bar. Ask specifically about pedestrian detection in darkness and the lead-vehicle avoidance speed, because those are the two requirements most of today's systems fail. Shopping used, the IIHS front crash prevention rating for the exact model year at iihs.org/ratings is the only public proxy for how close a car is to the coming standard. And when the extension proposal finally publishes, it gets a public comment period. That is the last lever before 2031 is locked in, so use it.
