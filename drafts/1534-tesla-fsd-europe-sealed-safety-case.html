Ten Countries Approved Tesla's Self-Driving. The Evidence Is Sealed.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 16, 2026

On April 10, 2026, the Dutch vehicle authority RDW approved Tesla's Full Self-Driving for the roads of the Netherlands. Ten European countries have since accepted that decision for their own roads. Not one of them ran its own test. Evidence behind the approval is sealed as a trade secret, and the regulator will not say what its testing found. Meanwhile, in the United States, the same driver-assistance system sits under two open federal investigations, one for running red lights and one for going blind in glare. A December vote could extend the sealed approval to all 27 EU member states at once.[1][2][3]

Approval arrived through Article 39 of EU Regulation 2018/858, a provision built for technologies too novel for existing standards. A national authority grants a provisional type approval with an exemption from normal requirements, and other member states may recognize it without conducting independent testing of their own. That architecture was designed for standardized physical properties: brake distances, crash structures, headlight angles. Applied to a self-learning neural network under active federal investigation, it produces something else: a cascade of continental approvals whose shared evidence cannot be publicly examined, because the manufacturer claimed trade-secret protection over the testing methodology and the regulator accepted it. Ten countries sounds like momentum. It is 64.4 million people out of roughly 450 million, 14.3 percent of the EU population, against the 65 percent a bloc-wide vote needs. Every one of those approvals traces to a single Dutch file no member of the public has seen.[1][4][6]

Secrecy was not incidental. Emails obtained by Reuters through public records requests show Tesla pressed RDW from late 2024 to keep FSD review documentation from public view. In an April 2025 exchange, a Tesla representative sought written confirmation that a specific document would "never" be released and asked how RDW would ensure it was "withheld from public disclosure," stating the company could not provide further information until that was settled. RDW replied that manufacturers can always seek a trade-secret exemption. Tesla claimed it broadly. Regulators in six European countries, including Germany, Norway and Denmark, have told Reuters they cannot release FSD testing data for the same reason. Even the approval certificate and its exemption annex, which would say which models, hardware and software the approval covers, were not publicly available as of October 2026.[1]

RDW's own confidence retreated within two months. In April 2026 the agency announced FSD was "safer than other driver assistance systems." By June that had softened to "at least as safe as other driver assistance systems." No data was provided in support of either claim. Norway's Public Roads Administration did get a look at the data RDW shared, and officials there noted it shows Tesla's vehicles are less safe than the company's publicly published statistics claim. Tesla's headline figure, that FSD cars travel seven times farther between crashes than the average driver, counts only crashes where airbags deployed, compared against a database capturing all crashes serious enough to require a tow. Marco Benedetti of the University of Michigan Transportation Research Institute corrected the comparison to a consistent severity threshold. Claimed advantage collapsed from roughly seven times to approximately 1.1 times. Statistically indistinguishable from the average driver. Neither Tesla nor RDW has addressed Norway's finding publicly.[1]

Now the American ledger. NHTSA Preliminary Evaluation PE25012, opened October 7, 2025, covers 2,882,566 Teslas with FSD after 58 incidents, 14 crashes and 23 injuries, alleging the software runs red lights and drives the wrong way. Several cars came to a complete stop at a red light and then drove into the intersection anyway. Multiple incidents happened at the same intersection in Joppa, Maryland, which suggests the problem is repeatable. NHTSA Engineering Analysis EA26002, upgraded March 18, 2026, covers 3,203,754 Teslas after nine incidents including one fatal crash, alleging FSD fails to detect when its own cameras are blinded by glare, fog or dust and fails to warn the driver until immediately before impact. In May 2026 Tesla reported a record 207 Autopilot and FSD crashes to NHTSA in a single month, the highest monthly total in the history of the ADAS reporting program, more than the company reported for the entire year of 2021. Tesla accounts for roughly 85 percent of all ADAS crash reports in the US industry.[2][3][1]

Fairness requires the other side, stated at full strength. RDW told Reuters it makes its own trade-secret determinations and withheld nothing at Tesla's request. Its stated justification reportedly echoed Tesla's language almost verbatim, but the agency's position is that the determination was independent. FARS data shows the Tesla Model 3 and Model Y with the lowest fatality rates in this site's dataset, though on small death counts from a young fleet, and a per-mile average cannot capture the specific failure modes the probes document. Provisional approval is the lawful route for novel technology, and ten sovereign decisions are not a rubber stamp. France and Sweden are objecting, which proves the process has friction. A December vote at the Technical Committee on Motor Vehicles needs a qualified majority, 15 of 27 states holding 65 percent of the EU population, and it may fail. If it does, the Dutch approval becomes invalid after six months and the national permits lapse. A reasonable reader could call that the system working.[1][4][7]

What to do: driving a Hardware 4 Tesla in one of the ten countries? Remember the system is supervised Level 2, not self-driving. RDW's own approval says so in so many words. Treat every red light as a supervised event, because the federal record shows the car can stop correctly and then go anyway. Watch the December committee vote. If you pay the 99 euros a month, know what you are renting: a system whose safety case you cannot read. In the United States, check NHTSA's investigation records for PE25012 and EA26002, and report odd behavior through the vehicle owner questionnaire portal, because those complaints are what opened the red-light probe in the first place.[2][8]
