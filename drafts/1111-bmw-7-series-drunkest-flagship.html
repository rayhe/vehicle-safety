Sobriety Report

The BMW 7 Series Packs Every Safety System Known to Physics. Its Drivers Rank 9th in Impairment.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 9, 2026

According to the toxicology reports—and there are a lot of them—the flagship of BMW's lineup is driven by some of the most chemically enthusiastic humans in the entire dataset. Of 230 BMW 7 Series drivers tested after fatal crashes between 2014 and 2023, 26.1 percent came back alcohol- or drug-positive, the 9th-highest impairment rate among all 307 nameplates in the federal data.[1]

It is not a one-car fluke: seven of the forty most-impaired nameplates wear luxury badges. Leading the luxury table, the Infiniti QX56 sits at number 7 with 26.3 percent, the Cadillac CTS at 10 with 25.9 percent across 931 tested drivers, the Lincoln LS at 17 with 25.2 percent, the Infiniti Q50 and Mercedes-Benz E-Class tied at 34 and 35 with 23.5 percent each, and the Lexus LS at 39 with 23.3 percent.[1] For calibration: the median sedan lands at 21.4 percent and the median SUV at 19.6 percent. That puts the 7 Series 22 percent above the sedan median and the QX56 34 percent above the SUV median.

These are the cars with the most safety technology ever bolted to a chassis. Night vision, driver-attention cameras, lane-keeping, auto-braking: a computer watching the road harder than any human ever could. It can practically drive itself down the autobahn, yet the thing sitting in the driver's seat is the least sober part of the machine. Carmakers spent two decades engineering the car to compensate for the driver, and the flagship numbers suggest the driver is outpacing the engineering.

Now for the uncomfortable math: a 2010 BMW 750i with 400 horsepower currently costs less than a used Honda Civic. Flagship depreciation runs faster than any other segment's, which means a lot of these “luxury” drivers are not executives in new $95,000 sedans. They are young men in $8,000 used 7 Series with bald tires and deferred maintenance. Exhibit A: the Lincoln LS has been dead since 2006 and still ranks 17th. The wealth story does not hold; this is a cheap-horsepower story wearing a luxury badge, and the data cannot separate a 2022 760i from a 2008 750i.

FARS toxicology is not a census, and testing rates vary by state and coroner, so 26.1 percent describes tested drivers, not all drivers, and those are real caveats. Impairment means any BAC above zero or any drug positive, including below-limit alcohol and substances that linger days after use. Coverage spans 2014 to 2023, and exposure is uncontrolled: night miles, driver age, city versus highway, but none of that erases a top-ten ranking.

What you should do: understand that no safety rating survives the driver. In 2024, 30 percent of American traffic deaths involved an alcohol-impaired driver: 11,904 people, one every 44 minutes.[2] If you shop used luxury, the five-star rating was earned by sober crash-test dummies; budget for the tires and brakes the previous owner skipped, because worn rubber on a 4,600-pound sedan erases the engineering. And know that the federal rule that would force every new car to detect impaired drivers missed its November 2024 deadline and still has not been issued, so no showroom car is required to stop a drunk driver yet.[3] IIHS told the agency not to shirk the assignment, arguing a mandate would force the detection technology to mature.[4]
