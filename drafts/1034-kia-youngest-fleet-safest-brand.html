The Gap

Kia Is the Safest Mainstream Brand in America. Its Cars Are Also the Youngest.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 1, 2026

Kia builds the safest mainstream cars in America. Strange sentence to type. Kia spent the nineties as the punchline of every dealership joke about getting what you pay for, and now the federal morgue data says its sedans die at 0.53 per 100 million miles while Honda’s die at 2.44.[1] That is not a rounding error. That is a 4.6-to-1 gap, running the wrong direction from every reputation you have ever heard.

4.6×
How much deadlier Honda sedans are than Kia sedans, per mile driven, in FARS 2014–2023

I aggregated all 337 models in NHTSA’s fatality data into brand fleets, weighted by actual miles driven, because the numbers are the whole point. Among sedans: Honda 2.44, Nissan 2.42, Chevrolet 2.10, BMW 1.80, Toyota 1.64, Ford 1.64. Then the floor drops out. Subaru 0.57. Mercedes-Benz 0.53. Kia 0.53. Acura 0.48. The SUV table tells the same story with different victims: Ford 1.05, Chevrolet 1.02, Jeep 0.79, and Kia at 0.20, the lowest of any brand with a real sample. Pickups: Chevrolet 1.20 deadliest, Toyota 0.86 the least bad of the honest entries.

So Kia wins. Hand out the trophies, update the commercials. Except there is a second table, and it ruins everything.

I computed the average model year of the cars that actually died, weighted by deaths. Honda’s dead sedans average model year 2006.4. Toyota’s: 2007.6. Nissan’s: 2010.1. Kia’s: 2015.1.[2] Read that again. The average Kia sedan in a fatal crash is nearly nine years newer than the average Honda. A 2015 car was born with mandatory stability control, side-curtain airbags, and a structure designed for the small-overlap crash test.[4] A 2006 car was born with a CD player.

This is not a Kia story. It is an age story wearing a Kia costume. Kia’s American fleet is young because Kia’s American history is short; the brand only started selling real volume here in the 2000s, so no 1998 Sephia is still commuting to haunt the statistics. Honda has been selling Accords since bell-bottoms. Strip out the birth certificates and the brand ranking collapses into a calendar ranking.

And here is the part that should make Nissan executives uncomfortable. Nissan sedans die at 2.42, essentially tied with Honda’s 2.44. But Nissan’s dead fleet averages model year 2010.1, nearly four years newer than Honda’s. Adjust even crudely for age and Nissan is the worst mainstream sedan brand in America by a distance. Honda gets to blame the calendar. Nissan does not.

Fair is fair, so here is the counterweight, stated at full strength. Mercedes breaks the pure-age theory: its dead sedans average 2009.8, older than Nissan’s, and its rate is 0.53, tied with Kia. Money still matters. Mass still matters. A 2009 E-Class was engineered by people with a budget and bought by people with assets to protect. Age explains most of the brand gap, not all of it. The VMT denominators are travel-survey estimates, not odometer readings, so treat the exact ordering among close brands as noise. And the Civic and Accord skew toward young drivers, who crash more in anything with four wheels. Demographics are not controlled here, and they never are.

What to do: When you shop, especially used, read the birth certificate before the badge. A 2016 Kia Forte (0.40) is a safer bet than a 2008 Honda Accord from the 3.07 era, whatever the badge snobbery says. Two regulatory birthdays matter: 2012, when stability control became mandatory, and roughly 2015, when small-overlap structures went mainstream. Buy the newest car you can afford. Then check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because even young cars get recalled. Youth is no immunity; it is just better odds.

Methodology

Brand rates use the same formula as the per-model rates in the dataset: total brand deaths divided by total brand VMT, scaled identically (verified against published per-model rates before aggregating). Only brands with 500+ deaths in the class are ranked, to keep small samples from wagging the table. The death-weighted mean model year weights each model year’s death count by the year itself, restricted to model years 1995–2023, so a handful of 1980s survivors cannot drag the average. The Ram pickup rate (0.12) is excluded as a documented fleet-denominator artifact.

Limitations

FARS captures fatal crashes only, about 40,000 deaths a year against roughly 6 million total crashes, so a brand with low fatality rates could still injure plenty of people. No control for driver age, geography, or urban versus rural mix, and the death-weighted model year conflates fleet age with per-year risk rather than cleanly separating them. Survivorship bias cuts deep here: the 2006 Accords still on the road in 2023 are disproportionately cheap used cars driven by high-risk owners, so part of the “age effect” is really a who-can-afford-new-cars effect. Model year 2024 and newer vehicles are absent from the 2014–2023 window entirely.
