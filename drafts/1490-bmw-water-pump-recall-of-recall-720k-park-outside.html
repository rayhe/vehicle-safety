BMW Recalled the Same 720,796 Cars for the Same Fire. The First Fix Didn't Fix It.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 13, 2026

Fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: your BMW might be on its second recall for the same fire, and the second recall has no fix. In August 2024, BMW recalled 720,796 cars because an improperly sealed water pump connector could let in water, short circuit, and catch fire.[2] Dealers inspected the pumps, replaced them and the plug connectors where needed, and installed a protective shield, free of charge, and owner letters went out in March 2025. Done. Except it was not done, because on November 4, 2026, BMW filed campaign 26V733, recalling the same 720,796 cars for the same connector, and this time the remedy is a parking instruction.[1][3]

720,796
Vehicles recalled twice for the same water pump connector. About 1 percent actually carry the defect, and BMW cannot say which ones.

Read the two filings side by side, because the words are the giveaway. Campaign 24V608, filed August 14, 2024: "An improperly sealed electrical connector on the water pump may be exposed to water and short circuit." Campaign 26V733, filed November 4, 2026: "An improperly sealed electrical connector on the water pump may be exposed to water and short circuit." Same sentence. Same cars, from 2012 to 2018 3 Series sedans through X5 xDrive40e plug-in hybrids, with the 3 Series alone accounting for 236,702 of them, the X3 another 163,728, and the 5 Series 110,102.[1][4] Two years, one new campaign number, and a defect description copied and pasted without a single word changed, which is either admirable consistency or a confession that nothing about the underlying problem ever moved.

What changed is the remedy, and it changed in the wrong direction. Most recalls travel from "no fix yet" to "fix available." This one went backwards. In 2024 the remedy was concrete: inspect, replace the pump and the connector, bolt on a protective shield. In 2026 the remedy is a sentence: "Owners are advised to park their vehicles outside and away from structures until the recall remedy is completed. The remedy is currently under development."[1] No parts. No date. No shield. Just a driveway. And the filing adds the line that should make every owner who complied the first time sit down: "This recall replaces NHTSA recall number 24V608. Vehicles already repaired under the previous recall will need to have the new remedy completed." You did everything right. You brought the car in, got the shield, kept the letter. You are back at the start, with a car BMW now says to keep away from structures.

Here is the arithmetic that makes the park-outside order stranger. NHTSA's 2024 record estimated that 1 percent of the population actually carries the defect.[5] One percent of 720,796 is about 7,200 cars with a live short-circuit risk, hiding inside a recall of 720,796, and nobody can tell the 7,200 from the 713,000. So all 720,796 owners get the same instruction and the same wait for a remedy that does not exist yet, while the cars sit outside in the rain, which is the exact condition that lets water reach the connector in the first place. A recall that covers 100 cars to catch 1 is a blunt instrument. A recall that covers 720,796 to catch 7,200 is a confession that the manufacturer cannot tell a good connector from a bad one, and the parking instruction is what you do when you have run out of engineering answers.

Count the days. August 14, 2024 to November 4, 2026 is 812 days. Owner letters for the first recall went out March 28, 2025, so some owners had their "fixed" car for barely 19 months before being told to park it outside.[2] Against that, the field evidence is thin: about 10 US field instances that relate or may relate to the issue, with no known accidents or injuries.[4] Ten reports, zero injuries, and a park-outside order on three-quarters of a million cars. That ratio is the whole story in miniature: a defect so rare that almost nobody will ever meet it, and so serious that BMW will not let you park the car in your garage until it figures out a second fix. Both of those facts are true at the same time, and the tension between them is what makes this recall feel absurd rather than reassuring.

What to do depends on which BMW is in your driveway. If you own a 2012 to 2018 BMW 2, 3, 4, or 5 Series, X1, X3, X4, X5 xDrive40e, or Z4, park outside and away from structures until the remedy is completed, and do not wait for the December letter to start. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls starting December 25, 2026, when VINs become searchable; the campaign is 26V733, and it supersedes 24V608.[1] If your car was already repaired under the 2024 recall, you are still in this one, because a repair receipt from the first campaign is not proof the car is clear of the second. If you are shopping a used 2012 to 2018 BMW from this list, verify 26V733 status before signing, and treat a 2024 repair receipt as a red flag rather than a clean bill of health. And remember the risk is not limited to driving: a parked car in rain can short, which is why the advisory is park outside, not just drive carefully.

Skeptics get their turn, because they have a real point. Ten field instances across 720,796 cars over two years is a tiny rate, and zero accidents or injuries is the number that matters most. Maybe the 2024 remedy worked for most cars, and BMW is re-recalling out of caution after a handful of post-repair failures, which is exactly what a responsible manufacturer should do. Recalls of recalls are common, and usually they mean the system is working: field data found the gap, and the manufacturer closed it. A park-outside advisory with no remedy yet sounds alarming, but it is the standard holding pattern while a new fix is engineered, and the 1 percent estimate means roughly 713,000 of these cars were never going to short at all. Fair. Hold that alongside the other fact: the shield did not shield, the same cars are back, and the second fix does not exist yet, so the system working looks a lot like the system failing twice.

Limitations, stated plainly: per-model counts, the supplier name, the field-instance count, and the December 25, 2026 letter and VIN dates come from automotor.news's reading of BMW's Part 573 report, not from the NHTSA API record directly, although the API record confirms the population, the filing dates, the park-outside flag, and the remedy text. NHTSA's 1 percent defect estimate is from the 2024 record for 24V608; the 2026 record publishes no defect percentage, so the figure of about 7,200 affected cars is an extrapolation from the 2024 estimate, not a 2026 measurement. BMW has not disclosed how many post-repair failures triggered the re-recall, or what the new remedy will be, so do not claim the shield failed in a specific number of cases. And the "about 10 field instances" figure is as reported in the Part 573 filing; the filing's exact wording was not independently verified.
