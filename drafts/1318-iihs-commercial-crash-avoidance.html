The Gap

Your Sedan Has Better Crash Tech Than the 14,000-Pound Truck Behind It

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 29, 2026

IIHS ran its first crash-avoidance tests on cargo vans and heavy-duty pickups this month, and the results read like a warning label for everyone else on the road. Eleven commercial vehicles went through the same protocols the institute uses for family cars. Nine of the eleven scored marginal or poor at avoiding a collision with another vehicle. Statistically, the machines most likely to kill you in a crash are the ones least equipped to avoid one.[1]

6,215
people killed in 2024 in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans, 16% of all U.S. roadway deaths

Look closer and it gets worse. Only the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned a good rating in the vehicle-to-vehicle test, and none of the four Class 3 pickups managed better than acceptable in either category, with the Silverado 3500HD, the Sierra 3500, and the F-350 all scoring poor at avoiding other vehicles. Motorcycles proved nearly invisible: several systems issued no warning and applied no braking in most motorcycle trials. Even the best pickup, the Ram 3500, only reached marginal.[2][3]

At the bottom of the table sit America's favorite workhorses. Chevrolet's Express 2500 and GMC's Savana 2500 rated poor in both tests, and the reason is not subtle: neither van has automatic emergency braking, not as standard equipment, not as an option, with forward collision warning available only as an extra. A 2027-model van you can buy today will not hit the brakes for you. Fleet managers have bought these vans for decades, and now a public rating says what the window sticker does not.[1]

Pedestrian testing produced its own horror show. Several trucks struggled more with a child dummy in daylight than an adult at night, and in some trials the child was detected at a distance, then "disappeared" from the system as the truck closed in, because the sensors sit up near the rearview mirror on a very tall front end. Only two vehicles earned good pedestrian scores: the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 and Rivian's RCV-Delivery 500, both electric. So the industry knows how to build this. Most of it just has not bothered.[1][5]

Part of the story is regulatory. Many federal safety standards that apply to your sedan, airbag requirements among them, simply do not apply to commercial vehicles. That technology gap is a policy gap. IIHS president David Harkey called the results "a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers" and pointed at the obvious: "Front crash prevention is especially important for these larger, heavier vans and trucks because of the danger they pose to other road users."[1]

To be fair, the fleets have a defense. Operators will tell you crash rates are mostly about drivers, hours, and mileage, not sensor suites, and they are not entirely wrong. Express and Savana vans are cheap partly because they are simple, and municipal fleets with their own shops have reasons to keep them that way. A test-track rating is not a body count. But 6,215 dead in one year is a body count, and a 14,000-pound truck with optional collision warning is a policy choice, not a law of nature. Sprinter proves the tech fits.

If you buy for a fleet, the scoreboard is now public: Sprinter 2500 for vehicle-to-vehicle, BrightDrop 400 or Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 for pedestrians. If you ride a motorcycle, treat every work truck as if its radar is decoration, because IIHS just showed you that for several of them, it nearly is. And if you are driving with a 2027 Express in your rearview mirror, remember it is not braking for you. Nothing is braking except you.
