## Angle (1-2 sentences)
On November 24, 2026, NHTSA proposed moving the automatic emergency braking mandate (FMVSS 127) from September 1, 2029 to September 1, 2031 — two more years before new cars must brake for you at up to 62 mph without touching the car ahead. NHTSA's own estimate for the rule is at least 360 lives and 24,000 injuries prevented per year, so the delay is priced, on the agency's own math, at roughly 720 lives and 48,000 injuries deferred — while the agency's own NCAP testing shows today's systems still fail the high-speed and low-light tests the rule would require.

## Verified facts
- **The move:** NPRM published/proposed November 24, 2026. Major automakers: Sept. 1, 2029 → Sept. 1, 2031. Small-volume manufacturers, final-stage manufacturers, alterers: Sept. 1, 2030 → Sept. 1, 2032. It is a proposal, not a final decision; 60-day public comment via regulations.gov, Docket NHTSA-2026-2476.
- **What FMVSS 127 requires:** forward collision warning + AEB on most light vehicles; systems must activate up to 90 mph and avoid collisions with lead vehicles and pedestrians across test scenarios at increasing speeds; the headline performance bar is no-contact stops with a lead vehicle up to 62 mph and pedestrian detection day and night.
- **Why NHTSA says it is delaying:** preliminary NCAP testing on model-year 2025 vehicles shows current AEB systems struggle to avoid contact consistently in high-speed and low-light scenarios. The NPRM also clarifies test speed tolerances and adds procedures to verify systems detect a malfunction and alert the driver. NHTSA is seeking comment on practicability, false-positive braking activations, and safety risks of unexpected braking.
- **The lawsuit:** the Alliance for Automotive Innovation (GM, Toyota, VW, Hyundai, others) sued in early 2025 to block the rule; the 62-mph no-contact requirement is the core objection ("practically impossible with available technology"). Suit pending as of November 24, 2026.
- **History:** the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (Nov 15, 2021) ordered the rule. NHTSA's draft proposed 3 years to comply; the May 2024 final rule allowed ~5 years (Sept 2029), saying the technology needed time to mature. NHTSA reconsidered in Nov 2024, kept 2029. A Jan 2025 regulatory freeze delayed the Nov 2024 changes to March 2025. Now the agency proposes 2031 — seven years after the final rule, ten years after the law ordered it.
- **The IIHS cross-check (November 2026 batch):** the same month NHTSA says the technology is not ready, IIHS's updated moderate-overlap tests show makers fixing rear-seat protection fast when tested — Nissan revised the Kicks' rear belts after a Poor rating and earned Good plus a Top Safety Pick — while the redesigned 2026 RAV4 (Acceptable) and CR-V (Poor) still fail the back-seat bar. Testing pressure works; the question is whether the delay is about readiness or about who pays for it.

## Original contribution: the delay math
All calculations from the figures above; arithmetic by The Crash Report.

- **The headline number:** 360 lives/year × 2 years = **~720 lives**; 24,000 injuries/year × 2 years = **~48,000 injuries**. That is the size of the benefit stream NHTSA's own estimate attaches to a two-year slip — deferred, not erased, but deferred past the point where the people in those crashes get any say.
- **The honest caveat (stated in the article, not buried):** 360/year is NHTSA's steady-state estimate at full fleet implementation, not a 2029 invoice. The real cost of the delay is two model years of new cars (roughly 15–16 million vehicles a year in the US, ~31 million across the two years) rolling off the line without FMVSS-127-grade AEB, each carrying the weaker performance bar for its ~12-year road life. The 720 figure is the right order of magnitude for the deferred benefit, not a body count with names attached.
- **The cost-benefit ratio the delay inverts:** $354 million/year in compliance cost vs. $5.82–7.26 billion in lifetime savings — a return of roughly 16-to-1 on the agency's own numbers. The two-year extension saves automakers at most ~$708 million in deferred compliance spending against ~$11.6–14.5 billion in deferred lifetime savings (two years of the mature annual benefit stream, same steady-state framing as the lives figure).
- **The adoption paradox:** AEB is already standard on 98% of 2026-model versions (NHTSA equipment records). The rule was never about presence — it is about performance: no-contact at 62 mph, pedestrian detection at night, no false-positive phantom braking. NHTSA's own NCAP data says today's near-universal systems fail those tests. So the delay is not "two more years before cars brake for you" — it is two more years before cars brake for you *well enough to count*.
- **The decade tally:** law ordered the rule Nov 2021 → final rule May 2024 → compliance Sept 2029 (as finalized) → proposed Sept 2031. That is a 10-year runway from order to compliance for a technology already in 98% of new cars.

## Strongest counterargument (must appear in article)
NHTSA's case is not frivolous, and the article must say so at full strength. Its own NCAP testing on 2025 models shows current systems cannot reliably meet the 62-mph no-contact bar or the low-light pedestrian tests — mandating a standard the fleet cannot meet on schedule would mean either mass noncompliance, rushed systems that phantom-brake on highways (NHTSA is explicitly seeking comment on false-positive activations and unexpected-braking risks), or a rule that exists on paper while the cars on the road do something else. The Alliance's lawsuit is pending, and a rule that loses in court helps nobody. Two years of testing time for a system that has to work at 90 mph in the dark is a defensible engineering call, and the voluntary 98% adoption rate means the cars rolling off lines in 2030 will mostly brake for you anyway — just not to the federal test standard. The delay costs real lives only if the stricter performance bar would actually have been met in 2029, and NHTSA's testing says it would not have been.

## Limitations (must appear in article)
- The 720-lives / 48,000-injuries figures are NHTSA's steady-state annual estimates multiplied by two — an order-of-magnitude framing, not a counted death toll. The true cost depends on fleet turnover, how fast the stricter systems would have ramped, and whether the 2029 deadline was achievable at all.
- The NPRM is a proposal. The 60-day comment period (Docket NHTSA-2026-2476) could change the dates, the test tolerances, or the outcome. Nothing is final until the final rule publishes.
- FARS 2014–2023 data in this repo cannot isolate AEB-equipped vs. non-equipped crash outcomes by model year; the IIHS 50%/56% effectiveness figures are the best available proxy and come from earlier-generation systems.
- The $354M cost / $5.82–7.26B savings figures are NHTSA's 2024 estimates, not independently verified here.

## Actionable takeaways
- The rule is a proposal with a 60-day comment window (Docket NHTSA-2026-2476 at regulations.gov). Anyone can comment — including you, including the family whose next car will be a 2030 model.
- Shopping now: AEB presence tells you almost nothing — 98% of 2026 models have it. Ask for the performance: IIHS front crash prevention ratings (vehicle-to-vehicle and vehicle-to-pedestrian, day and night) are the public test that separates systems that brake from systems that brake in time.
- If you own a 2025–2026 car, your AEB is voluntary-spec, not FMVSS-127-spec. Treat it as a backup, not a guarantee — especially at highway speed and at night, exactly where NHTSA's testing says it struggles.
- Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls; AEB-related recalls (phantom braking, loss of AEB) are filed under forward collision avoidance.
