By The Numbers

Waymo Drove 271 Million Miles Without a Driver. The Insurance Industry Checked the Math.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get worse, which for this column is practically a miracle. Waymo published its biggest safety update yet on September 24: 271.3 million fully driverless miles through June, five cities, and for the first time a body count in reverse — 841 injury crashes and 55 serious-injury crashes that the company says simply did not happen. Eighty-two percent fewer injury crashes than human drivers. Ninety-five percent fewer serious ones.[2]

81% vs 82%
Injury-crash reduction found by IIHS's independent audit vs Waymo's own claim. One point apart, from datasets built by people with opposite incentives.

That second number is the one that matters, because two months earlier the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety published its own audit, and IIHS is funded by auto insurers, not by Waymo. Lead researcher Eric Teoh pulled every AV crash report filed under NHTSA's Standing General Order from 2021 through 2024 in San Francisco, Phoenix, Los Angeles, and Austin, then did the thing nobody does: he threw most of them out. Duplicates, automation-not-engaged, off-public-road, no actual crash — gone. Then he applied a "would a reasonable person report this to police" filter, the same standard humans live under. Of 736 automation-engaged crashes, only 22 percent survived. Eighty-nine Waymo crashes, 50 Cruise, 10 Zoox, 10 everybody else.[1]

On the crashes that remained, with mileage data only Waymo was willing to provide (50 million driverless miles against 222 billion human miles in the same cities and years), the gap held. Sixty-eight percent fewer crashes overall; eighty-five percent fewer single-vehicle crashes; eighty-one percent fewer injury crashes. And Teoh included the Austin result where Waymo's rate ran 4 percent *higher* than humans, which is the detail a team cooking results would have quietly lost in a spreadsheet. The uncomfortable version of the claim survives the harshest filter available. That is not supposed to happen.[1]

Some calendar math for scale: 271.3 million miles at 12,000 miles a year is roughly 22,600 driver-years of exposure in a single dataset. Still a rounding error against the 3.2 trillion miles Americans drive annually, but past the point where you can wave it away as anecdote. And the methodology deserves the attention the headline won't get. AV companies must report every scrape to the feds; human drivers report about half of all crashes and two-thirds of injury crashes. IIHS built its filter specifically to erase that reporting advantage, discarding 78 percent of AV crashes, and the injury-crash reduction still landed within one point of Waymo's own claim. The strongest evidence for the robot is the version its critic would accept.[1][2]

Now the full-strength case against everything I just wrote, because the data demands it. Waymo operates in geofenced, mapped, sunbelt cities on surface streets. No snow. No ice. No unmapped rural highway at midnight in Montana. These numbers prove safety inside the operational design domain, and the domain is doing a lot of the work. Waymo's own benchmark for serious injuries carries no underreporting adjustment, which makes the 95 percent figure its softest claim, not its hardest. And the robots fail differently than we do: on September 21 NHTSA opened a probe into comma.ai's openpilot after five crashes that killed three people, most involving stopped vehicles the system did not adequately detect, and on September 4 a driverless Zoox drove around a road-closed barricade in Las Vegas in the first month of its paid exemption service. Per-mile superiority and catastrophic edge-case blindness can coexist. They do coexist.[3][4]

So what do you do with this, given you cannot hail a Waymo in most of America? Two things. First, the tech stack doing the work (pedestrian detection, cyclist detection, automatic emergency braking) is the same stack in cars you can buy today, so when you shop, demand an IIHS "good" pedestrian front crash prevention rating and a "good" or "acceptable" vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention rating; those are the 2026 Top Safety Pick+ criteria for a reason. Second, steal Teoh's method: whenever any company quotes you a safety percentage, ask what fraction of crashes they counted before they started dividing. The 22 percent filter is the question to ask of every autonomy safety claim you will ever read, including the ones that agree with you.[1][5]
