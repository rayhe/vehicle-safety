# Research: 1080 — The Height Tax (Melbourne pedestrian-height study)

**Article #1080 · Journalist: Rex Driverton · Kicker: The Gap**
**Status: RESEARCH complete 2026-10-06**

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
University of Melbourne researchers analyzed 10,000+ pedestrian crashes in Victoria (2012–2025) and priced vehicle height in deaths: every 10cm of height adds ~11% to a pedestrian's fatality odds; a US study independently measured 22% per 10cm of front-end height. The fleet keeps growing taller anyway — a per-centimeter death tax, paid by everyone outside the car.

## Original contribution: the height-tax arithmetic
Input: Melbourne elasticity e = 1.11 per 10cm of vehicle height (odds ratio).
- A vehicle ~30cm taller than a medium sedan (typical ute/pickup vs sedan): odds multiplier = 1.11^3 = 1.367 → **~37% higher pedestrian fatality odds**, height alone.
- A vehicle ~45cm taller (full-size pickup vs sedan): 1.11^4.5 = 1.606 → **~61% higher**.
- US sanity check with Tyndall (front-end height, e = 1.22): 30cm → 1.22^3 = 1.82 (+82%); 1.25m cap → 509 US deaths avoided/yr.
- Melbourne's own policy number: sedan-sized fleet → 8.4–9.9% fewer pedestrian deaths. Applied to ~7,000 US pedestrian deaths/yr: **~590–690 lives/yr**. Tyndall's independent 1,000 extra deaths from light trucks in 2021 is the same order of magnitude — convergence, not coincidence.
Method: multiplicative odds, observational estimates, rough by design; presented as illustration with inputs shown.

## Limitations (honest accounting)
- Odds ratios, not absolute risk; baseline pedestrian crash probability is low.
- Victoria 2012–2025 fleet differs from US fleet; height measured as overall vehicle height (Melbourne) vs front-end height (Tyndall) — not the same quantity, which partly explains 11% vs 22%.
- Observational: height correlates with weight, speed, road type, driver behavior. Big vehicles cluster on higher-speed rural roads; the elasticity may partly capture where they drive, not just what they are.
- Doesn't prove causation; doesn't model AEB/pedestrian protection standards that have improved since much of the sample period.

## Strongest counterargument
Occupant protection is also a design choice: the same height and mass that endanger pedestrians protect the people inside. Consumers rationally buy bigger vehicles to protect their own families in a fleet of big vehicles — a collective-action trap, not individual irrationality. And crash-avoidance tech (AEB) cuts crash probability regardless of height.

## Actionable takeaways
- Shopping? IIHS's pedestrian front-crash-prevention ratings are the number that predicts outcomes for people outside the car; ask about them, not just occupant stars.
- Front-end height is now a safety spec worth comparing across models.
- Check your VIN for recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls (free remedies), and support pedestrian-protection provisions in upcoming NHTSA rulemakings — IIHS's own numbers say a 1.25m height cap would save ~500 American lives a year.
