# Research: NHTSA to Rewrite 29-Year-Old Headlight Rules While Crash Data Says Glare Isn't the Killer

**Slug:** `1103-nhtsa-headlight-glare-rule-rewrite`
**Journalist:** Rex Driverton (deadpan, paradoxes; fits the complaint-vs-data paradox)
**Kicker:** Investigation
**Kill test:** PASS. First FMVSS 108 lighting rewrite in 29 years (2 days old) + an IIHS finding nobody in the complaint chorus talks about: glare crash footprint flat at 1-2 per 1,000 nighttime crashes while complaints exploded. The real nighttime killer is darkness, not glare. That's a paradox piece with a policy hook, not a data dump.

## The news hook
- **Oct 6, 2026 (Reuters, David Shepardson):** NHTSA announced negotiated rulemaking to update FMVSS 108 (vehicle lighting). Will modernize rules for LEDs, dusk sensors, automatic high beams, replaceable light sources; evaluate headlamp height and aim; address glare. Administrator Jonathan Morrison: "Today's standards do not allow automakers to deploy the latest safety-improving lighting technologies." Complaints about glare have increased; lawmakers pushed for max brightness standard; taller trucks/SUVs blamed for more glare.
- Federal headlight min/max brightness standards **unchanged since 1997** (per IIHS).

## Original contribution (required)
1. **The inversion:** The complaint boom and the crash data point opposite directions. IIHS lab testing shows measured excessive glare FELL from 21% to 3% of models (2017-2025) while public complaints ROSE. Complaint volume is decoupled from actual glare — a testable mismatch the site has never run.
2. **The one-driver math:** IIHS's own explanation — one car's headlights help one driver see while adding perceived glare to everyone else — implies an asymmetric ledger: ~19-23% fewer crashes for buyers of good headlights vs. 0.1-0.2% of nighttime crashes with any glare coding. The beneficiary count dwarfs the victim count by orders of magnitude.
3. **The daylight irony:** most "glare-coded" crashes happened in daylight, sun low on the horizon. Headlights are being blamed for what the sun does.

## Counterargument (at full strength)
The 0.1-0.2% figure is only as good as police coding, and police coding of glare is notoriously unreliable. NHTSA itself admits no quantified glare-dose-to-crash relationship. The people suffering most — older drivers who now avoid night driving entirely — are invisible in crash stats by definition: they don't crash because they stopped driving. Discomfort is a real welfare loss even if it doesn't kill. Brighter LEDs DO dazzle older eyes disproportionately (70+ most affected). The complaint boom may be measuring genuine quality-of-life harm that crash data cannot see.

## Limitations
- Glare attribution in police reports is subjective and likely undercoded; IIHS's 0.1-0.2% is a floor, not a ceiling.
- No FARS breakdown on this (FARS doesn't code glare); relies on 11-state police-report samples.
- Negotiated rulemaking outcome unknown; final rule likely years away. No claim about what the new standard will require.
- Aftermarket LED retrofit bulbs in halogen housings are a real-world glare source the IIHS studies don't isolate.

## Actionable takeaway
- Shop by IIHS headlight rating: vehicles with Good-rated headlights have ~19% fewer nighttime single-vehicle and 23% fewer nighttime pedestrian crashes than poor-rated. Check iihs.org/ratings before buying.
- Auto high beams matter: NHTSA's rewrite targets them; turn yours on.
- If you drive a tall truck/SUV: your lamps sit at car-driver eye level — the glare is geometric, not malicious.
