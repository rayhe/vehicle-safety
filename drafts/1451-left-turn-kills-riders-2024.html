The Left Turn Killed Riders in 1,532 Crashes Last Year. The Bike Was Going Straight.

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
November 9, 2026

Every rider knows the car. It waits in the oncoming lane, blinker ticking, driver staring straight through you. NHTSA's 2024 motorcycle data finally puts a number on what happens next. In 1,532 fatal crashes last year, the other vehicle turned left across a motorcycle going straight.[1] That is 45 percent of every fatal crash involving a bike and another vehicle. Not a drunk rider. Not a speeding rider. A rider going straight, and a car turning into the rider's path.

1,532
Fatal two-vehicle motorcycle crashes in 2024 where the other vehicle turned left, 45% of all such crashes (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 824)

Run the comparisons and the shape gets worse. Both vehicles going straight accounted for 689 fatal crashes, which means a left-turning car killed riders more than twice as often as two vehicles simply proceeding through. In 78 percent of fatal two-vehicle crashes, the motorcycle was struck on the front, the one direction that gives a rider no time and no escape.[1] Against the full death toll of 6,228 motorcyclists in 2024, those 1,532 crashes work out to roughly one in four rider deaths, and that is the floor, since a single crash can kill both rider and passenger. Motorcycles are 3 percent of registered vehicles and 0.7 percent of all miles traveled. They are 16 percent of all traffic deaths.

None of this makes riders innocent, and the same report is happy to say so. 37 percent of riders in fatal crashes were speeding, against 22 percent of passenger car drivers, and riders aged 15 to 20 were speeding in 56 percent of their fatal crashes. 26 percent of killed riders were alcohol-impaired, 1,529 of 5,950, and 40 percent of riders who died alone in single-vehicle crashes were drunk, against 18 percent in multi-vehicle crashes. 35 percent of riders in fatal crashes held no valid motorcycle license, two and a half times the 14 percent rate for car drivers in fatal crashes.[1] Young riders are the one group moving the wrong way: deaths under age 15 more than doubled, 14 to 30, while ages 15 to 20 rose 20 percent and 21 to 24 rose 15 percent, all in a year when total rider deaths fell 2.2 percent. Plenty of blame sits on the bike. Just not the plurality of it, not where another vehicle is involved.

Peer-reviewed work backs the pattern, with a wrinkle worth naming. IIHS researcher Eric Teoh, writing in Traffic Injury Prevention in 2023, found left-turning vehicles were the most frequent fatal two-vehicle motorcycle crash type at 26 percent for 2017 through 2021.[2] His definition was narrower, only a vehicle turning left in front of an oncoming motorcycle, while NHTSA's 2024 count also includes bikes passing or overtaking. Different rulers, same verdict: the left turn is the deadliest configuration on the road for a rider, and the federal crash-prevention test has never once put a motorcycle in front of a car's automatic emergency braking to see if the car would stop. That is the FMVSS 127 gap riders petitioned NHTSA to close in October, and it is the same gap that lets a car turn left across a bike it never registered.[3]

Helmets deserve their own paragraph, because the 2024 data runs a natural experiment on them. Only 17 states, plus the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico, require helmets for all riders. In states without a universal helmet law, 48 percent of motorcyclists killed were not wearing helmets. In states with one, 11 percent.[1] Same country, same year, same federal crash data. A 4.4-fold gap in who dies unhelmeted, decided by a state line. Reported helmet use among killed riders was 67 percent, and 48 percent among killed passengers, which means a third of dead riders and half of dead passengers were bareheaded at impact, in states where no law required a helmet and none was worn anyway.

So what do you do with it? If you drive a car, treat every left turn as the most dangerous thing you will do on that trip, because for the rider coming toward you, it is. Wait for a gap you can actually see, not one you hope is there. A motorcycle's speed is systematically underestimated by drivers judging a single headlight, and the bike closes distance faster than a car-shaped object in the same lane. If you ride, assume the oncoming car has not seen you, because the data says that assumption is right nearly half the time. Cover the brake through every intersection, slow for every car waiting to turn, and remember that 78 percent of fatal two-vehicle impacts are frontal, which means there is no swerve that saves you once the car commits. Wear a helmet regardless of what your state requires. Check whether your car's automatic emergency braking is rated for motorcycles at all, and if you are shopping, ask the dealer, because the federal test never did.[3]

Fairness demands the narrower version of the claim, because the headline version overreaches. FARS records the maneuver, not legal fault, so "other vehicle turning left while the motorcycle went straight" does not prove the car driver was at fault in every one of the 1,532 crashes. A speeding bike, a stale yellow, a rider running a red light can all put the bike in the wrong while the car is turning. Left turns are also simply the most common crossing maneuver, so part of the 45 percent is exposure rather than unique lethality. Teoh's 26 percent, on the narrower definition, is the conservative floor. What survives all of that is still the story: in fatal crashes between a bike and another vehicle, the car turning across the rider's path is the single most common configuration, it kills more than twice as often as both vehicles going straight, and the machine built to prevent it has never been tested against a motorcycle.
