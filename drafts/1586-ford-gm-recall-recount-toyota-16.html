We Re-Ran the Recall Census With the Fixed Method. Ford Didn't Move. Toyota Did.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 25, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they still came back the same. Ford is still at 40 recall campaigns touching 2026-model-year vehicles, GM is still at 15, and Toyota, which I corrected from 7 to 15 two weeks ago, is now at 16. That last number changed the day after the correction published, because Toyota filed a new recall on November 12 for a Tundra camshaft that can crack, snap, and stall the truck.[1][5]

0 of 73
2026-model-year recall campaigns across Ford, GM, Toyota, and Honda flagged as fixable over the air

Start with what the recount was supposed to do. Two weeks ago this site discovered its own census method was undercounting Toyota: NHTSA's recalls API returns an error when you query a hybrid-only model by its base name, so Camry, Land Cruiser, Sienna, and RAV4 Hybrid campaigns were invisible to the old queries, and three brand-new Toyota EVs were never in the query list at all. Fixing that doubled Toyota's count from 7 to 15 and collapsed Ford's lead from 5.7 times to 2.7 times. That correction ended with a debt, stated in plain text: re-run Ford and GM with the corrected vocabulary.[2][3][4]

Consider the debt paid, because I queried 81 Ford and GM model variants, including the ones the old method never tried: F-450 and F-550 Super Duty, Mustang GTD, Expedition MAX, E-Transit, Blazer EV, Escalade IQ. Ford came back at exactly 40, campaign for campaign identical to November 11, with the Explorer still leading Ford's lineup at 12. GM came back at exactly 15, verified against the earlier table one campaign at a time. Express and Savana still lead GM's with 4 each, including the same steering-gear nut recalled twice, and nothing moved. Which is the point: the vocabulary bug only ever bit Toyota, because only Toyota sells hybrid-only nameplates whose base names return nothing. Ford and GM were right all along, and now that is a verified fact instead of an assumption.[1]

Then Toyota moved anyway: campaign 26V750, filed November 12, covers 62,490 Tundra pickups from model years 2022, 2023, 2025, and 2026. One of the engine camshafts may have been manufactured incorrectly, so cracks can develop and a cracked camshaft can snap, stalling the engine. Toyota counted 31 field technical reports and 60 U.S. warranty claims as of October 30. Dealers will inspect the camshaft and replace it free of charge, and owner letters go out starting December 28.[5][6]

Two details in that filing deserve a second look. First, the 2024 model year is excluded entirely, and so are hybrid Tundras. Affected camshafts went into 2025 and 2026 trucks as original equipment and into 2022 and 2023 trucks as service parts, which means some owners are driving a replacement part that was itself defective. Second, this is the Tundra's third distinct engine defect in two years: machining debris could seize the engine, a main bearing could fail, and now a camshaft can snap. Three different parts, three different failure modes, one outcome: the truck stops. Toyota's loss-of-power tally for its newest vehicles now stands at four campaigns with four different root causes, three of them software, one of them a piece of metal.[6][4]

None of this changes the standings as much as the headline suggests. Strip out the paperwork and the order flips back: GM has 14 real-defect campaigns once you remove the missing owner's manual that pads nearly every GM model by one, while Toyota has 12 once you remove its four wrong load-capacity labels. A one-campaign margin on a metric that weighs a wiper-arm nut the same as a snapping camshaft is not a verdict on who builds better trucks. It is a count, and counts are useful and limited at the same time.[3][4]

What to do with this. If you own a 2022, 2023, 2025, or 2026 Tundra with a gasoline engine, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and ask your dealer whether campaign 26V750 covers your truck, because the filing gives no date for when the VINs become searchable and the owner letters do not start until December 28. If you own a 2024 Tundra or a hybrid, this recall is not yours, but check anyway: Toyota has now filed three different engine recalls on this truck, and the model years do not line up neatly. And if you own any 2026-model-year Ford, GM, Toyota, or Honda, know that none of the 73 campaigns touching your car can be fixed over the air, whatever the press releases say about software updates. Every one of them is a dealer visit.[1][5]
