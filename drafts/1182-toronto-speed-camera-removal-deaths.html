By The Numbers

Toronto Pulled Its Speed Cameras in November. By June, 101 of 104 Camera Sites Were Faster.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 15, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better. In November 2025, Ontario's government under Premier Doug Ford switched off Toronto's automated speed cameras, calling them a cash grab. On June 23, 2026, the city's own General Manager of Transportation Services handed city council the measured consequences: across 104 monitored former camera sites, the average 85th-percentile speed rose 4.8 km/h, and speeding increased at 101 of the 104 locations.[1]

4.8 km/h
Average rise in 85th-percentile speed across 104 former camera sites. Speeding increased at 101 of 104 locations studied.

Here is the table, with the denominators, because the denominators are the whole story. The share of vehicles traveling 11 km/h or more over the limit went from 2.0 percent to 8.1 percent. In 30 km/h zones, the share exceeding the limit by 16 km/h or more went from 1.4 percent to 7.2 percent. On roads posted at 50 km/h or higher, that share went from 0.5 percent to 2.9 percent, a roughly 480 percent relative increase built on a 2.4-point absolute shift.[2] Both framings are true. Only one of them tells you anything.

And the counts that do not need percentages: two fatal collisions occurred within 100 metres of former camera locations. Fatal collisions in the December-to-May window returned to 25, the highest figure in five years. By June 2, Toronto police had logged 23 fatal collisions citywide for the year, a 28 percent increase over 2025.[3] Police data as of October 5 show pedestrian traffic deaths on track for a five-year high, and motorcyclist deaths at 11, up 57 percent year over year.[4] For context, 2025 was Toronto's safest year in a decade: 39 traffic fatalities total.

Now the physics, because "three miles an hour" sounds like nothing and it is not nothing. AAA Foundation research on impact speed puts a pedestrian's risk of severe injury at roughly 10 percent at 16 mph, 25 percent at 23 mph, and 50 percent at 31 mph. Death risk runs about 10 percent at 23 mph and 25 percent at 32 mph.[5] The curve is steepest exactly in the 30-to-50 km/h band where urban streets live. A 4.8 km/h shift across the entire driving population is not one driver going slightly faster; it is the whole distribution sliding up the steep part of the curve. Same research, bonus finding: the risk to a 70-year-old pedestrian struck at 25 mph is about the same as the risk to a 30-year-old struck at 35 mph. Age is worth ten miles an hour.

Ottawa ran the same experiment with the same result. At eight former camera sites there, compliance with posted limits was above 85 percent with the cameras on; after the province forced them off on November 14, fewer than half of drivers complied.[6] On Toronto's Parkside Drive, vehicles traveling 20-plus km/h over the limit rose more than 500 percent year over year.[7] Meanwhile Ontario is defending the repeal in court under Bill 56, and Toronto's June 23 report is, in the most precise sense, a quantified, self-authored causation exhibit. A defendant municipality rarely hands the plaintiffs their best evidence ten weeks early. Toronto did.

What you should do with this depends on where you live. In Toronto: 101 of 104 former camera sites are faster now, including the ones that used to ticket drivers in school zones. For everyone else, this is a natural experiment in what happens when automated enforcement goes away, and the answer is measurable within months. If your state or province is debating camera bans, show a legislator two numbers: compliance above 85 percent with cameras, under 50 percent without. That is not a cash grab. That is the behavior.

Limitations, because I am not a press release. The report measures speed changes at former camera sites and counts fatalities citywide; it does not prove the repeal caused any specific death, and 2026 factors like traffic volume, weather, and enforcement levels are uncontrolled. The 480-percent figure is a denominator trick (0.5 to 2.9 percent). Toronto data is not FARS; definitions differ across borders. And the skeptics are right about one thing: three miles an hour feels like nothing. It is also, per the injury curve, the difference between a 10 percent and a 25 percent chance of killing someone.
