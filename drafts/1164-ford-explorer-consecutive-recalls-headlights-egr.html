Ford Filed Two Recalls One Number Apart. One Blinds Oncoming Traffic. The Other Kills the Engine.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 14, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they did not get better. On March 3, 2026, Ford filed two defect reports with NHTSA in the same session, and the agency issued consecutive campaign numbers: 26V121000 and 26V122000.[1][2] Read them back to back and you get a split portrait of the modern automobile. First up: certain 2025-2026 Explorers whose Dynamic Bending Light software swivels the passenger-side headlight in the wrong direction on curves, a failure of FMVSS 108 whose official consequence is increased glare for oncoming drivers. Second: an exhaust gas recirculation valve that can fail without warning across nine Ford and Lincoln models, cutting drive power at speed. One defect is a line of bad code. The other is a valve you can hold in your hand. Same filing day, one digit apart.

1 of 14
NHTSA campaigns touching the 2026 Explorer that list an over-the-air remedy

Start with the headlight one, because it is the weirder of the two. Adaptive bending headlights exist to look into curves, which is a genuinely good idea: more light where you are going, less where you are not. Ford's implementation inverted the idea on the passenger side, so on a left curve the right lamp swung right, into oncoming traffic, while the driver's own path stayed dark. NHTSA's consequence statement is unusually direct for the agency: increased glare to other road users. The fix is a dealer flash or an over-the-air update, free of charge, under Ford's internal number 26C12.[1]

Older-school carnage: the EGR valve. Nine models are in the population, spanning Ranger, Nautilus, Mustang, Maverick, Explorer, Escape, Corsair, Bronco Sport, and Bronco. Press tallies put the valve recall at 47,804 vehicles and the headlight recall at 35,772 Explorers.[4] There is no download for this one. The remedy is a dealer visit and a new valve, and until it happens, the engine can lose power with no warning.[2]

Here is the part I counted myself. NHTSA's database lists fourteen distinct recall campaigns touching the 2026 Explorer. I counted twice, because the first total felt like a typo. Windshield air bubbles, loose trailer hitch bolts, a windshield that may not be bonded to the frame, sticky seat switches, a driver's seat that reclines itself during remote start, incorrectly installed piston circlips, connecting rod bearings that can seize, piston domes that can fail, an airbag vent that may not open.[3] Of those fourteen, exactly one, the backwards headlight, lists an over-the-air remedy. The other thirteen need a service bay and your afternoon.

Ford's scale problem is the backdrop. Autoblog reported this week that Ford has filed 75 recalls in 2026, the most of any automaker, after a record 153 last year, and Hearn's data puts the average wait for a remedy at 174 days.[5] Only sixteen of Ford's recent recalls were handled over the air. So this pair is a controlled experiment in what the software-defined car actually delivers: the code defect gets patched in days, while the metal defect joins a 174-day queue.

In Ford's defense, recall volume is partly the oversight system working as designed. NHTSA fined Ford $165 million in November 2024 for handling recalls too slowly, the second-largest penalty in agency history, and a company under outside watch files early and files often.[5] More campaigns can mean faster reporting rather than worse cars. And the OTA headlight fix is genuinely the fastest class of remedy Ford offers.

What to do: own a 2025-2026 Explorer? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, then accept the OTA update or book the flash for 26V121, because mis-aimed beams blind the drivers coming at you, not you. For 26V122, schedule the dealer visit; if the engine stumbles or loses power, get to the shoulder. Shopping? Ask whether a recall's remedy is a download or a visit. On the 2026 Explorer, the score is one to thirteen.

Limitations

The population counts (35,772 / 47,804) come from secondary press summaries of the Part 573 reports, not the Part 573 PDFs themselves, so treat them as reported rather than re-derived. The OTA count comes from matching "over-the-air" or "OTA" in the API Remedy text; campaigns may permit OTA without the API saying so, and dealer-only language can lag behind reality. The 14-campaign census counts campaigns touching the 2026 Explorer in NHTSA's API, which includes shared-platform defects and compliance items alongside real hazards; it is a recall-load metric, not a danger ranking. No crash or injury figures are on record for either campaign. FARS cannot speak to this story: these are 2025-2026 vehicles with no fatal-crash history in the data window.
