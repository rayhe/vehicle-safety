NHTSA Spent Two Years Investigating Jeeps That Catch Fire While Parked. Yesterday It Closed the File.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

On September 29, NHTSA closed its preliminary evaluation into 2021-2025 Jeep Wranglers and Gladiators that catch fire while parked with the ignition off.[1] The probe opened in September 2024, after fires kept appearing to start near the power steering pump in vehicles nobody was driving. Two years later the recall population covers 1,076,999 vehicles, and the agency's position is that the recall addresses everything it was examining.[2]

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better.

A loose electrical connection in the electric-hydraulic power steering pump wiring is the defect. A connector manufactured outside spec never seats fully. Resistance climbs. Heat builds. Once it melts, the combustible material sitting next to it does what combustible material does. FCA's own Part 573 filing counts 63 customer assistance records, 72 field reports, of which 35 are confirmed to originate at the pump interface, plus 12 other service records.[3] One injury. Zero accidents. On May 28, 2026, the company's Vehicle Regulations Committee determined a safety defect existed.

51
reported fires NHTSA attributes to the defect across 1,076,999 vehicles

Now the part that should make you look twice at the closure. NHTSA's September 2024 probe covered about 781,500 vehicles from model years 2021 through 2023. Stack the June 2026 recall against it: 1,076,999 vehicles from 2021 through 2025. Subtract the two and you get 295,499 vehicles, a 37.8 percent expansion, that the probe never examined. Globally the count tops 1.3 million, with roughly 106,000 in Canada, 23,000 in Mexico, and about 125,000 in other markets.[4] In other words, the agency closed its file on a recall that had already outgrown the investigation.

FCA's filing also carries one date that refuses to fit. Field reports were received from July 13, 2019, to March 28, 2026. Builds in the US recall population start on June 24, 2020. So the earliest report predates the affected build window by nearly a year.[3] Either a report got misattributed, or the loose connector shows up outside the official population. Either way, it is a question, not a conclusion. Worth knowing before you assume the recall list is the whole story.

+37.8%
recall population growth beyond the 781,500 vehicles the NHTSA probe examined

Fifty-one fires against 1,076,999 vehicles works out to roughly 4.7 fires per 100,000 vehicles. Low. Each fire still threatened a garage, a house, or the car parked next to it, which is exactly why the park-outside advisory exists and exactly why a low per-vehicle rate is not the number that matters to the person whose Wrangler sits in an attached garage.

For owners, the closure changes nothing. Both a do-not-drive and a park-outside advisory appear in the Part 573 filing. What owners get is a free dealer inspection, with repair or replacement of the wiring harness or the pump as needed. Until that happens, the garage is the one place the vehicle should not be. A driveway away from the house will do.[5]

If you own a 2021-2025 Wrangler or Gladiator, including the 4xe, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 26V363000, FCA recall 21D. If it is open, park outside until the dealer inspects the pump wiring. If you are shopping for a used one, run the VIN before you hand over money. An unrepaired recall follows the vehicle, not the owner, and no federal law stops a private seller from selling you a used Jeep with an open recall.[2]

The other side of this spreadsheet deserves equal time. Fifty-one fires across 1.08 million vehicles is about 0.005 percent. Your individual odds of a parked fire are microscopic, and FCA's own words describe the guidance as an abundance of caution. NHTSA closing the probe is not a scandal; it is routine process. Once a recall covers the examined population, keeping the investigation open adds paperwork, not safety. Stellantis expanded the recall beyond the probe's model years voluntarily, which is the system working. The defect determination came on May 28; the public recall followed twelve days later, with a remedy promised within two months. By recall standards, that is fast.[4]

Limitations: FARS does not enter into this story; these fires caused zero accidents, so fatality data has nothing to say about it. Secondary reporting of NHTSA's count is where the 51-fire figure comes from, while FCA's filing reports 72 field reports with only 35 confirmed at the pump interface, so pick your denominator carefully. Nobody has published a remedy completion rate for 26V363 yet, which means the number of fixed Jeeps is unknown. I did not independently read the ODI closing resume for this article.
