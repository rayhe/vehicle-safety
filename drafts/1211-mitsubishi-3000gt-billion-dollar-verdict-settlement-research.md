## Angle (1-2 sentences)
A Philadelphia jury awarded Francis Amagasu $1.01 billion over his 1992 Mitsubishi 3000GT's seat belt and roof design — then Pennsylvania's Superior Court threw the whole verdict out because the jury was never told the one legal doctrine that mattered. This week Mitsubishi settled, closing a nine-year case that says as much about how courts handle crash safety as about any car.

## Verified facts (do not invent others)
- **Crash:** November 11, 2017, Bucks County, PA. Francis Amagasu was driving a 1992 Mitsubishi 3000GT with his 14-year-old son Katutoshi as a passenger. He attempted to pass another vehicle, lost control, left the road, struck three trees, and the car rolled over. (Autoblog, MotorBiscuit)
- **Injuries:** Francis was wearing a seat belt. His head struck the roof during the rollover, fracturing his cervical spine and leaving him quadriplegic. His son sustained minor injuries. (Autoblog)
- **Alleged defects:** (a) a rip-stitch (web loop) feature in the seat belt designed to tear during a collision, adding approximately four inches of slack; (b) roughly three inches of head clearance under the roof; (c) inadequate warnings. These were the family's allegations; the overturned verdict did not establish them as findings. (Autoblog, citing court records; confirmed in the Superior Court opinion via Justia: plaintiffs' experts Sicher and Dr. Fijalkowski testified the web loop "ripped apart during the first impact" and the 3000GT's roof "did not provide proper head clearance.")
- **The verdict (Oct 2023, Philadelphia County jury):** $1,009,969,395.32 total — ~$156M compensatory (incl. $925,477 past medical, $12.5M future care, $2.2M lost earning capacity, $20M past non-economic, $120M long-term suffering), $20M loss of consortium to wife Soomi Amagasu, and $800M in punitive damages. (Carscoops, MotorBiscuit, citing court documents)
- **The appeal:** Mitsubishi appealed May 2024. In December 2025, the Pennsylvania Superior Court (Judge Judith Olson, 49-page opinion, J-A18007-25, *Amagasu v. Fred Beans Family of Dealerships et al.*) vacated the verdict and ordered a new trial. The defect never caused the crash, so the crashworthiness doctrine applied: the jury had to assess what injuries would have occurred with a safer alternative design. The trial court (Judge Sierra Thomas Street) instead instructed the jury that if the belt was defective from sale, Mitsubishi was "liable for all the harm caused by the occupant restraint system." The Superior Court called the refusal to give crashworthiness instructions "not a logical and dispassionate determination." (Justia opinion text; Greenberg Traurig alert; NewsBeep)
- **Important nuance from the opinion:** Amagasu's own attorneys never argued a defect caused the crash — only that it worsened the injuries. Mitsubishi's experts (Dr. Nightengale, Dr. VanArsdale) testified the crash was so severe the head would have struck the roof regardless and the web-loop deployment did not cause the injury. (Justia)
- **The settlement:** Mitsubishi Motors Corporation announced (week of Oct 7, 2026) that Mitsubishi Motors North America settled; amount undisclosed; a loss will be recorded in Q2 of FY ending March 31, 2027; no change to earnings outlook; no admission of liability. Reported by Autoblog Oct 18, 2026. (Autoblog, TipRanks)
- **The 3000GT met or exceeded all federal safety standards when sold** (early 1990s). (Carscoops, MotorBiscuit)

## Original contribution
The Crash Report is the outlet that should explain the crashworthiness doctrine to drivers: in nearly every defect case, the defect did NOT cause the crash — it caused the second collision, your body against the car's interior. That distinction is the entire legal ballgame, and a $1B verdict died on it. Consumer frame: a belt designed to rip for chest safety adds slack; slack in a low-roof sports car puts your head in the roof. Know your head clearance before you buy a low-slung coupe.

## Strongest counterargument
The verdict's reversal does not vindicate the 3000GT's belt design or its three inches of head clearance. Mitsubishi settled after winning the appeal — companies don't pay nine-figure sums they could walk away from. Plaintiffs' experts gave coherent testimony (rip stitch + low roof = quadriplegia), and the retrial might well have re-awarded massive damages with proper instructions. The legal error was about jury instructions, not about whether the car was safe.

## Limitations
- The Superior Court opinion is marked NON-PRECEDENTIAL (Superior Court O.P. 65.37) — it binds this case, not future ones as broadly as a published opinion.
- Settlement amount undisclosed; "substantial loss" not quantified by Mitsubishi.
- The underlying defect allegations were never retried — treat them as allegations, not findings.
- The 20-minute deliberation detail (publiclawlibrary.org) is single-sourced; use only if attributed, or drop it.
- The 3000GT met federal standards at sale — the case was about design above the floor, not compliance.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
1. Buying a low-slung sports car? Measure your seated head clearance. The Amagasu case turned on roughly three inches of it plus a belt that added four inches of slack. Older coupes were built when roofs were low and belts were simple — the combination is exactly what the crashworthiness doctrine fights over.
2. Energy-management seat belts (rip-stitch, load limiters) are designed to trade slack for chest survival in frontal crashes. That trade is tuned for the crash mode, not for rollovers. In a rollover-prone vehicle, belt geometry and roof clearance matter more than the belt's sophistication.
3. If you or a loved one is ever injured where the car "didn't cause the crash but made it worse," the legal term is crashworthiness — and it requires proving a safer alternative design would have changed the outcome. Document everything; the defense will argue the crash alone did the damage.
