Full Self-Driving Stopped at the Red Light. Then It Drove Through It.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

Let's talk about what happens when a machine does everything right and then does the thing anyway. On October 7, 2025, NHTSA opened a federal investigation into 2,882,566 Tesla vehicles equipped with Full Self-Driving, after 58 reports of the software committing traffic violations.[1] Running red lights, driving into oncoming lanes, turning onto one-way roads facing the wrong direction.

2,882,566
Teslas with FSD under federal investigation — and at least some of them stopped for red lights before running them

In several cases, the car came to a complete stop at the red light, then drove into the intersection anyway. That is not the failure mode of a blind system, and this is not a failure to detect the signal. The car saw it, stopped, and went anyway, which is the failure mode of a system that decided.

This is where the engineering gets uncomfortable, because Tesla markets FSD as SAE Level 2, partial automation with a fully attentive driver who stays responsible at all times.[1] NHTSA's investigation zeroes in on the gap between that theory and practice: whether FSD's driving inputs can "forestall the driver's supervision when they are unexpectedly performed." Translation from regulator-speak: when a car stops for a red light and then accelerates into cross traffic, does a human hand on the wheel have time to do anything about it? That is not a philosophical question so much as a control-loop latency question, and the answer so far looks bad.

Then there is Joppa, Maryland, where NHTSA coordinated with the Maryland Transportation Authority and State Police and found multiple incidents at the same intersection, suggesting the behavior is repeatable.[1] A repeatable traffic violation in a deterministic system is not a freak occurrence. It is a scenario the software can reliably produce, which means it is a scenario that can be fixed, tested, and verified. Tesla has since acted on that intersection, according to the agency. Good. One down.

The second pattern is worse in its own way. The resume catalogs FSD lane changes into opposing traffic, crossings of double-yellow lines, and turns onto roads despite wrong-way signage.[1] Wrong-way driving is one of the most lethal things a vehicle can do, and it is a perceptual failure, not a decision failure. A system that cannot read a lane marking cannot be trusted to arbitrate which way traffic flows.

Meanwhile, getting answers has been slow: after the incident count grew roughly 60 percent to about 80 reports by December, NHTSA issued a sweeping information request covering complaints, crash data, video, event recorders, and CAN bus files, due January 19, 2026.[3] Tesla missed it, told the agency 8,313 records needed manual review at roughly 300 a day, and received a second extension pushing the deadline to March 9, 2026.[3] A company that can ship an over-the-air update to millions of cars in an afternoon needed eleven weeks to read its own log files. Digest that.

If you run FSD: treat every intersection as a supervised event, because the car is capable of behaviors no human would attempt. File a complaint at NHTSA's public VOQ portal when it does something wrong, because those 44 complaints are literally what opened this investigation. And check nhtsa.gov/recalls quarterly for your VIN, because if this probe ends in a recall, software will fix your car in the middle of the night while you sleep, and you should know when that night is.

The Strongest Counterargument

Human drivers run red lights constantly. NHTSA's own past estimates put red-light running in the hundreds of thousands of violations per day. Fifty-eight incidents across 2.88 million vehicles over months of deployment is a tiny reporting rate, and complaint data is a funhouse mirror: VOQs skew toward dramatic failures, FSD software versions span years, and the incidents mix old and new builds. Weird behavior is not the same as worse-than-human behavior. NHTSA has not answered the comparative question yet. It may never be able to, because Tesla does not publish per-mile exposure data for FSD in a form anyone can audit.

Limitations

This is a Preliminary Evaluation, not a finding of defect and not a recall. Zero fatalities appear in the opening resume; 14 crashes and 23 injuries are what the agency had at the time. The escalation numbers (about 80 reports, the March 9 deadline) come from press reporting of NHTSA's tally, not from a published NHTSA document. I did not run my own analysis on the SGO reports, and the Joppa intersection detail comes from the agency's summary, not from independent verification. FSD deployment mix matters enormously here and I do not have it.
