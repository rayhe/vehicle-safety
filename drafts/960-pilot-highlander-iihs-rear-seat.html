The Gap

IIHS Flunked Both Family SUVs on Rear-Seat Safety. Ten Years of Crash Data Picks the Same Winner.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 21, 2026

Let's talk about the dummy in the back seat. When the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety published its April 2026 ratings for three-row SUVs, the updated moderate-overlap front test put a crash-test dummy in the second row for the first time in the protocol's history, because for decades the front seats got all the engineering attention while the rear seats got the leftovers. Of the three SUVs tested, only the Mazda CX-90 earned a Top Safety Pick+; the Honda Pilot and Toyota Highlander, the two family haulers Americans actually buy by the hundreds of thousands, both walked away with nothing.[1]

0.29 vs 0.42
FARS fatality rate, deaths per 10M VMT, 2014-2023: Honda Pilot vs Toyota Highlander

Anyone who distrusts lab coats should look at the federal numbers. We cross-checked the IIHS grades against ten years of federal fatality data, and the lab result is not a fluke. In the federal Fatality Analysis Reporting System, the Honda Pilot recorded 514 deaths from 2014 through 2023 at a rate of 0.29 deaths per 10 million vehicle miles traveled, while the Toyota Highlander recorded 1,106 deaths at 0.42. The Highlander figure covers a fleet of 2.1 million vehicles and the Pilot 1.4 million, so this is not a small-sample artifact.[2] One test happens in a lab with instrumented dummies. The other counts actual bodies on actual roads. They point the same direction.

The grade breakdown is where Honda's engineering homework shows. Both vehicles were disqualified from any 2026 award by the same evaluation, yet they did not fail equally. The Pilot scored Acceptable, the second of four grades, with no specific injury finding against its rear occupants. The Highlander scored Marginal, the third of four, and the test data recorded increased head and neck injury measures for its rear passengers, with rear restraints and dummy kinematics also graded Marginal.[1] That is the seat your kid sits in.

Honda saw this test coming years ago. Starting with the 2025 model year, Honda revised the Pilot's rear seat belts specifically to improve second-row passenger protection, which is exactly what the updated evaluation measures, and the Pilot earned a Top Safety Pick+ for 2025 under the criteria then in force. Then the Institute tightened the criteria for 2026, an Acceptable no longer qualified for either award, and the Pilot lost its designation without a single bolt changing on the vehicle.[1] Award lists are moving targets. Engineering is not.

Toyota made no equivalent change, and the scoreboard beyond the crash test widens the gap in ways that matter at the dealership. The Pilot carries Good-rated headlights on every trim as standard, the only vehicle in its comparison group to manage that, while the Highlander falls short of that standard across its range.[1] Blind-spot monitoring and rear cross-traffic alert are standard equipment on the Pilot; on the Highlander they live on higher trims, so the cheapest Toyota in the showroom is not the same safety proposition as the cheapest Honda. Federal testing agrees with the Institute: in NHTSA's 35 mph front crash test and the side-impact test, the Pilot rates ahead of the Highlander in both, with rollover likelihood running 0.9 to 2.6 percent lower.[1][3]

Now the honest caveats, because data deserves them. FARS counts deaths, not injuries, and the IIHS test measures injury risk to a small-female dummy, which means the two datasets answer different questions and a vehicle can protect against death while still injuring rear passengers. Real-world second rows carry children in car seats, who are better protected than any belted dummy, so the lab result overstates the danger for properly restrained kids. And a Marginal grade does not make the Highlander dangerous: its 0.42 fatality rate sits near the SUV median of 0.35 and is dramatically better than the Ford Explorer, the third big three-row seller, at 1.54 deaths per 10M VMT.[2] Perspective is a safety feature too. One blind spot remains: the CX-90, the round's actual winner, is too new to appear in the 2014-2023 FARS data, so for now the lab result stands alone for Mazda's SUV.

So what do you actually do with this? If your children ride in the second row, the Pilot's rear restraints are measurably better and Toyota engineered no equivalent fix, which is the single most relevant fact in this comparison. Do not shop by the award badge alone, since the Pilot lost its Top Safety Pick+ with zero hardware changes; the criteria moved, not the car. Price your trim honestly: a base Highlander lacks blind-spot monitoring that comes standard on the base Pilot. And whatever you buy, run the VIN through the federal recall database before money changes hands, because no crash rating covers whether the recall repair was ever done.[4]
