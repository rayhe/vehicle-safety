# Research: #1323 — Utah's 0.05 BAC Law, Four Years Later (Dale Impactor III)

## Angle
Utah lowered its per se BAC limit from 0.08 to 0.05 in December 2018 — the only state to do so. A NEW IIHS study (released this week, Oct 27-29 2026) analyzed the first four years of post-law crash data and found the effect held: nearly 9% fewer alcohol-related crashes, 12% fewer alcohol-involved injury crashes, and a 12-21% decline in the alcohol-involved share versus AZ/CO/ID. Dale Impactor III's sardonic take: the fine print matters — and the fine print is mostly favorable.

Distinct from #826 ("Utah's 0.05 BAC Law Deters Heavy Drinkers," which used NHTSA 0.15-fatal data + 2016-2019 window, heavy-drinker deterrence framing). This article is about the new IIHS four-year study itself: methodology (late-added tests, BAC refusals counted), the COVID stress test, and the absolute-vs-relative math.

## Original contributions
1. Effect-size context: Utah started from the lowest alcohol-involved baseline in the country. A 12-21% relative decline on the smallest base in America is a small absolute number — the percentages impress, the body count is modest. (Must phrase carefully: we don't have Utah's absolute crash counts; this is inference, labeled as such.)
2. COVID stress test: the pandemic erased the gains temporarily (46% spike Mar-May 2020, +6%/month over 34 months) and the law's effect survived it — that's a harder test than a clean four years.
3. The comparison-state inflation: Utah's share fell 12-21% *relative* to AZ/CO/ID partly because their alcohol-involvement rates *rose*. The "reduction" is partly other states getting worse — the study's headline number is a net difference-in-differences, not a pure Utah drop.
4. National baseline: 15.1% of FARS fatal-crash drivers nationally are alcohol-positive — so a state starting at a 30% share has far more room (in absolute bodies) than Utah did.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Publicity preceded the law by more than a year, so driver behavior may have changed before the legal change — the study could be measuring a media effect, not a legal one. The comparison states' alcohol-involvement rates increased, so part of the 12-21% "decline" is the control group deteriorating rather than Utah improving. Police-reported alcohol involvement is a judgment call, and counting BAC-test refusals as alcohol-involved is an assumption baked into the numerator. And Utah is the driest-driving state in America; a 9% reduction on the smallest base yields few absolute lives saved, which is the number that actually matters.

## Limitations
- No causal proof; difference-in-differences with rising controls.
- Our FARS dataset has no state-level slice, so the 15.1% national figure is context, not a Utah baseline.
- BAC-refusal imputation inflates the numerator by assumption.
- Publicity lead-time confound acknowledged by IIHS itself.

## Actionable takeaways
- If your state is debating 0.05: the only real-world experiment says it moves the needle — 9% on alcohol crashes, 12% on injury crashes, sustained over four years and a pandemic.
- Practical: 0.05 is roughly one drink for many people; "buzzed driving is drunk driving" is now IIHS's line, not just a slogan.
- What to do: nothing to buy, no VIN to check — this one's legislative. Watch whether other states follow; Utah remains the only one.
