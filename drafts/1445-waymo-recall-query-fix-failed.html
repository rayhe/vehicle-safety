Waymo's School Bus Fix Lasted 354 Days. Now 94% of the 'Fixed' Fleet Is Under Federal Review Again.

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
November 9, 2026

Waymo told federal regulators its school bus problem was fixed on November 17, 2025. Every affected car updated, the recall paperwork filed a month later, case closed. On November 6, 2026, NHTSA opened a new file on the same cars. Most of the reported violations happened after the fix date, the agency said, and now 2,895 of the 3,067 vehicles Waymo recalled are back under federal review. That is 94.4 percent of the "fixed" fleet.[1][2]

94.4%
Share of the 3,067 vehicles in Waymo's December 2025 school-bus recall (25E084) now covered by NHTSA's November 6, 2026 recall query. (Reuters, Nov 9, 2026; NHTSA recall 25E084)

Start with what the cars were doing. Waymo's fifth-generation driving software could look at a stopped school bus, red lights flashing, stop arm extended, kids loading, and decide the bus was an obstruction to drive around. Not a red light. An obstruction. The recall, campaign 25E084, covered 3,067 vehicles running software released between August 20 and November 5, 2025. Waymo pushed the fix over the air between November 5 and 17, told NHTSA every car was repaired, and filed the recall paperwork on December 8. The fix was installed before the recall existed.[2][4]

Paper and pavement disagreed almost immediately. Austin's school district logged Waymo passes on December 11, 12, and 19, less than a month after the fix was supposedly complete, then again on January 12. By mid-January the district's count for the school year stood at 24. Federal investigators traced the January 12 pass to a remote agent in Michigan, a human working 1,300 miles from the intersection, who wrongly told the car the situation did not involve a school bus with active signals. NHTSA opened a fresh probe in October 2026 after new Atlanta footage, and on November 6 it escalated to a recall query, the instrument the agency uses when it suspects a remedy was inadequate. Three federal actions, 385 days, one behavior.[4][1]

Zoom out and the school bus story stops looking like a one-off. Waymo has filed six software recalls since February 2024. Three of them landed in the eight months beginning December 2025: stopped school buses, flooded roadways, freeway work zones. Each one is a software-defined fleet shipping a fix over the air, and each fix is a promise the regulator now has to verify after the fact. A car company recalls a part. A robotaxi company recalls a behavior, then recalls the fix for the behavior, then gets asked whether the fix for the fix worked. That last question is the one NHTSA asked on November 6.[5]

Fairness requires the other side, and it is strong. Zero collisions happened in any school bus passing incident, and Waymo's aggregate crash rates per mile run below human benchmarks in peer-reviewed comparisons. Human drivers in Austin alone blow past school bus stop arms more than 10,000 times a year, which makes roughly two dozen robotaxi passes a rounding error, and a human who does it gets a ticket while a robotaxi gets a federal file. Waymo says it has seen material improvement since the update, and the post-fix passes may be edge cases like the remote-agent error rather than the fix failing wholesale. A recall query is also the system working as designed: the regulator noticed the remedy might not hold, and it asked. None of that answers the question NHTSA actually posed, which is whether the cars stopped passing school buses. The agency's answer, in its own words, is that most of the violations came after the fix.[3][5]

If you ride a robotaxi near a school, treat a stopped bus exactly the same as you would behind a human driver, because the federal file on this behavior is open for the third time in thirteen months. If your kid's district uses stop-arm cameras, know the citations go to the vehicle owner, and for a robotaxi there is no driver to cite, only a company. And the general rule this saga keeps teaching: a "recall completed" headline is not a fix verified. Ask whether violations continued after the update, and whether the regulator ever closed the file. In this case it did not. It opened a new one.
