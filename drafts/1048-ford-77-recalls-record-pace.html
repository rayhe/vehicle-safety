By The Numbers

Ford's 77th Recall of 2026 Just Tied the Record Ford Itself Broke Last Year.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 2, 2026

I ran the numbers, then ran them again, and they didn't get better. Ford issued its 77th recall of 2026 this week: 11,451 Rangers whose 10.1-inch center screens can lose power and take the rearview camera image with them.[1] Seventy-seven recalls in the 272 days from January 1 through September 28. That works out to one recall every 3.5 days, weekends included. At that pace it stops being a quality problem and becomes a manufacturing schedule.

77
Ford recalls in 2026 through September 28, tying GM's 2014 all-time record, with more vehicles already recalled than in all of record-setting 2025.

That number, 77, used to mean something, because it was the all-time single-year record, set by GM in 2014. Then Ford issued 153 recalls in 2025 and turned the record book into a team photo.[2][7] Ford's 2026 has now tied a record that Ford broke a year ago, which is the most Ford sentence ever committed to a dataset.

Before anyone deploys the "but it's better than last year" defense, the table. In 2025: 153 recalls, 12.96 million vehicles, about 84,700 vehicles per recall.[2] In 2026 through September 28: 77 recalls, 14.05 million vehicles, about 182,500 per recall, and you should read that twice.[1][2] Ford is running half as many recalls and has already recalled a million more vehicles than last year. Count down 50 percent, blast radius up 115 percent. Ford recalls fewer things now; they are just bigger: a 4.32-million-vehicle trailer-module software campaign (26V104), a nearly-1.5-million-vehicle backup-camera recall last October, a 254,640-SUV rearview software recall in March.[7][1] Fewer fires, bigger explosions, and the regression line points the wrong way.

Meanwhile, and I checked this three times because it cannot be true, Ford ranked first among mainstream brands in the 2026 J.D. Power Initial Quality Study, the first time since 2010. Up from 15th in 2023, with 41 fewer problems per 100 vehicles, the largest improvement of any mainstream brand, and the F-150, Mustang, and Super Duty each topping their segments for the second straight year.[5] Both things are true at the same time, because they measure different things. J.D. Power grades the first 90 days of ownership; recalls grade the lifetime.[6] The truck is excellent for three months; the fourth month has a schedule.

The cruelest number is not the count; it is 198 days. That is the average time Ford took to remedy recalls issued from 2025 through mid-2026, per NHTSA data compiled by the Detroit News, across 22 recalls, and Ford disputes the analysis.[3] Harder to dispute: dealers report months-long waits for fixes, with owners driving vehicles carrying collision and fire risks because the remedy does not exist yet.[2] This week's Ranger recall arrives with the remedy "under development" and owner letters scheduled for October 5.[1] A recall letter is a promise; the appointment is the product.

What to do: run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls once a quarter, not once, because a clean check in March can be a recall by September at this pace.[10] Before buying used, make the dealer confirm every open recall is completed, not merely identified, since a VIN lookup shows presence and not remedy status. If the dealer says the fix is not available yet, get it in writing, and keep driving only if the notice carries no do-not-drive warning; 198 days is half a year. If you are shopping a 2026 Explorer, it carries 15 recall campaigns on the model year already, per RecallChecks' compilation of NHTSA data.[8] Check the specific VIN, because the model average will not save you.

Here is what this analysis does not prove: these tallies are media compilations of NHTSA filings taken at different snapshot dates, not one database query, so treat the exact counts as directional. One more statistical caution: 14,048,838 divided by 77 is a mean over a skewed distribution; a handful of mega-campaigns do the heavy lifting, and the median recall is far smaller. That 198-day figure covers a 22-recall subset, and Ford disputes it. Now the strongest counterargument, at full strength, because the company's defenders are right about this much: a recall means Ford found a problem and fixes it free, and the true quality sin is suppression, not disclosure. GM's ignition-switch era was a low-recall era because GM hid the defect, not because the cars were good.[9] The honest rejoinder is narrower: the defects are real, the remedy lag converts a fixed-for-free promise into six months of unrepaired driving, and mega-campaigns signal systemic failure modes, not corner cases, while the system keeps working at a volume nobody designed it for.
