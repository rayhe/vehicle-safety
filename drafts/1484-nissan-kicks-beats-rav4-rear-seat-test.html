The Back Seat Decided It: How a Nissan Kicks Beat the RAV4 to a Safety Award

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 12, 2026

Five small SUVs went through the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety's tougher back-seat crash test this fall, and one earned a Good. It was not the redesigned Toyota RAV4, one of America's best-selling vehicles. It was not the Audi Q3. It was the Nissan Kicks, the entry-level subcompact that scored Poor in the same test when it debuted for 2025.[1][2]

Poor → Marginal → Good
The Kicks' updated moderate overlap rating across two hardware revisions: Poor as a 2025, Marginal on early 2026 builds, Good on builds after June 2026.

Start with what the test measures, because the name undersells it. IIHS updated the moderate overlap front test in 2022 and put a crash dummy in the back seat. Front-seat protection had improved so much that the rear became the weak point: rear belts and airbags lagged a generation behind. Scoring tightened again in 2024, specifically on how much force rear belts put on a passenger. A Good in this test is now a prerequisite for any IIHS safety award, and every car in this round aced the front-seat tests. All five earned Good in small overlap front and side. Front-seat protection is table stakes now, and the award gets decided behind the driver.[3][4]

Nissan's fix was not a redesign; it was a belt. Engineers revised the rear seat belt load limiters at the start of the 2026 model year, hardware that lets the belt spool out a fraction under load so the force on a passenger's chest stays below the injury threshold instead of spiking, and that moved the car from Poor to Marginal. Then they revised the rear seat and the limiters again for builds after June 2026, and the car retested to Good. IIHS publishes both tests on the same ratings page, with test IDs, so the climb is on the record: early 2026 builds scored Marginal with rear shoulder belt tension of 4.0 kilonewtons, while post-June builds scored Good at 3.6. Four-tenths of a kilonewton was the gap between a fail and an award. Audi tried the same move on the Q3, modifying rear belts in May 2026 according to secondary reports, and still scored Poor. Same idea, different outcome. Engineering detail is the difference between a fix and a gesture.[1][5]

Q3's failure had a specific shape. Testing found the rear lap belt could slip off a passenger's pelvis, which turns a restraint into a lever aimed at soft tissue. Good ratings everywhere else did not save it; one Poor in this test disqualifies a car from any award. Toyota's redesigned RAV4 landed in the middle and split by powertrain: hybrids scored Acceptable, plug-in hybrids Marginal. Same nameplate, same redesign, two ratings, because the battery pack changes the crash. Chevrolet's Trailblazer also scored Marginal, then missed on headlights and front crash prevention besides.[2][6]

None of that matters if you buy the wrong build month. The award applies only to Kicks built after June 2026. A 2026 Kicks built in May has the old belts; one built in July has the new ones, same badge, same brochure, same model year. Manufacture date lives on the certification label on the driver's door or the B-pillar beside it, and almost nobody looks. Hyundai's Palisade carries the same asterisk from the same round of testing: Top Safety Pick+, but only for models built after November 2025, when Hyundai changed the rear seat belt buckles. Model-year shopping is dead for safety, and production-month shopping is the rule now.[1][7]

Grant the obvious objection, because it has teeth. The Kicks won on crashworthiness while its vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention rates Marginal, second-worst on the scale. That is the system that stops the crash from happening at all, and the RAV4 beat the Kicks there. A car that protects you beautifully in a crash you could have avoided is a strange definition of safe. IIHS agrees, in its own way: the Marginal prevention score is exactly why the Kicks earned Top Safety Pick and not Top Safety Pick+. Look closer and the failure is oddly specific. The Kicks avoided every passenger-car target at 31, 37, and 43 mph, then scored no speed reduction at all on the 31 mph motorcycle target. It sees cars fine and does not see bikes. If you weight avoiding the crash over surviving it, the RAV4 arguably wins and the award misleads. Both readings are honest. Pick the one that matches how you actually drive.[1]

Ratings are not body counts. IIHS scores measure performance in a specific crash scenario, and the redesigned Kicks has no long FARS track record yet. A Good rating predicts nothing about real-world fatality rates until the cars accumulate years on the road. This round covered five SUVs, not the segment. Audi's May 2026 belt change is reported by secondary coverage, not confirmed on an IIHS page. Nobody was injured in any of these tests; the stakes are prospective, and prospective is the honest word for them.

What to do with this. Shopping for a 2026 or 2027 Kicks, open the driver's door and read the certification label before you sign anything, because June 2026 is the line. Shopping for a RAV4, know the hybrid outscores the plug-in hybrid in the rear-seat test, so the cheaper powertrain is the safer one for rear passengers. Shopping for a Q3 or a Trailblazer, ask the dealer what changed since the test, and listen for specifics instead of reassurance. And whatever you buy, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. The habit is the point even when the recall is not the story.
