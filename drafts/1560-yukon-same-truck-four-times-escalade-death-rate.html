GM Sold the Same Truck Four Ways. One of Them Kills Four Times as Often.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

General Motors built the same truck four times, gave it four names, and charged four prices for it. Then the bodies started arriving, and the four trucks turned out to have four different death rates. The GMC Yukon kills at 2.55 deaths per 100 million miles. The Cadillac Escalade, same frame, same era, same basic structure, kills at 0.64.[1] That is a four-fold gap between two versions of one vehicle, and the usual explanation does not work, because the Escalade’s drivers are drunker.

4.0x
The Yukon’s fatality rate divided by the Escalade’s. Same platform, same era, same basic structure

Run the whole family and the pattern holds: Chevrolet Tahoe, 2.49 on 2,592 deaths; Chevrolet Suburban, 1.36 on 593; Cadillac Escalade, 0.64 on 211. All four ride the GMT800 and GMT900 platforms, which is to say they are the same truck with different jewelry.[1] Impairment data refuses to explain any of it: Yukon drivers test impaired 21.4 percent of the time, Escalade drivers 22.0 percent, with alcohol and drug rates nearly identical. Strip the impaired drivers out of both rates entirely and the gap stays exactly where it was, four to one, which means whatever is killing Yukon drivers at four times the Escalade’s pace is not the bottle.[1]

Do the counterfactual and the gap gets a body count: at the Escalade’s rate, the Yukon’s 350,000-truck fleet would have produced about 280 deaths over the decade instead of 1,114, a difference of 834 people on one nameplate. Model years offer the only clue. Yukon deaths pile up in 2001 through 2005, 563 of the 1,114, then thin out, and the Tahoe does the same thing bigger, with 1,355 of its 2,592 deaths in those five vintages, while the Escalade never had a bad vintage at all, its deaths spread thin across every year with 30 at most in any one. Something changed around 2006, and the obvious candidate is stability control, phased in as federally mandatory from 2009 to 2012 and credited by IIHS with cutting fatal single-vehicle crashes roughly in half, but obvious is not proven, because FARS does not record which trucks had ESC.[3][4]

Now the case against all of this, at full strength, because it is strong. Start with the denominator, an estimate built from sales figures and mileage surveys rather than odometers, on the smallest fleet of the four. Escalade buyers are older, wealthier, and drive fewer miles, so the Escalade’s rate may measure its owners rather than its engineering, while police use pads the Tahoe’s number. Add that FARS counts every death in the crash, including people the big SUV killed in the other car, and the badge gap could be an artifact of who buys what and where they drive it, none of which can be ruled out.

If you are shopping a used full-size GM SUV, the 2001-2005 Yukons and Tahoes are the deadliest vintages in the file, and a 2010-or-newer truck from the same lines carries a fraction of the risk, because every 2012-or-newer vehicle has stability control by law. Buy on model year and condition, not the crest. Check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before you pay. Four versions of one truck, four death rates, and one of them is four times the safest, and nobody at GM has ever explained why.
