The Deadliest Model Year on American Roads Is 2005

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 19, 2026

Somewhere in America tonight, a 2005 sedan is merging onto a freeway with no electronic stability control, a twenty-year-old structure, and a driver who has no idea the car is the single deadliest model year on the road. FARS counts 11,363 deaths in 2005-model-year vehicles between 2014 and 2023, more than any other model year, and nearly twice as many as every 2020-to-2023 car put together.[1]

62.3%
Of the 187,058 deaths in the 337 models tracked, in cars built before stability control even began phasing in. Pre-2012 cars account for 71.9% of the dead.

Cut the same ten years of fatal crashes by the date that actually changed what cars can do, and the picture sharpens. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 126 phased electronic stability control in from model year 2009 and required it on every new light vehicle by model year 2012.[3] Cars built before that phase-in began account for 116,597 deaths, or 62.3 percent of the total. Cars from the three phase-in years add 17,894, or 9.6 percent. Everything built since the mandate took full effect accounts for 52,567, or 28.1 percent. Median model year of a person who died in one of these crashes: 2006.

ESC deserves the fuss, because it is the most important safety device most drivers have never thought about. A yaw sensor, a steering-angle sensor, and a computer that brakes one wheel at a time can catch a slide a human driver cannot, in the first 150 milliseconds of a loss of control, before the car ever leaves its lane. IIHS puts the effect at roughly half the risk of a fatal single-vehicle crash and a fifth off fatal multiple-vehicle crashes.[4] That is not a marginal gadget. It is the difference between a skid and a statistic, and three in five of the dead were in cars built before the rule that made it standard even started.

Five model years do the bulk of the damage. The 2003-to-2007 cohort accounts for 53,695 deaths, 28.7 percent of the total, and the top five single years run in order: 2005, 2004, 2003, 2006, 2007. Stretch the window to the whole 2000-to-2009 decade and the share passes half: 94,290 deaths, 50.4 percent. One out of every two people who died in these crashes was in a car built in the 2000s.

Whole nameplates are stuck on the wrong side of the mandate. Among models with at least 1,000 deaths, the Chevrolet Cobalt, S-10 Pickup, Buick LeSabre, Dodge Dakota, Chevrolet Cavalier, and Mercury Grand Marquis are 100 percent pre-2012 model years. The TrailBlazer sits at 99 percent and the Ranger at 98 percent. Look at it by median model year instead and the oldest fleets still killing people are a 1995 Chevrolet GMT-400, a 1998 S-10, and a 2000 Ranger. These are not museum pieces. They are the work trucks and first cars still doing daily duty on American roads.

And the fleet is aging into the cohort, not out of it. S&P Global Mobility put the average age of American light vehicles at a record 12.8 years in its May 2025 release, up from 12.6 in 2024 and 12.1 in 2021, with 289 million vehicles in operation and passenger cars averaging 14.5 years.[2] Do the subtraction and the average car on the road today is roughly a 2012, the hinge year itself. New-car buyers get ESC and a modern structure as standard equipment. Everyone else is driving a fleet that is parked on the line the mandate drew.

Now the honest complication, because the counterargument is real and this dataset cannot settle it. These are raw death counts, not rates. Older cars are disproportionately driven by younger, poorer, and riskier drivers, and this site's own fleet-age work shows impairment rises as fleets age. A 2023 car had one year inside a ten-year crash window while a 2005 car had ten, so the recent-year counts are mechanically small and the article cannot claim a 2023 car is thirty times safer than a 2005 one from these numbers. ESC was also available before the mandate on plenty of 2005-to-2008 cars, so the mandate date is a fleet-level hinge, not a fact about any individual car. What the numbers prove is narrower and still damning: the cars doing the dying are the ones built before the single most effective crash-avoidance technology became mandatory, and the fleet keeps getting older.

What to do: if you drive a pre-2012 car, especially a 2003-to-2007 model, find out whether it has ESC. Watch for the traction-control light at startup, or check the VIN build sheet. No ESC means the biggest safety gap between your car and a new one is not the airbag count or the crash-test stars. It is a computer your car was never given. Shopping used, a 2012-or-newer car buys the mandate as standard equipment, and within the same model the 2012 is a different safety proposition from the 2011. Whatever you drive, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because the oldest fleets carry the most open recalls and a 2005 car has had twenty years to accumulate them.[5]
