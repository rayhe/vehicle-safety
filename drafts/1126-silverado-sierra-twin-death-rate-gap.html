The Gap

GM Builds the Same Truck Twice. The Cheap Badge Kills 24% More Per Mile.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
August 10, 2027

Somewhere in America, a Chevrolet Silverado has killed more people than any other vehicle on the road. Not per mile, not adjusted for anything, just bodies: 9,591 in ten years.[1] America's bestselling vehicle, the Ford F-150, has a fleet 875,000 trucks larger and managed 9,194. Chevy sold fewer trucks and buried more people.

1.25 vs 1.01
Deaths per 100M miles: Chevrolet Silverado vs. its platform twin, the GMC Sierra, with alcohol-positive rates identical to the decimal at 15.7%

Zoom out to per-mile rates and the point sharpens: the Silverado kills at 1.25 deaths per 100 million miles, and Ford's F-150 sits at 1.04. But the number that should embarrass GM's boardroom wears a GMC badge. Park the two side by side: the Sierra rides on the same platform, rolls out of the same factories, offers the same engines and transmissions.[2] It kills at 1.01, which means the same truck with a different grille delivers 24% fewer funerals.

Run through the usual excuses and they all die. On alcohol, Silverado drivers test positive at 15.7% against the Sierra's 15.7%; on drugs, 8.7% versus 9.2%, a rounding error; and on old trucks skewing the count, the model-year death distributions are near-identical, with 50.9% of the Silverado's deaths coming from pre-2005 trucks versus 50.1% for the Sierra: same steel, same booze, same old trucks.

What survives the excuses is who buys which badge. More than 80% of Sierras leave the lot as SLT, AT4, or Denali trims: suburban, optioned-up, driver-assist-equipped.[3] Chevy's Silverado is the volume play, work-truck WTs from the low $30,000s, fleet orders by the dozen, regular cabs rolling to job sites at 5 AM down two-lane rural highways. GM doesn't build two different trucks; it sells the same truck to two different lives.

And the Silverado doesn't just kill more per mile; its crashes are deadlier per crash. Ford's F-150 is involved in more fatal crashes (20,066 to the Silverado's 19,732) yet produces fewer deaths, which means when a Silverado is in a fatal crash, the fatality is a Silverado occupant 48.6% of the time, versus 45.8% for the F-150.[4] A side note for your group chat: the brand with the lifted-truck stereotype, Ram, has the safest per-mile rate of the American trio at 0.78.

A few caveats, stated plainly: the per-mile rates rest on estimated vehicle-miles with roughly 15% uncertainty per model, and the worst end of that error band could swallow most of the 24% gap. The sales-based version, deaths per 1,000 vehicles (1.686 vs. 1.362), shows the same gap, though it is the same arithmetic wearing a different coat. And the strongest case for the badge's innocence is exposure: work trucks drive more miles, carry more occupants, and spend their hours on rural roads where crashes kill instead of injure. This data proves the gap exists; it doesn't prove which variable owns it.

If you're shopping used, the trim matters more than the badge. Fleet-spec work trucks, regular cab, vinyl seats, no driver-assist, are the deadliest configuration on the lot regardless of brand. Buy the crew cab with automatic emergency braking, the thing Sierra's trim mix bundles by default. And check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before the test drive, because that's the part you control.
