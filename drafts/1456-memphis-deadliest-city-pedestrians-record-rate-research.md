Pedestrian deaths fell nationally for a second straight year in 2024 and are projected to fall a third straight year in 2025, with the national rate dropping below 2.0 per 100,000 for the first time since before the pandemic. ## Key verified facts
- **Memphis: 5.5 pedestrian deaths per 100,000 residents (2020–2024)**, the highest rate of any of the 101 largest metros and the highest in the 11-year history of Dangerous by Design. In the first edition (2009), no metro exceeded 3.52. This year the top 14 metros all exceed 3.52.
- **369 pedestrians killed in Memphis, 2020–2024, up from 221 in the previous five years (+67%)**, while the region's population did not grow to match (William's Walk, citing SGA/FARS).
- **Pedestrians are nearly 27% of all traffic deaths in Memphis, vs 18% nationally in 2024** (William's Walk; NHTSA DOT HS 813 818). Memphis also had the highest total traffic fatality rate of any big city, 30.77 per 100,000 (Crash Report #1453 research, FARS).
- **National: 7,080 pedestrians killed in 2024, down 3.9% from 7,367 in 2023**; 71,635 injured, up 5.0%. One killed every 74 minutes, one injured every 7 minutes. 18% of all traffic fatalities (NHTSA DOT HS 813 818, Table 1).
- **2025 projection: 6,732 pedestrians killed, down 7%, third consecutive annual decline**; national rate 1.97 per 100,000, below 2.0 for the first time since before the pandemic; still 5% above the 2019 pre-pandemic level of 6,412 (GHSA, July 2026).
- **Tennessee: 162 pedestrian deaths in 2024, 162 projected in 2025 (flat)**; state rate 2.29 (2024) and 2.26 (2025) per 100,000 vs national 2.13 and 1.97. Memphis (5.5) runs 2.4x its own state's rate (GHSA Table 1 and rate table).
- **The long climb: pedestrian deaths up 72% from 2009 to 2024 nationally** (SGA). 2024 total higher than every year on record since 1982 except 2021–2023. At the current rate of decline, the US would not reach 2009 levels until 2042, and 96,615 more people would be killed walking by then (SGA).
- **Rankings lie:** Charleston, SC moved from #9 to #12 and Tucson, AZ from #3 to #4 in the rankings while both got deadlier in absolute terms; they "improved" only because other metros got worse faster (SGA). Delaware cut its pedestrian fatality rate more than any other state and still ranks 10th deadliest. Of the 20 deadliest states, 19 showed no improvement or got worse (SGA).
- **Disparities:** American Indian and Alaska Native pedestrians die at 3.7x the national average; Black Americans at 1.7x. The two groups are 16% of the US population but 22% of pedestrian deaths 2020–2024 (SGA). Nationally, Black Americans are killed while walking at 171% of the average rate; Native American and Alaska Native pedestrians at 367% (William's Walk).
- **The striking vehicle matters:** SUVs and pickups account for more than half of pedestrian fatalities (GHSA). Tall, blunt front ends are associated with a 43.6% increase in pedestrian fatality risk vs low, sloped front ends (IIHS Hu et al. 2024, via GHSA).
- **Pedestrian crashes are getting more lethal, not just more common:** the share of pedestrian crashes that kill someone rose from 4% in 2015 to 10.1% in 2024, more than doubling (GHSA/CRSS). Vehicles are getting larger and heavier; greater force means pedestrians are less likely to survive.
- **Crash shape (NHTSA 2024):** 76% of pedestrian deaths in the dark; 84% urban; 73% at non-intersections; 24% hit-and-run; alcohol involvement (driver and/or pedestrian BAC ≥ .01) in 43% of fatal pedestrian crashes; 69% male; 90% single-vehicle crashes. Crossing an expressway: 4,170 crashes, 56.7% fatal (GHSA). Speeding in 8.1% of pedestrian fatalities (GHSA).
- **Cost:** $9.4 billion total economic toll of 2024 pedestrian fatalities (GHSA).

## Original calculations (show math in article)
- **Memphis excess deaths:** national 2020–2024 pedestrian deaths = 6,565 + 7,470 + 7,593 + 7,367 + 7,080 = 36,075, average 7,215/year. Average US population 2020–2024 ≈ 334 million, giving a five-year average national rate of ≈ 2.16 per 100,000. Memphis at 5.5 is 2.55x the national rate. Applied to the count: 369 × (2.16 / 5.5) ≈ 145 deaths expected at the national rate. Excess ≈ 369 − 145 = **~225 people** who would not have died had Memphis walked at the national rate over five years.
- **The 67% jump:** (369 − 221) / 221 = **+67.0%** in five-year pedestrian deaths, population flat.
- **Share gap:** 27% vs 18% = pedestrians are **1.5x the share** of Memphis traffic deaths that they are nationally.
- **State gap:** Memphis 5.5 vs Tennessee 2.26 (2025) = **2.4x its own state**; vs national 1.97 = **2.8x the country**.
- **The 2042 math is SGA's own:** at the current decline rate, 96,615 more pedestrian deaths before the US returns to 2009 levels. That is ~13.7 years × ~7,000/year.
- **Lethality doubling:** 4.0% → 10.1% of pedestrian crashes fatal, 2015 to 2024 = **2.5x**.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Memphis's rate is a five-year average over a metro of ~1.3 million people, and small-number volatility cuts both ways: a metro this size sees roughly 74 pedestrian deaths a year, so a bad stretch of a few dozen crashes can move the rate. The national decline is also partly a statistical gift: 2021–2022 were historic highs, so three years of decline still leaves 2025 projected deaths 5% above 2019. And the "designed to kill" framing risks overstating intent: Memphis's danger is a mix of high-speed arterials, poverty, and a warm climate where people walk year-round, not a single design decision anyone voted for. The disparity numbers (3.7x, 1.7x) are real, but they describe where dangerous roads are, not proof that any road was built to target anyone. None of that changes the count: 369 people in five years, in one metro, is not a rounding error, and the rate is the highest SGA has measured in eleven years of measuring.

## Limitations
- SGA metro rates are five-year averages (2020–2024) from FARS; 2025 Memphis data is not yet in the federal file, so the record rate is already two years stale by publication and could have moved either way.
- GHSA 2025 numbers are preliminary state-reported data, adjusted for historical underreporting, and are not directly comparable to FARS (states include deaths after 30 days and some off-roadway crashes; FARS does not). The 6,732 projection is GHSA's, not NHTSA's.
- Memphis metro boundaries follow SGA's 101-metro definition; other analyses using city limits instead of the metro area produce different rates (William's Walk notes some put Memphis more than 3x the major-city average).
- The excess-deaths calculation assumes the national rate is the right counterfactual for Memphis; it is not — Memphis differs in climate, income, vehicle mix, and road design, all of which push the rate up. The ~225 figure is an illustration of scale, not a causal claim.
- FARS counts only deaths within 30 days of a crash on public roadways; the true toll is slightly higher.
- The front-end finding (+43.6%) is an association from IIHS research, not a Memphis-specific measurement; no dataset breaks Memphis pedestrian deaths down by striking-vehicle front-end profile.

## Actionable insights
- If you walk in Memphis (or any Sun Belt metro in the top 14): the danger concentrates after dark (76% of deaths), away from intersections (73%), and on high-speed arterials. Cross at signals, wear something reflective, and assume the driver cannot see you — the data says that is usually true.
- If you drive an SUV or pickup: your front end is the risk factor. GHSA's finding is blunt — tall, blunt front ends raise pedestrian fatality risk 43.6% vs low, sloped ones. Slow down on arterials; the 8.1% speeding share understates it, because speed determines whether a pedestrian crash is survivable at all (4% fatal in 2015, 10.1% in 2024).
- If you live in Memphis: the fix is street design, not slogans. SGA's own prescription is proven countermeasures — narrower lanes, raised crosswalks, pedestrian refuge islands, lower speed limits on arterials — funded and built, with accountability when they are not. Ask your council member which of those is funded on the arterial nearest your house.
- Check the ranking for your own metro at smartgrowthamerica.org — the report covers all 101 largest metros, and a better ranking does not mean fewer deaths: Charleston and Tucson both "improved" while burying more people.
