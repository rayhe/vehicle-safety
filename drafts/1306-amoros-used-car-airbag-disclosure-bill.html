A Seller Who Hides a Missing Airbag Could Face Life in Prison Under a New Bill

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 28, 2026

Before you sign for a used car, ask one question nobody at the lot will volunteer: are the airbags actually in it? Anthony Amoros was 18 when he died in a 2013 crash in a car his family had bought without knowing it had no airbag at all. His name is now on a federal bill that would make hiding that fact a crime punishable by up to life in prison when a death results.[1]

Life
Maximum prison term under the bill when a death results from a knowing sale without disclosure

Representative Mike Lawler of New York introduced the Anthony Amoros Law Right to Know Act in October 2026.[1] Covered sellers include dealerships, auctions, and private individuals, in other words every channel a used car can change hands through. Disclosure means two things at once: a written statement in at least 10-point bold type on the front of the sales contract, receipt, or invoice, plus a conspicuous sign on the windshield. Vehicles manufactured on or after September 1, 1997 are covered. Enforcement would fall to the Federal Trade Commission and state authorities. Penalties scale with outcome: up to 10 years for a knowing sale without disclosure, up to 20 years when serious bodily injury or paralysis results, and up to life when someone dies. Courts could also award an additional $25,000 in an action involving death or paralysis.[4]

Timing matters, because federal safety regulators confirmed a grim data point weeks before the bill arrived. NHTSA confirmed on September 3, 2026 an 11th death tied to a banned Chinese replacement inflator marked DTN60DB, in a Dallas crash on August 27 involving a 2018 Chevrolet Equinox that the agency described as otherwise survivable.[3] Every earlier death involved a Chevrolet Malibu or Hyundai Sonata, plus three severe injuries, and the agency had barred import and sale of the inflators earlier in 2026, its first such ban in more than 20 years. A VIN lookup cannot catch one of these inflators, because an aftermarket part has no link to a vehicle identification number. Only an inspection can. Signs include trim that does not match, tear seams that are absent or poorly formed, faint SRS lettering, and an inflator label that does not match the OEM part number.[3]

Existing federal law already bars dealers, manufacturers, distributors, rental companies, and repair businesses from knowingly disabling a safety device. Private individuals are not on that list. That omission is the hole a flipped salvage car drives through: roughly 2.5 million vehicles totaled by insurers last year eventually returned to American roads, according to an Automotive News estimate reported in October, and retitling inspections vary by state, with many states requiring no airbag inspection at all.[4] Strongest case against the bill is straightforward, and it deserves a fair hearing. Criminal law is a blunt tool for a paperwork failure, private sellers may simply stop selling older cars rather than risk prison over a component they never inspected, and a disclosure signed by a dishonest seller protects nobody. All three points bite. But they argue for pairing disclosure with inspection, not for keeping buyers ignorant: New York has run a dealer version of this law since 2021, and the federal bill extends the same idea to every channel, including the private sale where the original Amoros car changed hands.[2] One honest gap remains in the bill itself. It never mentions counterfeit parts, so whether a car carrying a banned DTN60DB inflator counts as deficient will turn on enforcement interpretation, not on the bill's text.[3]

Buyers should treat a clean VIN check as necessary and nowhere near sufficient. Demand airbag certification in writing, check the history report for deployments and total-loss events, and pay a certified technician to inspect the car before signing, watching for an SRS lamp that never lights at startup. Sellers, even private ones, should fix a missing airbag or disclose it in bold on the contract and on the windshield, because a disclosure costs almost nothing and a hidden defect can now cost a decade or more. Nobody should buy a used car on the assumption that a dark airbag light means an airbag is present. Sometimes it means there is nothing there to light up.
