## Angle (1-2 sentences)

In 2024, crashes involving young drivers (15–20) killed 370 fewer people than in 2023. The young drivers themselves accounted for none of that improvement: their own deaths rose by 6 while passenger deaths fell 221, other-vehicle occupant deaths fell 91, and pedestrian deaths fell 64. Every life saved in 2024 was someone else's. The danger is concentrating on the kid behind the wheel, and the sex split underneath it is worse: young male driver deaths are up 27% since 2015 while young female driver deaths are down 23%.

## Key numbers (all from DOT HS 813 814 unless noted)

### The decomposition (Table 1) — the whole article in one table

| Person type | 2023 | 2024 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young drivers killed (15–20) | 2,156 | 2,162 | **+6** |
| Passengers of young drivers | 1,114 | 893 | **−221 (−20%)** |
| Occupants of other vehicles | 1,617 | 1,526 | **−91 (−6%)** |
| Nonoccupants (pedestrians, cyclists) | 730 | 666 | **−64 (−9%)** |
| **Total** | **5,617** | **5,247** | **−370 (−6.6%)** |

- Young drivers were **41.2%** of everyone killed in crashes involving young drivers in 2024 (2,162 / 5,247) — the driver's share rose as everyone else's fell.
- 10-year view (2015→2024): young driver deaths +13.6% (1,903→2,162); passengers −9%; other-vehicle occupants +15%; nonoccupants +25%; total +10.6% (4,744→5,247).
- 66% of the passengers of young drivers who died (593 of 893) were themselves 15–20 years old.
- Note: the fact sheet's key-findings bullet misprints "increased 7 percent" for the 5,617→5,247 total; Table 1 and the page-2 text both say decreased 7 percent. This article uses the table (decrease of 370, −6.6%).

### The sex split (Table 2) — the crisis is male

- Young male drivers in fatal crashes: +21% since 2015 (3,014→3,657). Young female drivers: −10% (1,337→1,209).
- Young male driver deaths: **+26.7%** since 2015 (1,395→1,767). Young female driver deaths: **−22.8%** (508→392).
- Fatal-crash involvement rate per 100,000 licensed drivers, 2024: young males **56.72**, young females **20.27** — a **2.8x** gap.
- Speeding in fatal crashes (Figure 4): young males 39% (highest of any age/sex group), young females 20% (highest among females). Overall male 21%, female 12%.
- Alcohol in fatal crashes: 26% of young male drivers involved had alcohol in their systems vs 19% of young females.

### Overrepresentation

- Young drivers: 5.2% of licensed drivers (12.4M of 239.8M), but 9.0% of drivers in fatal crashes and 12% of drivers in all police-reported crashes — **1.73x** overrepresented in fatal crashes.
- 4,873 young drivers in fatal crashes in 2024 (−6% vs 5,160 in 2023); total drivers in fatal crashes 55,620 (−4%).
- Young drivers were 10.3% of drivers in single-vehicle fatal crashes vs 8.3% in multivehicle fatal crashes — they crash alone more than they crash into people.

### Restraint + alcohol compound (Tables 5–7)

- 53% of young passenger-vehicle drivers killed were unrestrained (vs 49% of all drivers killed). 15% of young survivors were unrestrained (vs 10% all).
- 29% of killed young drivers had BAC ≥ .01; 25% had BAC ≥ .08 — despite under-21 drinking being illegal in every state. Of the 637 killed with alcohol in their systems, 547 (86%) were at .08 or higher.
- Alcohol involvement among young drivers in fatal crashes is up **34.4%** since 2015 (871→1,171); 24% had alcohol in 2024 vs 20% in 2015.
- Age 19 had the most young drivers killed (581). Age 20 had the highest alcohol share among killed (34%).
- The compound: of drinking young drivers killed, **65% were unrestrained**; of non-drinking young drivers killed, 47% were unrestrained.
- 47% of young drivers of passenger vehicles in fatal crashes who had been drinking were unrestrained (vs 23% of non-drinking).

### Motorcycles (one paragraph, not the lead)

- 583 young motorcycle riders (15–20) killed in 2024 vs 485 in 2023 (**+20%**); 10,113 injured vs 7,044 (**+44%**). Injuries surged more than twice as fast as deaths: more young riders are crashing, not just dying more.
- 41% of young riders in fatal crashes were unlicensed or invalidly licensed (vs 35% of all riders).
- 17% of killed young riders were unhelmeted (vs 33% of all riders killed) — young riders helmet MORE, and still die more. Helmets are estimated 37% effective at preventing rider fatalities.

### Licenses (Table 4)

- **21.1%** of young drivers in fatal crashes (1,030 of 4,873) had invalid licenses. Of those, 13.4% had previous suspensions or revocations within 5 years.
- 15.5% of young drivers in fatal crashes had previous speeding convictions; 63.3% had no previous driving record at all.

### Distraction (DOT HS 813 792)

- 241 distracted teen drivers (15–19) involved in fatal crashes in 2024; 264 people killed in crashes involving distracted teen drivers; 186 teens killed in distraction-affected crashes (7% of all teen traffic deaths).

### States (Table 8)

- Deaths in crashes involving young drivers ranged from 5 (Rhode Island) to 644 (Texas). Young drivers killed: 3 (Alaska, Rhode Island, DC) to 253 (Texas).
- Young-driver crashes were 13.4% of all traffic deaths nationwide; state shares ranged from 9.6% (Maine, Rhode Island) to 19.6% (Hawaii).

## Original contributions (computed for this piece)

1. **The −370 decomposition**: every life saved in 2024 in young-driver crashes was someone other than the young driver (+6 / −221 / −91 / −64 sums exactly to −370).
2. **The driver-share rise**: 41.2% of deaths in young-driver crashes were the young driver in 2024 — up from 38.4% in 2023 (2,156/5,617) — the highest driver share in the 10-year table.
3. **The sex divergence**: +26.7% male deaths vs −22.8% female deaths since 2015; 2.8x involvement-rate gap (56.72 vs 20.27).
4. **The drinking-unbelted compound**: 65% vs 47% unrestrained among killed (drinking vs sober).
5. **Motorcycle injury-vs-death gap**: injuries +44% vs deaths +20% — the crash surge outruns the death surge.

## What this article must NOT claim

- That young drivers got more dangerous in 2024. Their fatal-crash involvement FELL 6% (5,160→4,873) and their deaths were essentially flat (+6). The story is that the improvement went everywhere except to them, not that they got worse.
- That the 583 motorcycle deaths contradict #862's 609 (DOT HS 813 824). Different fact sheets, different table definitions/ARF vintages. Use 813 814's 583 with its own 2023 comparator (485); do not mix the two sheets' age tables.
- That the sex gap proves young men are worse drivers per mile. Exposure by sex is unknown (no male/female VMT split); the rates are per licensed driver, not per mile. Say so.
- That GDL or any single intervention explains the passenger-death drop (−20%). The sheet doesn't attribute causes. GDL is context, not the explanation.
- That 2024 ARF figures are final. They are Annual Report File numbers and can be revised when the Final File lands.

## Strongest counterargument

The passenger-death drop may be a composition effect, not a safety success: fewer teens riding together (smaller cars, fewer passengers per young-driver crash, post-pandemic driving patterns) would cut passenger deaths without any driver getting safer, and the flat driver-death count would then be the honest headline — nothing improved for the person at risk of dying most. The sex divergence may likewise be exposure, not behavior: young men may simply be driving more miles, later at night, in older cars. Without sex- and age-stratified VMT, "young men got more dangerous" is unproven; "young men are more exposed" fits the data equally well. And the whole 2024 improvement (−370) sits on ARF data that NHTSA itself may revise.

## Limitations

- FARS captures fatal crashes only; 184,129 young-driver injuries are CRSS survey estimates, not counts.
- BAC values are imputed where test results are unknown (NHTSA's multiple-imputation model); the 29%/25% alcohol shares carry that uncertainty.
- 2024 figures are Annual Report File, subject to revision in the Final File. The 2023 comparator in Table 1 (2,156) differs from the 2023 fact sheet's 2,148 for the same reason (ARF→Final revision).
- No exposure data by sex, by passenger count, or by time of day — the mechanisms behind the decomposition (why passengers got safer, why males diverged) are not in this dataset.
- "Young drivers" = ages 15–20 operating motor vehicles; 15-year-olds are a small slice and mostly unlicensed.

## Actionable takeaways (required)

- Parents: the passenger seat is where 2024's improvement happened — GDL passenger restrictions and a hard no-riding-with-new-drivers rule for teens are the levers with evidence behind them. 66% of dead passengers were teens themselves.
- Young male drivers are the highest-risk group in the dataset by a factor of nearly three over young females; graduated licensing, night-driving limits, and passenger limits target exactly this group (IIHS).
- Seat belts: 53% of killed young drivers were unrestrained, and 65% of the drinking ones. A belt is the cheapest intervention in the entire fact sheet.
- Motorcycles: 41% of young riders in fatal crashes had no valid license. No license, no bike — that is an enforcement gap, not a training gap.
- Check the VIN: none of this is a recall story, but the restraint and licensing numbers are the actionable core.
