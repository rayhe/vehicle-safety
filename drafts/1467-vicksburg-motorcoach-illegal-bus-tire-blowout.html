Seven People Died in a Bus That Was Never Legal in America

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 11, 2026

At 12:33 a.m. on August 31, 2024, a motorcoach doing 71 mph on I-20 near Vicksburg, Mississippi, lost its left front tire. It did not go flat. It came apart. Three seconds later the bus was off the road, in a 275-foot median, on its side. Seven passengers died. Every one of them had been thrown from the bus.[1]

NTSB investigators spent 23 months on what happened next and published their final report, HIR-26-07, on August 11, 2026. According to the report, the tire failed because of impact damage inside its structure: broken steel cords, tears across the inner liner, the tread separating from the belts. None of it was visible from outside. Tread depth measured 8/32-inch, well above the legal minimum, and the steers had been replaced seven months earlier. What investigators could find, and what matters more, is that both front tires were running overdeflected, the signature of chronic underinflation or overload: chafing at the bead seat, a compression groove at the rim flange, wear around both shoulders.[1]

A machine on board had one job: keep the tires inflated. Vigia’s central tire inflation system was supposed to hold the steer tires at pressure and warn the driver when it could not. When investigators examined the bus after the crash, every axle button on the system sat in the deactivated position, and the audible alert was switched off too. Even switched on, the system was programmed to stop at 100 to 105 psi on tires rated to 123 psi. Across 41 maintenance events in the year before the crash, the carrier recorded not one tire pressure reading. Pressures on the night of the crash cannot be known, because the system stores no data and displays only an equalized axle pressure, never individual tires. Switched off, set too low, and unable to say what it was doing: that is not a safety system.[1]

If the blowout was the crash, the seat belts were the deaths. Forty-six passenger lap belts rode in that bus. One was functional and accessible. Forty-five others hung below the seat pans or were wedged between the cushions, unreachable. None of the six passengers investigators interviewed wore a belt. Two had asked the driver and co-driver about belts and were told the bus had none. NHTSA’s own estimate puts lap-only belts at 76 percent effective at preventing fatalities in rollover crashes, but that number assumes a belt you can reach and latch.[1][4]

That bus should not have been on an American highway at all. It was a 2018 Volvo 9800 built for the Mexican market and never formally imported: no USDOT certification label, absent from NHTSA’s import-eligibility databases. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 208 has required lap and shoulder belts in every passenger seat of an over-the-road bus built after November 28, 2016, with a gross vehicle weight rating over 26,000 pounds. This bus weighed 54,564 pounds and carried lap-only belts. Standard 217 requires emergency exit labels in English; the roof hatch instructions were in Spanish only. No valid Texas travel permit had covered the bus since October 2022, and the carrier obtained none for any other state on this trip. Nobody checked, because nobody checks: Customs inspects commercial vehicles for contraband and documents, and FMVSS compliance is examined only when a vehicle is formally imported. A bus can cross the border every week for years without anyone asking whether it meets American crash standards.[1][3]

By the NTSB’s own account, the driver did nearly everything right. He was belted, alert, and had both hands on the wheel once it yanked left. He braked lightly, believing harder braking could roll the bus. Four days of onboarding had covered hours of service, weather, and security; nothing in it covered sudden tire failure, and he had never experienced one. Training on steer-tire failure is not federally required and is not commonly provided anywhere in the industry. Investigators concluded that even a properly belted driver with both hands on the wheel would have found it extremely challenging to keep the bus on the road, given the sudden, unanticipated force. Technology that could have helped exists and is not on buses: Kodiak Robotics demonstrated in 2022 that an automated driving system can hold its lane through a catastrophic steer-tire blowout and stop in a controlled manner, and Volvo’s Dynamic Steering, which limits sudden steering displacement, has helped European truck drivers in blowout situations. Neither system is installed on a motorcoach.[1]

Transportes Regiomontanos of Laredo, Texas, had been on FMCSA’s radar. Its Crash Indicator score had sat in alert status since July 2024, about eight weeks before the crash, and the company had four crashes in four years, one fatal. A postcrash compliance review found seven violations, none critical, and rated the carrier satisfactory. Then the carrier could not renew its insurance, could not find a policy anywhere else, and ceased operations. Investigators also found several related carriers with common ownership and management, the pattern of a possible reincarnated carrier, and passed the information to FMCSA.[1][5]

One gap is closing, slowly. Roadside Level I inspections check only the driver’s seat belt, never the passengers’. After Vicksburg, the Commercial Vehicle Safety Alliance began drafting procedures to inspect passenger belts on motorcoaches built after November 28, 2016, and to cite inoperable or inaccessible belts under FMVSS 208. Its draft was slated for a vote at the alliance’s annual conference in September 2026. It took a crash that killed seven people to add a belt check to an inspection that already checks the driver’s belt.[1][6]

The counterargument, at full strength: broken steel cords inside a tire with 8/32-inch of tread were invisible to every inspection the industry performs: the carrier’s 41 maintenance events, a roadside Level I check, the driver’s pretrip walk-around. Carrier records show the tires were checked, the steers replaced seven months earlier, the bus maintained. Behind the wheel sat a man who did nearly everything right. NTSB investigators themselves say even a perfect response probably would not have kept the bus on the road. If the blowout was bad luck on a tire no one could have condemned, then negligence is the wrong frame for the crash itself. What the evidence does support is narrower and grimmer: a random failure met a bus with no margin, and the margin is where the deaths happened. A blowout may have been unavoidable. Ejections were not.[1]

What this does not prove: investigators could not determine when or how the tire was damaged, what the tire pressures were on the night of the crash, or whether the inflation system was switched off before the crash. Loaded weight was never recorded, so whether the steer tires were overloaded cannot be established, only that both showed overdeflection. Passenger seating positions could not be determined, so the belt findings rest on the postcrash inspection and six interviews. One crash is not a rate: seven deaths do not establish a trend in motorcoach tire failures. The closest comparator is Wawayanda, New York, in 2023, where a steer-tire failure killed two and injured 42.[1][2]

What This Means for You

If you ride a motorcoach, pull your seat belt out of the seat and latch it before the bus moves. Should it be buried, broken, or missing, tell the driver and consider another bus: in Vicksburg, 45 of 46 belts were unusable, and every person who died was thrown from the bus. Ask for a safety briefing; the Vicksburg passengers never got one, and two who asked were told there were no belts. If you charter a bus, ask whether its coaches are certified for US operation, with a USDOT label, because a Mexico-market coach can look identical to a US one and lack shoulder belts and English exit labels. And if your own vehicle has a tire-pressure system, check that it is switched on and set to the tire’s rated pressure, not a lower convenience setting: the Vicksburg system was off, and even on it would have held 100 to 105 psi against a 123 psi rating.[1]
