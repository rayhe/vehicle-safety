In a Saturn S, a Fatal Crash Is Your Fatality. In a Ram 2500, It's Somebody Else's.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 14, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. The federal government tracks two numbers for every car on the road: how many fatal crashes it gets involved in, and how many of its own occupants die. Divide the second by the first and you learn something no crash test can tell you. When a fatal crash involves a Saturn S-Series, someone inside the Saturn dies 92.4 percent of the time. When one involves a Ram 2500, someone inside the Ram dies 20.5 percent of the time. Everybody else is a rounding error or a bystander.

92.4% vs 20.5%
Share of fatal crashes where the fatality was one of the vehicle's own occupants: Saturn S-Series versus Ram 2500

Physics has always been the hungriest accountant in the car business. IIHS has spent decades documenting that heavier vehicles protect their occupants while raising the risk for everyone outside them.[3] What I did, because nobody else on this site had, was run the funeral arithmetic nameplate by nameplate across ten years of FARS data.[1] The death-sentence tier reads like the inventory of a cash-for-clunkers lot: Saturn S-Series at 92.4 percent, Chevrolet Aveo at 89.0, Corvette at 88.4, Buick Park Avenue at 88.2, Ford Escort at 87.7, Toyota Echo at 85.9, Dodge Neon at 85.6, Chevrolet Cavalier at 85.7 with 1,225 bodies to back it up. In those cars, a fatal crash is a suicide note with your name already filled in.

The survivor tier is the opposite ledger: Ram 2500 at 20.5 percent, Ford Transit at 30.8, and the Acura TLX, Audi Q5, Mercedes ML-Class, Ram 1500, and Toyota Land Cruiser all between 30 and 35 percent, which means your fatal crash is their problem. And here is the part that should make you put down the coffee: the Toyota Land Cruiser has the worst per-mile fatality rate in the entire dataset, 6.27 deaths per 100 million miles, the highest number on the board. Its own occupants survive two-thirds of its fatal crashes; it survives by killing others. Mass transfers the funeral across the lane line.

You might assume the difference is behavior, that drunks drive beaters and sober professionals drive German SUVs, but the toxicology data disagrees, rudely. Across FARS, about one in five tested drivers in fatal crashes is impaired, and that share barely moves across the extremes: Saturn S 19.2 percent, Cavalier 22.4, Neon 23.2, Ram 2500 19.7, F-150 18.9, Accord 20.0. The only real outlier is the Corvette at 26.2 percent, which is what happens when you sell a midlife crisis with a throttle. Impairment explains a rounding error of a 4.5x lethality gap; the rest is metal and birth year. The death-sentence tier is light, old, and cheap, discontinued between 2002 and 2005, carrying whatever safety technology the Clinton administration required, while the survivor tier is heavy and new.

None of this is the IIHS discovering gravity; they proved mass wins twenty years ago, and the new part is the seating chart. A Ram 2500's 20.5 percent is not a safety achievement; it is an aggressivity statistic. Surviving four out of five of your own fatal crashes means the other side of the ratio is full of other people's funerals. We talk about crashworthiness as if it were a private good, like fuel economy. It isn't. It is a transfer payment, collected in bone and steel from whoever brought the lighter car.

So what do you do with a number like this? If you drive a 2000s compact beater, understand the math: your car has no second line of defense to offer you, so crash avoidance is the only safety system you own, and it runs on speed, following distance, and tire condition. Shopping used: heavier and newer beats lighter and older, every time, and no structural advantage survives a defective airbag or a recalled brake booster, so check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls like a person who has read this column before.[5] And if you drive the heavy truck: your survival odds are partly paid for by other people's bodies, so drive like you owe them something.
