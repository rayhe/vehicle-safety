By The Numbers

109 People Died on Scooters in 2024. FARS Doesn't Count a Single One as a Pedestrian.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
August 31, 2027

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. Ask NHTSA how many pedestrians died on American roads in 2024 and the answer comes back clean: 7,080.[1] But the same fact sheet carries a second body count in Table 10, one almost nobody quotes: 271 people killed on "personal conveyances."[2] Rental scooters, skateboards, wheelchairs. Counted as pedestrians, no.

Scooter deaths are the line that moved: 65 in 2022, 81 in 2023, 109 in 2024.[2] That is a 68% increase in two years, while overall traffic deaths fell 4.3% and pedestrian deaths fell about 3% in the early estimates.[3]

68%
Increase in scooter deaths, 2022 to 2024 (65 to 109), while overall traffic deaths fell 4.3%

Wheelchair and mobility-aid users are the category's biggest line item, at 123 deaths in 2024, and almost nobody talks about them.[2] These are the humans least equipped to dodge a two-ton mistake, dying at a rate that appears in no press release because their table has no headline. Skateboards added 22, skates 4, self-balancing boards 3: the long tail of Table 10, every row a way of moving that the headline statistics treat as a rounding error.[2]

Now the part where I argue with myself, because the data insists on it. 271 deaths is 0.7% of the 39,254 total, and the headline trends are genuinely good: the second quarter of 2024 marked the ninth straight quarter of declining fatalities.[3] Every scooter trip replaced something: a 48-city travel analysis estimated each shared-scooter trip displaces about 0.15 car miles.[4] More trips can produce more deaths even as each trip gets safer: the absolute count and the per-trip risk tell two different stories, and the denominator does half the arguing.

Zoom in to the devices and the comfort fades. From 2017 through 2024, the CPSC counted 206 e-scooter deaths and roughly 80,000 e-scooter injuries, with emergency-department visits quadrupling.[5] Consumer Reports, testing scooters since 2022, watched top speeds climb from 15.5 to 28 mph and now advises against riding them at all: standing riders fall headfirst, and no baseline safety standards exist.[6] In New York, 52% of stand-up scooter deaths between 2017 and 2025 involved machines capable of exceeding 20 mph; the city now enforces against sellers of prohibited high-speed machines.[4] In Germany, e-scooter deaths rose by 13 in the first half of 2026, the only road-user group moving the wrong way while total deaths fell 9%.[7]

Methodology notes, because you should know what I cannot tell you. FARS does not separate rental scooters from owned ones, or electric from kick, so the 109 is the whole bucket and "109 rental-scooter deaths" would be a lie with good typography. CPSC death counts are anecdotal, assembled from media reports and death certificates, with recent years probably incomplete. The 2024 FARS file is the annual-report version, subject to revision before it goes final. The direction is solid; the decimals are negotiable.

Riders: wear a helmet, stay off the sidewalk (Milwaukee bans it outright), and don't ride impaired.[8] An NYU Langone study found pedestrians struck by these devices suffer brain injuries at nearly double the rider rate: the machine endangers bystanders too.[8] The American Academy of Pediatrics says under-16s should not operate e-scooters; two-thirds of injured e-scooter kids in the LA children's hospital data were riding illegally.[9] Cities hold the best lever: half of stand-up scooter deaths involved machines capable of 20-plus mph, so enforcing existing speed-cap sale rules is the whole game. The federal baseline does not exist.[4]

Add the 271 back to the 7,080 and the real vulnerable-road-user toll for 2024 is 7,351, a number that appears in no headline I could find. They are counted, just filed where nobody looks. Table 10: I looked.
