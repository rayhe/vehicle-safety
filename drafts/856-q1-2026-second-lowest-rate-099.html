0.99: One Hundredth From History

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, because 0.99 looked like a typo. NHTSA's early estimates for the first quarter of 2026 put the traffic fatality rate at 0.99 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, with an estimated 7,770 people killed, down 4.3 percent from the same quarter last year.[1] That 0.99 is the second-lowest first-quarter rate in American history, one hundredth above the 0.98 record set fifteen years ago.

0.99
Q1 2026 fatality rate per 100M VMT, one hundredth above the all-time record, set while Americans drove 11 billion more miles

Here is the part nobody in the press release said out loud. In the first quarter of 2011, when the 0.98 record was set, America was still crawling out of the Great Recession. Unemployment sat near 9 percent, gasoline flirted with four dollars a gallon, and vehicle miles traveled were depressed. A record rate set on empty roads is a record with an asterisk. Q1 2026 earned its 0.99 the hard way: Americans drove 11 billion more miles than in Q1 2025, 30 states plus Puerto Rico posted declines, and the rate fell anyway.[1] As far as I can tell from the published series, this is the first near-record rate in the dataset achieved with more driving, not less, and that is the number that made me sit up.

Then there is the streak: Q1 2026's 4.3 percent decline makes 16 consecutive quarters of year-over-year fatality declines, running since Q2 2022,[2][3] sixteen quarters, and in FARS data going back to 1975, no sustained improvement run has lasted this long. Do the counterfactual, because rates are abstract and bodies are not: 7,770 deaths divided by 0.99 per 100M VMT implies roughly 784.8 billion miles driven in the quarter. At the Q1 2021 rate of 1.28, those same miles would have produced about 10,045 deaths.[3] The difference is roughly 2,275 people who would have died at the pandemic-peak rate and did not. That is what a rate decline compounds into when you multiply it by 785 billion miles.

The release's other headline deserves its own paragraph: speeding deaths are finally unwinding. NHTSA projects 10,035 speeding-related deaths in 2025, down 11 percent from 11,288 in 2024, a return to pre-pandemic 2019 levels,[1] which is not the same thing as solved, since speeding still accounted for 29 percent of 2024's fatalities. And the behavioral multipliers in the 2024 data are the kind of numbers that should end arguments at dinner parties: speeding drivers in fatal crashes were unbuckled at 50 percent versus 21 percent for non-speeders, and at BAC 0.08 or higher at 37 percent versus 17 percent.[1] Speeding is not one bad decision; it is the decision that arrives with all the other bad decisions already in the car. Young men remain the epicenter: 39 percent of male drivers ages 15 to 20 involved in fatal crashes were speeding, the highest of any group.[1]

What to do with all this. If you make policy, NHTSA's state table names the states that did not decline in Q1 2026. Find yours on that list and ask why your neighbors are improving and you are not. If you are buying a car, the 2025 subcategory data showed fatalities in vehicles under ten years old fell 12 percent; fleet turnover is doing measurable, boring, life-saving work.[4] If you are driving: slow down and buckle up, because the multipliers above say those two choices travel together, and understand that 0.99 is an average spread over 785 billion miles, not a force field around your car. The rate is the denominator's story; the numerator is still 7,770 people in ninety days, eighty-six a day.

Now the honest objections, stated at full strength, and the first is the simplest: 7,770 dead in three months is not a victory, it is 86 funerals a day, and a rate headline can launder that into good news. The numerator barely moved, down 4.3 percent; the denominator did the heavy lifting. Second: these are early estimates, statistical projections that NHTSA itself says will be revised when the FARS Annual Report File lands, and revisions have gone in both directions before. Third: the 2011 comparison is directional, not precise: I did not re-verify Q1 2011 VMT from FHWA's tables, and the "empty roads" framing simplifies a recession-era mix of less driving, fleet-mix shifts, and enforcement changes. What the data does support without qualification: the streak is the longest on record, the rate is one hundredth off history, and it happened while America drove more. Everything else is me reading the tea leaves, and I have labeled it as such.
