Four Headlight Recalls in 14 Months. Zero Burned-Out Bulbs.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 1, 2026

Yesterday Ford recalled 41,748 Expeditions and Super Duty trucks because a single contaminated LED chip can kill the daytime running lights, parking lights, high beams, and low beams.[1] That is not a headlight problem. That is a computer problem that happens to emit photons.

0
Burned-out bulbs, cracked lenses, or bad filaments across four 2025–2026 headlight recalls. Every failure mode was electronic.

The table starts in July, when Stellantis recalled 628 premium headlamp assemblies on 2026 Ram 1500s after supplier Marelli shipped units with internal harness connector terminals that were never fully seated, so the parking lamps and DRLs just switched off. NHTSA's filing estimates 77 percent of the 628 were defective, a defect rate you normally associate with a bad batch of Halloween candy, not a safety system.[2] Last year, Ford's own teardown of dead Nautilus, Mach-E, and Mustang headlamps found a burnt Schottky diode, the power-regulating component inside the LED driver module, traced to quality escapes across three diode lots from a Tier-3 sub-supplier two steps removed from Ford. Five of those vehicles lost the headlamp and the rear lamp at the same time. One diode, both ends of the car.[3] And Daimler Trucks recalled headlamps whose thermal protection logic contained a software error, so the low beams went dim for minutes after every high-to-low switch on a hot day.[4]

Notice what is missing: no filament fatigue, no moisture in the housing, no vibration-cracked lens. Every failure lives in the electronics: a contaminated chip, a burnt diode, an unseated terminal, a firmware bug. The sealed LED assembly consolidated four separate lighting functions into one unit driven by one driver module, and in doing so it consolidated four separate failure modes into single points of failure. Your grandfather's sealed-beam headlight had exactly one job and one filament. It could not take out the parking lights because it did not know the parking lights existed.

Now the counterargument, stated fairly, because it is strong. LED headlights are genuinely better at the actual job of lighting the road. IIHS data ties good-rated headlights to fewer nighttime crashes, and only solid-state lighting makes adaptive driving beams possible. These four recalls are manufacturing quality escapes, not an indictment of the technology. A $4 halogen bulb burning out is also a single point of failure for that lamp, and it did so constantly. Nobody is nostalgic for the era of carrying spare 9005s in the glovebox.

Fair. But the failure modes moved up the stack, and the repair moved with them. A dead halogen bulb was a ten-minute, ten-dollar fix in a parking lot. A contaminated LED chip means replacing the entire sealed assembly, which on a 2026 Super Duty is a four-figure part plus labor, ordered through a dealer, after your dashboard politely informs you that your headlights have stopped working. Ford's own filing describes the warning as a dashboard indicator that illuminates for inoperative low or high beams, which means the car tells you while you are driving it, at night.

What to do: If you own a 2025–2027 Expedition or a 2026 Super Duty (F-250 through F-550), check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now instead of waiting for the October 26 letter. When shopping any new vehicle, ask whether the headlamps are sealed LED units. If they are, price a replacement assembly before you buy. The bulb used to be the consumable; now the computer is.

Limitations

This analysis covers four recalls I could verify in NHTSA filings, not a census of every 2025–2026 lighting action, and two of the four are Ford's. Read it as a case series, not an industry census. I did not pull population counts for the Ford 25V519 or DTNA 26V475 filings, so the article cites their failure modes, not their scale. Ford has not published the contamination vector for the 26V606 LED chips, so I make no claim about where in the supply chain it happened. Most importantly, recall counts measure reporting, not field failure rates: I can show that recent headlight failure modes are electronic, not that LED headlights fail more often than halogens did. The claim here is about where failures live, not how often they happen.
