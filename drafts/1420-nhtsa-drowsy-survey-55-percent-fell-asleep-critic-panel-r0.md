## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0/10
Fair to every party it criticizes. NHTSA gets credit for finally fielding the survey and for publishing its own limitations; the messaging-paradox finding is reported with the agency's own explanation, not spun into an accusation. No victim-blaming: drowsy drivers are treated as people failed by a system with no biomarker and no law, not as villains. The 633 official deaths are called a floor, not a lie. No sensationalizing of a risk the data states as modeled, not measured.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0/10
Headline is the share: "55% of American Drivers Have Fallen Asleep at the Wheel." Pull stat 132 million is a number people repeat at dinner. "381 feet, blind, at highway speed" is the line that travels, and "a third did it in the first hour" inverts the reader's prior belief, which is the exact mechanism that makes people forward an article. Slightly weaker than the best pieces only because the survey is four years old in fielding, which the article discloses rather than hiding.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
Every number, date, and claim verified against the Traffic Tech text as read in full: DOT HS 813 895, 3,765 respondents, fielded 2022, 55%, 0.23 mean, 33% within the first hour, 62% multilane above 55 mph, midnight-5:59 AM 27%, 6-7 hours modal sleep at 24%, over 80% unaware of state law, Arkansas and New Jersey as the only two states with drowsy-driving laws. The "only Arkansas and New Jersey" claim is the Traffic Tech's own phrasing, not an inference. No invented letter dates, no invented DOT numbers. The 2002 comparison is explicitly flagged as not strictly comparable, which is the correct hedge.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Genuine original contribution: the 132 million headcount, the ~660 million annual episodes, the 37-to-55 trend, the 33-vs-15 first-hour comparison, the 381-feet microsleep arithmetic, the six-hours-is-the-modal-night reading. Limitations are a dedicated paragraph, not inline hedging: low response rate, recall and social-desirability bias, 2022 fielding vs 2026 publication, survey-mode differences in the 2002 comparison. Counterargument stated at full strength, including that the 55% could be inflated by loose self-definition or deflated by shame. Methodology for the two big calculations is shown in the piece and the disclaimer. One nit: the sample-size discrepancy between the Traffic Tech (3,765) and the ROSA blurb for the full report (10,700) is documented in the research file but not in the article; the article correctly uses 3,765 and says nothing false, but a one-line note would have been more transparent.
