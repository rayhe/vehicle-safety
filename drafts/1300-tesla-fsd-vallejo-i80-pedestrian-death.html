A Tesla Braked, Swerved, and Still Killed a Man Crossing I-80. The Second Driver Didn't Even Stop.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 27, 2026

At 2:34 on Thursday morning, a 71-year-old man from Vallejo walked onto westbound Interstate 80 from the right shoulder, heading for the center divider.[1] A Tesla traveling about 70 mph in the second lane braked and steered left. Its right front corner caught him anyway. He died where he fell, and then at least one more vehicle hit him and kept driving.[2]

70
Miles per hour, per CHP's preliminary review, when the Tesla's dashcam recorded a pedestrian stepping off the shoulder into the No. 2 lane at the I-780 interchange

Let us talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds, because that is the whole story here. At 70 mph a car covers 103 feet every second. A pedestrian stepping off a dark shoulder into a live lane gives every system on the road, human or machine, a fraction of a second to notice, decide, and act. CHP's preliminary review of the Tesla's dashcam says the car did notice, did brake, and did steer left.[2] It still hit him with its right front. That is not a software anecdote. That is physics collecting on a bad situation faster than any reaction loop can pay.

One line in CHP's account deserves more attention than it got. "The driver stated the vehicle was being operated in Full Self-Driving (FSD) autonomous mode."[3] Stated, by the driver, to officers. CHP has not confirmed that FSD was engaged, and neither has Tesla. No model, model year, or software build has been reported, and only the car's logs can settle what mode was active, what the system detected, and when.[5] Until those logs surface, anyone telling you this crash proves FSD failed is guessing, and anyone telling you it proves FSD worked is guessing too. What is confirmed is narrower and worse: a man crossed a freeway on foot at 2:30 in the morning, a car tried to miss him and could not, and somebody else ran over the body and left.

Nine days earlier, on the same highway, a 22-year-old driver told CHP he was asleep with FSD engaged when his Tesla drove through a coned freeway closure in Fairfield and struck a parked patrol car, seriously injuring two officers.[4] In September, another driver was cited for sleeping at 60 mph with FSD engaged in Solano County.[4] Two I-80 crashes in nine days, both with drivers claiming the car was driving, one asleep and one watching a pedestrian die in front of him. CHP has now said twice in two weeks that Full Self-Driving is not fully autonomous and the driver stays responsible.[4] Tesla's own product page agrees: FSD (Supervised) requires a fully attentive driver.[4] Supervised means supervised, and a name that says otherwise is a marketing decision, not an engineering one.

What you can actually do: if you use FSD (Supervised), keep your eyes on the road at night on freeways above all, because that is where the edge cases live, pedestrians where they should never be, at speeds where no system gets a second chance. If you drive past a stopped car or an object in the lanes at night, slow down before you reach it, because the Vallejo driver who kept going fled a fatal crash scene while the Tesla driver who stopped is a witness, and which one you are gets decided in about two seconds. If you saw anything on I-80 that night, call CHP's Solano Area office at 707-639-5600.[1] If your car breaks down on a freeway shoulder at night, stay in the car with hazards on or get behind the guardrail and call for help. Walking across live lanes at 2:30 a.m. is close to unsurvivable at any speed, driven by anyone or anything.

What this article did not prove: FSD engagement is unconfirmed, detection timing is unknown, and causation between the two impacts is undetermined. CHP has not said why the man was crossing the freeway, and no reason should be assumed. No speed beyond about 70 mph is claimed, and no vehicle model or software version is claimed, because none has been reported.

Fair counter, stated at full strength: a pedestrian crossing a freeway at 2:30 a.m. is nearly unsurvivable no matter who or what is driving, and blaming software for a person in the worst possible place at the worst possible hour stretches the evidence past what it can hold. Fair, and it misses the second half of the story. Nobody is on trial here for the laws of physics. But a driver who hits a body in the road and keeps driving has made a choice no physics explains, and a product named Full Self-Driving that still needs you watching every second has made a promise its own manual will not keep. Both of those are worth saying plainly, even while the logs are still missing.
