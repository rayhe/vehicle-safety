Rain Can Kill Your Range Rover's Backup Camera. The Dealer's Fix Is a Drill.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
December 15, 2027

Land Rover sells you invincibility: wade depth of 35 inches, terrain response, the official SUV of people who own both a ski chalet and a beach house. And yet 93,997 of its 2022-2026 Range Rovers cannot reliably survive a rainstorm. NHTSA campaign 26V678000 states it plainly: the rearview camera housing has insufficient drain holes, water pools around the camera, the camera dies, and your backup screen goes dark.[1]

93,997
2022–2026 Range Rovers recalled because rainwater can pool around the rearview camera until it stops working

This is the part where I get judgmental, because this is my beat: water management is first-year engineering, and every electronic module mounted outside the cabin gets a drainage path, which means you drill holes, you slope surfaces, and you assume the weather will win and design for the loss. Someone at Land Rover either skipped the math or trusted a seal that did not deserve it. The failure chain is almost elegant in its stupidity: rain falls, water collects where it cannot drain, electronics sit in a puddle, corrosion does the rest, and a $120,000 vehicle gets defeated by the same physics that defeated your basement.

Now the remedy, which I love, because it is honest in a way recalls rarely are. Dealers will drill additional drain holes in the underside of the tailgate trim. Then inspect the camera. Then replace it if the water already did its work, all free of charge. The official federal safety fix for a six-figure luxury SUV is a drill bit.[1] Also tucked in the filing: interim owner letters go out December 11, 2026, with more letters "once the final remedy is available," and you should read that twice, because it means the final remedy is not yet available and they are drilling the holes now while finishing the fix later, exactly the phased rollout you run when you discover a drainage problem too late to engineer the whole answer at once.

Zoom out, because the pattern is the story: this is the second rearview-camera recall for Jaguar Land Rover in two years, following campaign 24V678000 in December 2024, which covered 2024 Defenders, Discovery Sports, Range Rovers, Evoques, Velars, and Range Rover Sports whose overheating sensing module blacked out the surround and rearview camera images.[2] Different mechanism, identical outcome: shift into reverse, get a dark screen. Note the campaign numbers: 24V678000 and 26V678000, two years apart, same digits, same dead screen.

That screen matters more than it used to, because backup cameras have been mandatory on new vehicles since May 2018, after NHTSA estimated backover crashes killed roughly 210 people and injured 15,000 a year, with children under five absorbing 31 percent of the deaths.[3] The mandate worked: a 2025 pediatric surgery study found severe backover injuries in children under five fell 62 percent after the rule took effect, and backover child deaths fell 78 percent.[4] So a camera that quits in wet weather is not the camera the mandate was built on. And a dead screen may be worse than no screen at all, because a driver who has backed up with a camera for eight years has built their entire backing routine around one, which means expectation is a safety feature until it is not.

What you do about it is boring, which is good, because boring is what fixes this. If you own a 2022-2026 Range Rover, do not wait for the December letter: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now and get the holes drilled. Today, before you back out of the driveway: shift into reverse and confirm the image appears. If the screen is black or the image freezes, the camera is already drowned and you need the replacement, not just the holes. Until the dealer touches it, back up like it is 2015, mirrors, windows, shoulder check, full stop before the sidewalk, because the camera has been federally required for nearly a decade but the law cannot fix yours; only a dealership with a drill can.

What this article did not prove: the filing reports no injuries or crashes tied to this defect, so the water-kills-camera chain is an engineering failure with consequences so far limited to dead screens. Nobody outside Land Rover knows how many of the 93,997 cameras have actually drowned. The interim/final remedy split means the December fix may be partial; watch for the second letter before declaring victory. And rain is not the only suspect, parked cars in car washes and pressure-washer enthusiasts also bathe that trim. The headline says rain because the defect report says water, and the report is the source of truth.
