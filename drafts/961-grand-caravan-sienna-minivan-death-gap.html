The Gap

The Deadliest Minivan in America Has Soberer Drivers Than the Safest One, So Blame the Van

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 21, 2026

I ran the numbers, then ran them again because the first pass had to be a sorting error. It was not. The Dodge Grand Caravan kills at 2.7 times the rate of the Toyota Sienna: 1.33 deaths per 100 million miles against 0.49. Same vehicle class, same family-hauler job description, and a death gap you could drive a Grand Caravan through.

Your first instinct is the driver's fault, and mine was too. It is the correct instinct for nearly every lopsided crash statistic on this site, which is exactly why I bothered to check it. FARS toxicology for fatal-crash drivers: Grand Caravan drivers test impaired at 15.3 percent, Sienna drivers at 19.0 percent, so read that twice. Yet the van with the soberer drivers kills at nearly triple the rate. Blame-the-driver is off the table, and it is the table I eat at.

So it is the machine. Ten years of FARS says the Grand Caravan is not an unlucky van, it is a structurally inferior one, and the Insurance Institute has been saying so since before the Obama administration left office. In IIHS small-overlap front crash tests, the Grand Caravan, its Chrysler Town & Country twin, and the Nissan Quest all scored Poor, the worst possible grade, while the Sienna earned Acceptable and the Odyssey earned Good.[3] A crash type that routes forces around a vehicle's main energy-absorbing structure is precisely where the Caravan's 2008 platform, sold essentially unchanged through the 2020 model year,[5] shows its age: twelve years, one platform, two million family road trips.

But here is the part that made me put the spreadsheet down and stare out the window: the newer lab tests say the Sienna is not innocent either. When IIHS updated its moderate-overlap test in 2023 to put a dummy the size of a small woman in the second row, exactly where minivan kids sit, every minivan flunked it: Pacifica, Carnival, Sienna Marginal, Odyssey Poor.[2] In April 2026, for the first time I can find, no minivan at all made the IIHS Top Safety Pick roster.[4] A vehicle class built for families that cannot pass a family safety test is a strange thing to witness. The Sienna is merely the least embarrassing contestant, which is a strange thing to write about a vehicle with a 0.49 fatality rate. It is also the only minivan with second-row seatbelt pretensioners and force limiters, which might explain some of that 2.7x all by itself.

Now the counterargument, stated at full strength because it deserves it. The Grand Caravan was the cheapest minivan in America, stickered around $26,000 against $35,000-plus for a Sienna, and price selects buyers. Lower-income households, older vehicles kept past their prime, deferred maintenance, and heavy fleet and taxi use: the Caravan was the default airport-shuttle van of the 2010s. Some fraction of that 2.71 ratio is socioeconomic, not metallurgical, and I will not pretend otherwise. But the IIHS small-overlap rig does not know what the driver earns, and it still handed the Caravan a Poor and the Sienna an Acceptable. Both explanations are true, and neither excuses the other.

Methodology, briefly, since you should not trust a ratio without one. Rates are FARS 2014-2023 deaths divided by estimated fleet vehicle-miles-traveled, built from registration counts and NHTS annual mileage surveys, not odometer readings, so treat small-fleet models with suspicion. Both nameplates here are high-volume, which shrinks that uncertainty. FARS captures fatal crashes only, and impairment percentages are floors, not ceilings: testing is inconsistent across states, and some drivers are never tested. Partly, the Pacifica's tiny 0.19 rate is a young-fleet artifact. The Grand Caravan's 1.33 is not an artifact of anything except itself.

What to do with this, if you are shopping for a used family hauler. The 2008-2019 Grand Caravan and Town & Country are the statistical outliers of the class; the 2015-and-newer Sienna and Odyssey rate far better in the real world. Ignore the NHTSA star count for this decision, since both vans carry five stars and the lab disagrees with the stars, so read the small-overlap rating instead. And sit in the second row of whatever you buy and look at the seatbelts, because that is where your kids ride, and in every minivan except the Sienna those belts are missing the pretensioners and limiters that the front seats got a decade ago.[6] The safest family vehicle is still whichever one was engineered like the family sits inside it.
