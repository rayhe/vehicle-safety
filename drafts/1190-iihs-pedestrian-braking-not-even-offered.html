Three Vehicles Got a Poor Pedestrian-Braking Grade. They Never Even Took the Test.

By Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

IIHS published its October pedestrian-braking list this month, eleven vehicles rated poor or marginal and none of them eligible for a Top Safety Pick until the systems improve. That is standard institutional shaming, fine on its own terms, except three of the eleven never actually flunked the test. Pedestrian braking isn't offered on the Jeep Wrangler, Jeep Gladiator, or Dodge Durango at any trim, so IIHS rates them Poor by default.[2] There is no option package to fix it and no software update coming, because the hardware simply isn't in the building.

3 of 11
Vehicles where pedestrian braking is not available at any price: Poor by default, not by performance

Jeep will sell you a Wrangler with heated leather seats, blind-spot monitoring on some trims, and a fold-down windshield. What it won't sell you is a sensor that brakes for a person in front of the truck. IIHS's own rating page states it flatly: "Pedestrian front crash prevention is not available on this model."[2] Same story for the Gladiator, which IIHS flagged for failing "headlight and pedestrian crash avoidance standards" in its December 2025 award round.[3] The Durango, a three-row family SUV bought specifically for school runs, sits on decade-old architecture where new safety tech goes to be postponed.

I ran the cross-tabulation myself: against FARS fatality data, the measurable ones span a 58-fold spread: the Nissan Altima and Chevrolet Trailblazer carry rates around 2.8 deaths per 100 million VMT, more than five times the fleet median of 0.54, while the Ford Bronco sits at 0.05.[4] Both the Altima and the Trailblazer only manage "marginal" pedestrian ratings. The Altima is also a fleet and rental favorite, which multiplies its public footprint.[1]

Here is the honest limit of that math, because the fine print matters. FARS counts deaths in crashes involving the vehicle, which overwhelmingly means the people inside it. It is not a ranking of which vehicles kill the most pedestrians. A Wrangler that doesn't exist as a FARS row for the Gladiator at all says nothing about a person in a crosswalk. The IIHS test, meanwhile, is a lab evaluation of one system against dummies in daylight and darkness.[5]

The honest defense of these vehicles is real, and their occupants are protected. The Wrangler finally stopped tipping over in small-overlap tests after October 2025 frame reinforcements, and the Ridgeline, Bronco, and Trax have genuinely good crash structures. Not having the system just means the driver does the braking, and your right foot remains the original automatic emergency braking.

But a missing system cannot be improved with a recall, retuned with an update, or compensated for by anything except attention. And attention is precisely what night testing measures, since night is when most pedestrian deaths happen. Drivers killed 7,148 pedestrians in 2024, the second straight annual decline and still roughly 20 percent above 2016 levels.[6] For the marginal scorers, the advice is concrete: treat the system as a backup, slow down early near crossings, especially after dark. For the three that never took the test: if you want pedestrian braking, shop somewhere else, because it is not on the options sheet.
