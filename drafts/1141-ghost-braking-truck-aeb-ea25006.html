Congress Mandated Truck Brakes. The Brakes Have Other Ideas.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 12, 2026

In 2021 Congress told NHTSA to require automatic emergency braking on heavy trucks. Five years later the rule is sitting in a regulatory waiting room, and the agency's own defect investigators have an open Engineering Analysis, EA25006, on roughly 250,000 Freightliner and Western Star trucks whose brakes fire for absolutely nothing.[1]

24
Crashes in the federal file blamed on braking for ghosts

The file covers 2017–2022 Freightliner Cascadia and Western Star 5700 tractors running Detroit Assurance or Wabco OnGuard radar. Since May 2023 drivers have been telling NHTSA the same story: no obstacle in the road, no warning, and then the truck decides it is about to die. Sometimes the event is a momentary blip; sometimes it is a full stop in the travel lane, which is a remarkable thing to have happen in an 80,000-pound vehicle. The resume lists 347 reports, 24 crashes, 13 injuries, and one fatality incident. On October 23, 2025 the probe was promoted from preliminary evaluation to engineering analysis, one notch below a formal recall demand. It remains open today, 355 days later.[1]

My favorite exhibit in the file is the trench plate. NHTSA's own test center drove an AEB-equipped Cascadia at a steel plate lying in the road, and the truck initiated hard braking for it. Daimler Trucks North America responded that a steel plate was not representative of real-world driving, which is fair. Still: a radar that reads flat steel in the pavement as an imminent collision is doing exactly what it was built to do, except aimed at nothing.[2]

Meanwhile the mandate those brakes were supposed to satisfy is in limbo. The June 2023 proposal promised 19,118 crashes prevented and 155 lives saved per year, drawn from the 60,000 annual rear-end crashes in which a heavy vehicle is the striking vehicle. A revised supplemental proposal was expected as early as this past July; the docket is empty as of today. The arithmetic against delay is brutal: every year of limbo is another year of that body count. The case for patience is sitting in Michigan, in the form of a driver named Carrie Moore, whose AEB phantom-braked on a snowy highway in 2023 and jackknifed her rig toward a median — a story Rep. Troy Nehls read into the congressional record.[3][4]

And now the symmetry, fresh from last week's IIHS track. The institute's first-ever commercial-vehicle crash-prevention ratings show Class 3 work pickups that cannot avoid a car target: the Chevrolet Silverado 3500HD failed to avoid the passenger-car dummy in all six vehicle-to-vehicle tests. So the state of the art is a fleet that emergency-brakes for steel plates in the road and fails to brake for actual cars. Trucks stop when they should roll. They roll when they should stop.[5]

If you run a fleet, ask which AEB generation your trucks carry. NHTSA's claim data shows Detroit Assurance 2.0, 4.0, and 5.0 systems generated claims at similar, elevated rates, while Wabco OnGuard ran substantially lower in comparable duty. If you are buying, know the mandate has no compliance dates yet; the 2023 dates were proposed, never final, and the rule will not require retrofitting existing trucks. And if your rig has ever stopped for a ghost, file the report: NHTSA opened this probe on the strength of 18 driver questionnaires, and the file is still open.[1][4]

What this does not prove: that the mandate's 155-lives projection is wrong, or that phantom braking is common. Complaint counts both understate frequency (most events are never reported) and overstate attribution (a hard jolt is not always AEB). Engineering Analysis is not a defect finding, and it can end with no action. But the file exists, it is a year old, and it is the reason your truck may be allowed to keep its phantom brakes for a while longer.
