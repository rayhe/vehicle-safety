All Seven Koenigsegg CC850s in America Were Recalled. One of Them Rolled Away First.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 27, 2026

Seven cars. That is not a rounding error or a pilot batch. Seven is every Koenigsegg CC850 delivered to the United States, and as of last week every one of them is under recall, because one of them rolled away after its driver did exactly what a button labeled as a brake promises.

NHTSA recall 26V694000 covers 2025 and 2026 CC850s built between September 28, 2025 and August 2, 2026, for unintended vehicle movement from a disengaged electronic parking brake.[1] With the engine running and Reverse selected, pressing the electronic parking brake button will not keep the brake engaged unless the driver holds the button down continuously.[2] Koenigsegg figures all seven American cars carry the bug, which makes this a recall with a 100 percent defect rate and a population smaller than a dinner party.

7
CC850s in the entire American fleet under recall 26V694000, with Koenigsegg estimating 100% carry the parking-brake software bug, after a single rollaway incident

October 12, 2026 supplied the incident. Koenigsegg learned that a CC850 had rolled away after its driver tried to set the parking brake with the engine still running, Reverse still engaged, on a slope, and then stepped out.[2] Nobody got hurt, which is either luck or a slope with nowhere important to go. Koenigsegg opened an investigation that same day, ran real-world testing and a failure-rate assessment through October 19, and then decided on a voluntary safety recall.

What makes this recall worth more than a laugh is the gap it exposes. NHTSA notes that this glitch is not covered by the usual static parking brake tests.[2] Static tests examine a parked car holding still, not a running car in Reverse with a driver stepping out, so a failure mode that needs a running engine and a selected gear slips straight past the test designed to catch it. A parking brake used to be a cable and a lever. Now it is software with modes, and software has corners that nobody thought to stand in until a $3 million car rolled down one.

What you can actually do: if you own one of the seven, accept the free over-the-air software update before parking on any slope, and until it is installed, never trust a tapped parking-brake button with the engine running in Reverse. Use Park, and hold the button if you must use it at all. For everyone else, the lesson travels: an electronic parking brake that releases on its own is a reportable defect, not a quirk, and owner complaints to NHTSA are what turn single incidents into recalls.

What this article did not prove: no injury was reported in the October 12 rollaway, and the recall is voluntary, not a federal defect finding. Sources say the over-the-air update was supposed to go live the week before October 27, but they do not confirm that all seven cars have received it. Seven is the American fleet only; no worldwide production total is claimed here.
