Australia Priced Your Car's Height in Dead Pedestrians: 11% per 10 Centimeters

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 6, 2026

Researchers in Melbourne just did something no safety rating ever bothered to do. They priced your car's height in dead pedestrians, and the exchange rate is grim: roughly 11 percent more fatality odds for every 10 centimeters of vehicle height. Not weight. Not speed. Height. Even the air between your bumper and the ground has a body count.[1]

37%
How much more likely a pedestrian is to die if the striking vehicle is 30cm taller than a sedan, height alone, all else held equal.

The University of Melbourne team went through more than 10,000 pedestrian crashes in Victoria from 2012 to 2025 and regressed fatality odds against the vehicle that did the hitting. The ledger reads like a tariff schedule: ten centimeters taller means 11 percent deadlier, ten centimeters longer adds 4 percent, and a hundred kilos heavier tacks on 6 percent. The effect isn't evenly distributed, either: in the women-only model, that same 10cm costs 14 percent. For pedestrians over 50, it costs 11 percent, same as the headline number, which should tell you the headline number was never the worst of it.

Here is where it stops being one country's problem. A US study published in the journal Economics of Transportation measured front-end height specifically and got 22 percent per 10cm, roughly double the Melbourne number.[2] Different measure, different continent, same verdict. The American estimate says light trucks caused about 1,000 extra pedestrian deaths in 2021 alone, and that capping front-end height at 1.25 meters would save 509 lives a year. A separate review of 682,509 collisions found SUVs 44 percent more likely to kill an adult pedestrian than a car, and 130 percent more likely to kill a small child.[3] Three studies, three datasets, one direction.

So let's do the arithmetic nobody put on the window sticker. Take a ute or pickup that sits 30 centimeters taller than a medium sedan, which is a completely ordinary height gap. Raise the Melbourne elasticity to the third power: 1.11 cubed is 1.37. Call it the height tax: about 37 percent more deadliness, assessed at the crosswalk, payable by whoever isn't inside the vehicle. Stretch the gap to 45 centimeters, full-size-pickup territory, and you're at 1.11 to the 4.5, roughly 61 percent. The Melbourne researchers ran the policy version themselves: shrink the fleet back to sedan size and pedestrian deaths fall 8.4 to 9.9 percent.[1] Applied to roughly 7,000 annual US pedestrian deaths, that's on the order of 600 lives a year. Rough math, sure; the direction is not.

Now the paradox, because there's always one: cars have never been safer for the people inside them. Five-star ratings, curtain airbags, crash cages engineered to the millimeter. And yet US pedestrian deaths climbed roughly 75 percent since 2009, and Australia's own September 2026 review found road deaths involving light commercial vehicles up 12.1 percent while passenger-car deaths barely moved, up 0.3 percent.[1] We built a fleet that protects drivers from each other and forgot about everyone outside the windshield. Height is the tax the survivors impose on the bystanders.

Fine print, because the data deserves it: these are observational odds ratios, not guarantees, and Victoria is not Ohio. Height travels with weight, speed, and road type, and big vehicles cluster on faster rural roads, so part of that 11 percent may be where they drive rather than what they are. Melbourne measured overall vehicle height; the US study measured front-end height. That difference probably explains why one says 11 and the other says 22, and neither is the whole story. What the data does not prove: that height causes every one of those deaths. What it strongly suggests: that height is the variable you could regulate tomorrow, and 509 American lives a year is the receipt.

And the uncomfortable counterargument, stated at full strength, because it's the one that matters. That same height protects the people inside, and every buyer choosing the taller vehicle is rationally insuring their own family against a fleet of tall trucks. Nobody in this story is being stupid, which is precisely what makes it a trap: individual rationality, collective body count. The only exit is a design rule nobody has to volunteer for, which is why Tyndall's 1.25-meter cap exists on paper and not on the road.

What you can actually do: when you shop, ask about IIHS pedestrian front-crash-prevention ratings, the number that predicts outcomes for people outside the car, not just the occupant stars on the sticker.[4] Compare front-end height across the models you're considering; it's now a safety spec, whether the brochure admits it or not. Check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because the remedies are free and the letters get lost. And if the height tax bothers you, NHTSA is writing pedestrian protection rules right now. Tell them the per-centimeter price is known, and it's 11 percent.
