1.27 Million Ram 1500s Have a Seatbelt That Clicks, Tugs, and Feels Fine. About 1,271 of Them Will Rip Loose in a Crash.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 20, 2027

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. Webbing locks. Your torso loads the belt with several thousand pounds of force. Every ounce of that force runs through one bolted bracket, into the body of the truck. On about 1,271 Ram 1500 pickups, that bracket was never bolted to anything.[1]

1,271
trucks out of 1,271,294 recalled that Chrysler estimates actually carry the defect: a 0.1% hit rate
8.2 years
build span, February 2018 to April 2026, during which the assembly error survived at Sterling Heights

NHTSA campaign 26V495, filed July 30, 2026, covers certain 2019-2026 Ram 1500s: the second-row center and left-side buckle anchors may have been improperly installed, which means the trucks fail FMVSS 210, Seat Belt Assembly Anchorages.[1] What needs fixing is not a part. It's a bolt somebody forgot to torque, or forgot entirely: bracket present, bolt absent. In an affected truck, the belt clicks into the buckle like it should, tugs back like it should, and passes every inspection an owner can perform, because no owner can perform the one that matters, and Chrysler's quality system couldn't either.

On January 20, 2026, Transport Canada told FCA Canada that a customer had found a loose buckle anchor on a 2025 Ram 1500.[6] From February through July, the company's safety group reviewed the issue with engineering and manufacturing before filing the recall. One Canadian customer did the detecting while the factory spent eight years not noticing.

Do the math the way a forensic engineer does: trucks recalled, 1,271,294.[4] Chrysler's estimate of the actual defect rate: 0.1 percent.[5] That works out to roughly 1,271 trucks whose rear seatbelt cannot do its job, and 1,270,023 dealer visits that end with a shrug and nothing reattached. Add Canada, Mexico, and export markets and the global population reaches about 1.52 million trucks, with a worldwide estimate near 1,517.[7] Stellantis knows of one potentially related injury, with zero confirmed fatalities.[8]

Is this a real crisis or an expensive compliance ritual? Fair question: one unconfirmed injury in eight years across a million and a half trucks is thin evidence for panic, and for 999 of every 1,000 owners this recall is pure administrative theater: drive in, wait, drive out, nothing changed, and the numbers favor the skeptics.

But the skeptics miss what FMVSS 210 exists for.[3] An anchorage is the single load path. Everything downstream assumes it holds: webbing, buckle, pretensioner. An unbolted anchor doesn't degrade restraint; it deletes it, turning the buckle into a projectile and the occupant into an unrestrained one. Because the defect is invisible, "probably not mine" is not information, and nobody knows which 1,271. That is exactly why the standard is written the way it is, and exactly why a 0.1 percent defect rate still bought a 1.27-million-truck recall.

If you own a 2019-2026 Ram 1500, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 26V495 (Chrysler 67D) and book the free inspection.[2] Move it to the top of the list if anyone small rides in your back seat. If you're buying used, put 67D on the checklist next to the Carfax. A belt that looks perfect is the entire point of this recall.
