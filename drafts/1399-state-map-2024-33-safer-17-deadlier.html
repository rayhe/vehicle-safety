Thirty-Three States Got Safer in 2024. Seventeen Got Deadlier. The National Average Is Lying to Both.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 5, 2026

Deaths on American roads fell 4.3 percent in 2024, to 39,254, the third straight annual decline, so pop the champagne, then look at the map, because the national number is a weighted average of two maps moving in opposite directions, and it describes exactly no state in particular.

356
Deaths added by the 17 states (plus D.C.) that got deadlier in 2024, while the 33 improving states removed 2,127

Thirty-three states got safer while seventeen got deadlier, plus the District of Columbia. California cut 293 deaths, Florida 237, Georgia 207. Minnesota added 75, a 19 percent jump, and New Jersey added 65. Maine added 42, which sounds small until you notice it is a 31 percent increase, the largest percentage swing in the country.[1]

Small states swing on a handful of crashes, and that objection is fair, but Minnesota is not a small swing, and neither is New Jersey. Those are 140 extra funerals in two states while the country celebrated a decline. A national average that hides that is a bad dashboard for the people who actually set speed limits and DUI policy, which is to say, the states.

Now the part that should bother you more. In eight states, total deaths fell while alcohol-impaired deaths rose. Alabama, Idaho, Kansas, Montana, Oklahoma, Utah, Vermont, West Virginia. Combined, their drunk-driving deaths rose by 110 in the same year their roads got safer overall. Oklahoma is the poster child: total deaths down 9.3 percent, alcohol deaths up 27 percent, from 177 to 225. One in three Oklahoma road deaths is now alcohol-impaired, up from one in four a year earlier.[1]

California ran the same play in reverse. It cut 293 deaths, the biggest improvement in America, while its alcohol deaths moved by five, which is to say the entire California improvement was a non-alcohol story, and its alcohol share actually crept up from 32 to 34 percent. Georgia is the rare state that did it right on both columns: 207 fewer deaths total, 102 fewer alcohol deaths, a 24 percent drop in drunk-driving fatalities.[1]

And then there is the rate table, which is where the body-count ranking falls apart. Texas had the most deaths, 4,160, because Texas has the most people and the most miles. Mississippi had the highest rate, 25.6 deaths per 100,000 residents, against Rhode Island's 4.7, in the same country, the same year, under the same federal safety standards. A Mississippian is more than five times as likely to die on the road as a Rhode Islander.[2]

Grant all of that, because the decliners do dominate the math: 2,127 removed beats 356 added, and Maine's 42 extra deaths could be a bad winter and a few terrible weekends. It still leaves the fact that matters: the improvement is not uniform, the states that got worse got worse for specific reasons, and in eight states the thing killing people changed even as the total improved. If your state is one of the seventeen, the national celebration is not your story. If your state is one of the eight, your drunk-driving problem got worse in a year your roads got safer, and nobody is going to fix that from Washington.

So check the table, which NHTSA publishes, and ask your state highway safety office what changed in 2024 if you live in Minnesota, New Jersey, or Maine. If you live in Oklahoma, Idaho, Kansas, Montana, Utah, Vermont, or West Virginia, your remaining deaths are increasingly an alcohol problem, which means ignition interlocks and DUI enforcement funding, not another round of road resurfacing. And wherever you live, check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change. The map split in two this year, so find out which half you are standing in.

Limitations: 2024 figures are the FARS Annual Report File, subject to revision when the final file publishes (2023 was revised from 40,901 to 41,025 between ARF and final). Small-state swings are noisy: a dozen crashes either way flips a percentage. Alcohol-impaired counts are NHTSA estimates, with BACs imputed where test results are unknown, not lab-confirmed counts. Per-capita rates use resident population, not miles driven, so states with heavy through-traffic look worse than their residents' risk alone would suggest. One year of state movement is not a trend.
