The Car That Always Loses: Chevrolet Cavalier's 86% Death Share

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 26, 2026

Every fatal crash has a loser, and federal data says the Chevrolet Cavalier loses more often than any other high-volume car in America. Between 2014 and 2023, Cavaliers were involved in 1,429 crashes where someone died, and a Cavalier occupant died in 1,225 of them, or 86 out of every 100.[1] Nobody bought a Cavalier expecting it to win a fight with a truck. But 86% is not losing a fight. It is barely showing up to one.

86%
Share of fatal crashes involving a Chevrolet Cavalier in which a Cavalier occupant died, 2014-2023

Compare that with the other end of the lot. A Ram 1500 was involved in 2,095 fatal crashes in the same decade, and a Ram occupant died in 714 of them, or 34%.[2] Put the two numbers side by side and the Cavalier's share is two and a half times the truck's. Per mile traveled, the gap is wider still: 2.43 occupant deaths per 100 million miles for the Cavalier against 0.13 for the Ram 1500 in the same dataset.[1] Size explains most of it. In a collision between vehicles of different mass, the lighter vehicle's occupants absorb more of the violence, and the Institute's own research on size and weight says exactly that.[3]

Nor is the Cavalier alone at the bottom. Its stablemates and classmates fill out the worst list: the Dodge Neon at 86% (602 deaths in 703 fatal crashes), the Buick LeSabre at 82% (1,344 in 1,633), the Chevrolet Cobalt at 81% (1,540 in 1,907).[2] Every one of them is a cheap, old, small sedan, the kind of car that ends up as a first car, a commuter beater, or the only car a family can afford. Impairment does not explain the pattern either. Among Cavalier drivers with toxicology data, 22.4% showed any impairment, barely above the Ram 1500's 20.3%.[4] Drunk drivers crash more, but they crash everything. Something about the car itself is doing the killing here.

Age sharpens the point. Cavalier deaths by model year peak in 2002 (203 deaths) and 2004 (210 deaths), cars now old enough to have grandchildren.[1] Production ended after model year 2005, which means every Cavalier on the road today predates the side-curtain airbag and stability-control era and has had two decades of rust, wear, and deferred maintenance to get worse. A critic could fairly note that small old sedans are driven disproportionately by young, poor, unbelted drivers on rural roads, and that they collide with trucks outweighing them two to one. Strip out the other vehicle and the road type, and the Cavalier's own structure might look ordinary. But a buyer does not get to strip out the other vehicle. Roads are full of trucks, and this metric measures the car in the world it actually drives in.

So if you drive a Cavalier, a Neon, a Cobalt, or any twenty-year-old economy sedan, drive like the statistics say you should: belted, every seat, every trip, because in a crash bad enough to kill someone, the odds say that someone is you. If you are shopping at the bottom of the used market, let mass and model year beat brand loyalty, since a newer, larger car with side-curtain airbags and stability control is worth stretching for over an older, smaller car with a lower sticker price. Before buying anything used, check its IIHS ratings and run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. A cheap car with a fatal flaw is not cheap.
