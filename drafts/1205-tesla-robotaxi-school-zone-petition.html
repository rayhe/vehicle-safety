Four Videos, Zero Injuries, One Federal File: The Robotaxi School Zone Petition

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 17, 2026

An unnamed person sent NHTSA three video links and a short letter on September 23, and on October 9 the agency opened Defect Petition DP26010, covering roughly 1,000 Tesla Robotaxis.[1] Allegation: driverless cars speeding in active school zones. Casualty count, per the agency’s own resume: zero crashes, zero injuries, zero deaths.[2]

4
incidents in NHTSA’s tally: one formal complaint, three social-media videos — against a fleet of about 1,000

Any member of the public can file a defect petition with NHTSA’s Office of Defects Investigation, and the agency gets 120 days to grant or deny it, which lands this one at January 21, 2027.[3] A grant opens a formal investigation, a road that can end in a recall, while a denial closes the folder, though NHTSA has found nothing defective yet.

School zones are the one place where speeding is treated as a moral event instead of a traffic event: fines double, parents organize, yet the vehicle under review has nobody to fine. In Texas, citations for an ADS-engaged vehicle go to the owner or the driverless authorization holder, because there is no driver.[4] The federal government is spending 120 days deciding whether to investigate a machine that cannot learn a lesson. Bright lines still matter, though nobody is standing on this one.

NHTSA’s school-transportation numbers, 2015 through 2024: 962 fatal crashes, 1,069 dead, an average of 107 per year.[5] Seventy percent were occupants of other vehicles; pedestrians outnumbered school-bus occupants 162 to 119. Of 204 school-age children killed, 77 were pedestrians, and more than half of those were five to ten years old. The deadliest hour was 7 to 8 a.m., and humans built every bit of this risk.

Now do the division the agency will not. Four incidents over an estimated 1,000 vehicles is 0.4 percent, while past escalations have drawn on hundreds of complaints, not four. By the human standard, this file should not exist. By the robot standard, it must: one bad speed map speeds through every school zone the fleet passes at once, and Tesla has lived this movie. In January 2023, NHTSA questioned FSD Beta’s handling of variable speed zones, and the ending was a 362,758-vehicle over-the-air recall.[4]

The strongest case against the joke is that the system is working. The resume names no cities, speeds, or dates; the videos are unverified; the 1,000 figure is an agency estimate; nobody has been hurt, and a denied petition is also a verdict. But the low bar is rational: a human speeder endangers one zone per trip, while a fleet-wide flaw endangers all of them simultaneously. Four videos before the body count is the cheapest possible moment to catch it, when the fix is a patch and not a memorial.

Three things to do: file the petition yourself if your dashcam catches one in Austin, Dallas, or Houston; three video links opened this federal file. Watch the 7 to 8 a.m. window and the dismissal hour.[6] And with National School Bus Safety Week starting October 19, remember the most likely killer near a school is still a human passing a stopped bus, about 2.5 deaths a year, half of them minors.[7] Watch the curb, because the robots are being watched already.
