Same Truck, Two Badges, Twice the Death Rate

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 24, 2026

General Motors built the same compact pickup twice. One wore a Chevrolet badge and the other wore a GMC badge, and both rolled off the same line on the same GMT325 platform with the same sheet metal underneath. The Chevrolet version killed at more than twice the rate of the GMC version. Federal data says the gap is real, it is not small, and GM has never publicly explained why.[1]

2.27×
How much deadlier the Chevrolet S-10 was than its GMC Sonoma twin, the same truck with a different badge

From 2014 through 2023, the Chevrolet S-10 racked up 1,427 deaths at a rate of 4.83 per 100 million miles traveled. That puts it eighth worst out of 337 nameplates in the federal Fatality Analysis Reporting System, in company with the Mustang and the Impala. Its twin, the GMC Sonoma, recorded 378 deaths at a rate of 2.13 over the same decade. Divide one by the other and you get 2.27. Same truck. Same factory. One badge kills at more than twice the rate of the other.[1]

Production ended in January 2004. GM replaced the S-10 with the Colorado, and the Colorado's fatality rate is 0.28 per 100 million miles. Run that division and the truck that replaced the S-10 is 17.2 times safer per mile than the truck it replaced, while its GMC twin, the Canyon, comes in at 0.59, or 8.2 times safer. Even the S-10's contemporaries did better: Ford's Ranger managed 2.91, and Dodge's Dakota managed 2.62.[1]

And the pattern repeated. Colorado's GMC twin, the Canyon, rates 0.59 against the Colorado's 0.28, a 2.1-times gap on the same platform, one generation later. Whatever separates a Chevrolet badge from a GMC badge, it survived a full redesign.[1]

A dead truck should stop killing people, but this one never got the memo. In the decade from 2014 through 2023, a truck that had not been sold new in twenty years still accounted for about 143 deaths a year, and the federal fleet estimate still counts roughly 219,000 S-10s on the road. More than half of those deaths, 56.4 percent, came from model years 1998 through 2003, the peak of the second generation. A 2000 model year S-10 alone accounts for 178 deaths. Its final model year, 2004, a crew-cab-only farewell run with tiny production, accounts for just 16.[1]

Drunk drivers are the first suspect in any pickup fatality story, and here the suspect has an alibi. Among S-10 drivers in fatal crashes, 20.5 percent tested positive for alcohol or drugs, against 17.4 percent for Sonoma drivers and about 20 percent across the fleet. A three-point gap in impairment cannot produce a 127 percent gap in fatality rate, and the statistics agree: the 95 percent confidence intervals for the two rates, 4.58 to 5.08 for the S-10 and 1.92 to 2.34 for the Sonoma, do not overlap, which means this is not noise. Something real separates the two badges.[1]

Crashworthiness explains part of the S-10's problem and none of the twin gap. A driver-side airbag did not arrive until the 1995 model year, which means the earliest trucks in the fatality file had no airbag at all, and no S-10 ever offered electronic stability control.[7] IIHS rated the 2003 truck Marginal in the moderate overlap test, and Edmunds said it plainly back in 1996, praising the Ranger and Dakota's good crashworthiness against the S-10's poor crash test scores.[3][4] All of that is true, and all of it applies equally to the Sonoma, which is the same truck. So the gap lives somewhere the crash lab cannot see: who bought which badge, what trim they could afford, where they drove it, and how hard. FARS does not break out trim level or buyer demographics, so the mechanism is, for now, an open question with a 2.27-times exclamation point on it.

Before anyone declares the bowtie cursed, the honest caveats. FARS only counts fatal crashes, so a low fatality rate is not proof of a low injury rate. Its rates rest on estimated miles and estimated fleet counts, not odometer readings, and those estimates wobble by roughly 15 percent for old, low-volume models like these. Sonoma's sample is small, 378 deaths against the S-10's 1,427, and if the underlying registration split between the two badges is misestimated, the gap shrinks. The Canyon/Colorado repeat is suggestive, not proof: both pairs rest on the same registration data, so a badge-split artifact would show up in both. None of that closes it, because even at the outer edges of the uncertainty, where the S-10's rate falls to 4.58 and the Sonoma's rises to 2.34, the Chevrolet twin remains roughly twice as deadly as its GMC sibling.[1]

Shopping for a cheap used compact pickup, skip the 1998 to 2003 S-10. Its direct replacement, the Colorado or the Canyon, is 17 times safer per mile, and the Canyon is the same truck as the Colorado with a different badge, a distinction that apparently matters more than anyone at GM ever admitted. If you already own one, trucks from 1995 onward have a driver airbag, and earlier trucks have none. Check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change, and think twice before putting a kid in an extended-cab jump seat, because a truck that scored Marginal in 2003 has not gotten any safer since.[6]
