Orange County Stopped Warning Parents About E-Motos. Now It Charges Them.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
January 3, 2028

Orange County prosecutors have charged 12 parents since January 2026 with child endangerment for letting children illegally ride electric motorcycles on public roads, according to announcements from District Attorney Todd Spitzer in October 2026.[1] Twelve parents. Nine months. One county decided that warnings had run their course.

Start with Garden Grove, because prosecutors did. A 13-year-old was speeding, running red lights, and popping wheelies on an E-Ride Pro SS 2.0, a machine its brand rates at 55 mph, when he lost control on May 7, hit a raised center divider, was ejected, and collided with a concrete pole. He died at UCI Medical Center. His location-sharing app showed 43 mph when he lost control. Six months earlier he had broken his leg on a different electric vehicle, and prosecutors allege his father bought him a faster machine anyway despite repeated warnings. His father faces felony child endangerment and misdemeanor contributing-to-delinquency counts carrying up to six years in state prison; his mother faces a misdemeanor count carrying up to one year in county jail. All charges are allegations, not convictions, a distinction that matters more than usual when grieving parents are defendants.[2]

Yorba Linda supplied the other two cases, and both children survived. An 11-year-old collided with a pickup truck on June 30 while riding a machine capable of more than 33 mph, suffering broken bones and an arterial bleed; his father faces a misdemeanor child endangerment count. A 9-year-old was speeding on a machine capable of more than 47 mph when he was hit by a pickup truck; his mother faces a felony child endangerment count plus an infraction for allowing a child under 18 to drive a motor vehicle without a license. Note the ages: nine, eleven, thirteen. California crash data cited by ABC News puts ages 11 to 14 at 61.7 percent of the state's electric motorcycle crashes. Orange County is prosecuting the median rider, not outliers.[3]

Why are these motorcycles at all, legally speaking, when sellers list them as e-bikes? California classifies by hardware, not by product title. A legal e-bike has working pedals and a motor at or under 750 watts, with assistance capped by class at 20 or 28 mph, and Class 3 riders must be at least 16. A machine with no pedals, a bigger motor, or a higher top speed falls outside that definition and gets treated as a motorcycle or moped: license, registration, and insurance, none of which a 9-year-old can hold. That gap between the listing and the statute is where this enforcement wave lives.[4]

Prosecutors did not start with prison. An April 2026 case shows the ladder: an Aliso Viejo mother was charged after her son's Surron electric motorcycle struck an 81-year-old substitute teacher near a high school, critically injuring him, and the charging papers describe deputies warning her in a 28-minute body-camera conversation in June 2025, after she admitted buying the machine and knowing her son rode it recklessly. She faces up to six years and eight months. Read that sequence straight: warning, then injury, then charges. Spitzer told CBS News he needed to send a message to save other children's lives. Whether charges deter anyone is testable: his office says these machines are now the leading cause of juvenile emergency-room visits in the county, which gives the experiment a before-and-after number.[1][5]

Now the strongest case against all of this. Prosecuting bereaved parents is brutal, and it may not work. Sellers legally market these machines to adults as e-bikes while the state has done a poor job of making the pedals-and-watts line legible to a normal shopper. Impoundment, fines, civil liability, and point-of-sale warnings could plausibly do the same job without a courtroom, and Orange County enforcement binds no other county. Until the district attorney publishes juvenile emergency-room numbers from before and after the charges, deterrence is a theory wearing a press release. Fair, and incomplete: a theory is more than the nothing that preceded it, which was a warning, a faster bike, and a dead 13-year-old.[5]

So check the machine before you buy it, and check it by hardware, not by listing title. Working pedals? Motor at or under 750 watts? Assistance stopping at 20 or 28 mph by class? If any answer fails, California calls it a motorcycle, and no child can legally ride it on a public road. If one is already in your garage, keep the key and the charger apart and keep children off public roads on it entirely, because a warning from a deputy is no longer the worst outcome on offer. Orange County has shown what comes after the warning.[4]
