A Seat Switch That Won't Turn Off Has 94,622 Genesis Sedans Parked Outside With No Fix

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 26, 2026

Turn the car off, walk inside, go to sleep. Somewhere in the garage, the seat motor is still running. That is the defect behind NHTSA campaign 26V664, filed October 9, 2026, covering 94,622 Genesis sedans whose front power seat switch can stay engaged and overheat the motor until it catches fire, parked or driving.[1] No remedy exists yet; until one does, owners are told to park outside.

946
Estimated cars that actually carry the defect, 1% of the 94,622 recalled

Covered cars are 54,644 model-year 2015-2016 Hyundai Genesis sedans and 39,978 model-year 2017-2020 Genesis G80 sedans.[1] It is the same second-generation DH-platform sedan: Hyundai moved the car under its new Genesis brand in 2017 and changed the badge, not the car. Only cars with non-memory power front seats are included, which means a model-year match alone proves nothing and the cheaper trims carry a risk the loaded trims do not.

Why a parked car is the exposure: if the slider or height switch is damaged or knocked out of position, it can stay engaged, so the motor keeps running after the occupant leaves.[2] No driver required. No key required. No motion required. Hyundai reports two alleged fires in the United States tied to the defect, with no crashes and no injuries as of October 1, 2026.[2] A burning smell from the seat area or a dead seat switch means stop using the switch and call a dealer.

Now the part owners will hate: a fix is still under development, with no date attached.[1] VINs became searchable on NHTSA.gov on October 13, and owner letters are expected December 7, 2026. A repair, when it arrives, will be free, with reimbursement for owners who already paid, and dealers can offer loaners to owners uncomfortable driving.[2] Cold comfort to someone whose garage is off limits for an indefinite winter.

And this is the second time these cars have been exiled to the driveway. In 2021, campaign 21V160 recalled 94,646 of the same model lines for an anti-lock brake module that could short-circuit and cause an engine-compartment fire, also with a park-outside advisory.[3] Different system, different defect, nearly the same headcount, a gap of just 24 cars between the two populations, and the same badge back in the same driveway for a second fire recall.

Fair objection, stated properly: two alleged fires across 94,622 cars is a tiny absolute risk, and Hyundai's own 1% estimate means roughly 99% of owners are parking outside for a defect their car does not have. Months outdoors is a real cost without a driveway, and it punishes a careful majority for a few hundred bad switches. That objection is mostly right about the odds and still wrong about the advice: a parked-car fire in an attached garage is a house fire at 3 a.m., and that asymmetry, not the probability, justifies the advisory.

Honestly stated, this article does not prove the counts: the two-fire total and the 1% estimate come from Hyundai's own filing, not independent verification, so the true defective count could differ either way. No fatality data is involved, so no death rate is computed or should be inferred, and remedy timing is unknown.

What you actually do: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now instead of waiting for the December letter, and park outside if your car is included, even if the seat works perfectly today. Shopping one of these cars used, check both campaigns, 21V160 and 26V664, because clearing the latest recall does not clear the earlier one. Ninety seconds of VIN checking beats a winter of guessing.
