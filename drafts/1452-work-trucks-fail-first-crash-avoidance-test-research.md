## Key verified facts (from IIHS, Oct 8, 2026)

- 11 commercial vehicles tested: 7 cargo vans, 4 Class 3 pickups (GVWR 10,001-14,000 lbs).
- **Vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention: 9 of 11 rated substandard** (7 Poor, 2 Marginal). Only the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned Good; only the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 earned Acceptable.
- **Not one Class 3 pickup earned Good in either test.** Silverado 3500HD, F-350, Sierra 3500: Poor vehicle-to-vehicle. Ram 3500: Marginal. Pedestrian: F-350 and Ram 3500 Acceptable; Silverado/Sierra Marginal.
- **Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500: Poor in BOTH tests because they have no AEB at all** — optional forward collision warning only. These are 2025-2027 model-year vehicles being sold new today.
- Cargo van table: BrightDrop 400 Acceptable/Good; Express 2500 Poor/Poor; Transit T250 Poor/Marginal; Savana 2500 Poor/Poor; Sprinter 2500 Good/Acceptable; ProMaster 2500 Poor/Marginal; Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 Marginal/Good.
- **Motorcycle detection was the biggest failure.** "Several systems did not issue warnings or slow at all in most of the motorcycle trials." Others slowed but were downgraded for late warnings.
- **The child dummy disappeared.** Several vehicles, including the Acceptable-rated F-350 and Ram 3500, had a harder time detecting a child in daylight than an adult at night. Sensors sit near the rearview mirror; tall front ends + short child dummy = the child was detected at distance, then "disappeared" as the vehicle closed in.
- Nighttime adult-pedestrian trials: poor/marginal systems failed to slow enough in most trials; some performed substantially worse on low beams than high beams. Some did worse at the slowest test speed than at faster speeds.
- **The body count:** "In 2024, 6,215 people were killed in crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans, accounting for 16% of all roadway fatalities in the U.S."
- IIHS President David Harkey: "These results are a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers. Front crash prevention is especially important for these larger, heavier vans and trucks because of the danger they pose to other road users."
- IIHS launched its commercial vehicle program in June 2026 with driver protection checks (airbags, seat belt reminders). "Many government standards for passenger vehicles, including airbag requirements, don't apply to delivery vans, box trucks, tractor-trailers and larger work pickups."
- Testing continues through 2027 (headlights, rear AEB added); Class 4-6 trucks phased in later.

## Key verified facts (regulatory gap)

- FMVSS 127 (April/May 2024) mandates AEB + pedestrian AEB on all passenger cars and light trucks **with GVWR of 10,000 pounds or less**, by September 1, 2029. Systems must stop and avoid contact with a lead vehicle up to 62 mph, detect pedestrians day and night, auto-brake up to 90 mph for vehicles and 45 mph for pedestrians.
- Class 3 pickups start at **10,001 lbs GVWR — one pound over the line.** The entire class tested by IIHS is exempt from the AEB mandate by design of the weight cutoff.
- NHTSA + FMCSA announced a separate NPRM in June 2023 to require AEB on heavy vehicles including tractor-trailers; per NHTSA's own release it is still "in the process of finalizing" — no final rule as of November 2026.
- FMVSS 127 itself is in legal limbo: Auto Innovators sued in D.C. Circuit (Jan 17, 2025); DOT got the appeal held in abeyance (Feb 28, 2025) pending review; the September 2029 deadline stands on paper but the rule's future is unresolved.

## Original computations (not in the source)

- Substandard rate: 9 of 11 vehicles (81.8%) rated Marginal or Poor on vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention. For passenger cars in IIHS's current ratings, substandard vehicle-to-vehicle ratings are rare — the commercial fleet is failing a test passenger cars routinely pass.
- The 1-pound cliff: FMVSS 127 covers GVWR ≤ 10,000 lbs. Class 3 begins at 10,001 lbs. A Ram 3500 at 10,001 lbs GVWR is exempt; a Ram 1500 at 10,000 lbs is covered. One pound of rated capacity is the entire difference between "must stop itself" and "may not even warn you."
- Death share: 6,215 / 39,254 = 15.8% of all 2024 traffic deaths involved heavy/medium-duty vehicles or light vans — IIHS rounds to 16%. That is roughly 17 deaths per day, every day, in crashes involving the vehicle classes with the weakest crash-avoidance tech.
- The discontinued good van: the BrightDrop 400 earned one of only two Good pedestrian ratings — and GM ended BrightDrop production in October 2025 (per trade coverage). The good van is already dead; the vans that can't stop are still on sale.
- Fleet economics: at $55,000+ average cost per on-the-job crash (IIHS via Automotive Fleet), a 100-van fleet averaging one crash per van per year would burn $5.5M annually — before injury or fatality costs. The Sprinter's Good rating is not a luxury spec; it is a line item.
- Zero-for-four: 0 of 4 Class 3 pickups earned Good in either test (8 test slots, 0 Good). The best-selling work trucks in America — F-350, Silverado 3500HD, Sierra 3500, Ram 3500 — collectively failed the first test ever applied to them.

## Novel angle (kill test: PASS)

The AEB mandate was sold as the rule that would end rear-end crashes. It has a weight cutoff, and the cutoff is exactly where the killing starts: everything over 10,000 lbs GVWR is exempt, and IIHS just proved what that exemption buys. The vehicles that do the most damage to other people — 6,215 deaths, 16% of the national toll — are the ones that were never required to stop themselves, and when finally tested, 9 of 11 couldn't. The punchline is the arithmetic: the mandate line is 10,000 lbs, Class 3 starts at 10,001. One pound. Site coverage check: #986/#999/#1014 covered the passenger-car AEB mandate; #945 covered the old-fleet ESC gap; #1426 covered FMVSS 127's motorcycle blind spot for passenger cars. Nobody has covered the first IIHS commercial-vehicle ratings, the 10,000-lb cliff, or the Express/Savana no-AEB-at-all fact. The motorcycle-detection failure also ties the story to #1426 from the other side: the same blind spot, now in a 14,000-lb truck.

## Strongest counterargument (must state)

IIHS tests are laboratory protocols, not real-world crash outcomes — a Poor rating means the system failed IIHS's specific scenarios (unloaded vehicle, specific speeds, specific targets), not that the truck is proven deadly in service. Commercial vehicles are usually tested unloaded or as incomplete builds, and heavy loads and upfits change braking performance in both directions. The Express/Savana's missing AEB is a real spec gap, but these vans are also among the cheapest work vans sold, and fleet buyers who chose them chose price — the market, not just the regulator, produced this outcome. And the 6,215-death figure counts crashes *involving* these vehicle classes, not crashes *caused* by them; a delivery van rear-ended at a red light counts the same as a van that plowed into stopped traffic. The honest claim is narrower: the exemption left a measurable capability gap, IIHS measured it, and the gap is largest exactly where the vehicles are heaviest.

## Actionable takeaways (required)

- For fleet buyers: the IIHS ratings are now a procurement document. A Sprinter 2500 (Good vehicle-to-vehicle) or BrightDrop 400 / Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 (Good pedestrian) versus an Express/Savana with no AEB at all is a spec-sheet decision with a $55,000-per-crash price tag attached. Ask the dealer for the IIHS rating before signing a fleet order, not after the first rear-end claim.
- For van shoppers: if you are buying a 2025-2027 Chevrolet Express or GMC Savana, know that no AEB exists on the option sheet at any price. Forward collision warning only — a beep, not a brake pedal.
- For everyone else on the road: the 14,000-lb truck behind you in traffic is statistically less likely to stop itself than the sedan next to it. Leave the gap. The truck's driver may be excellent; the truck's software is not.
- For buyers of heavy pickups: none of the four Class 3 pickups earned Good in either IIHS crash-avoidance test. If you tow with an F-350 or Silverado 3500HD, your truck's crash prevention is rated Poor or Marginal — drive like the system is decorative, because IIHS just showed it mostly is.

## Limitations (required)

- IIHS ratings are protocol results, not real-world crash rates; no FARS-based fatality rate by commercial model exists in this file to cross-check the lab results against outcomes.
- Vehicles were tested unloaded or as incomplete builds; loaded/upfitted performance may differ (IIHS's own caveat, via Automotive Fleet).
- The 6,215-death figure is crashes *involving* heavy/medium-duty vehicles or light vans, not crashes *caused* by them; fault attribution is not in the IIHS release.
- 2024 death total (39,254) is from the FARS Annual Report File, subject to revision when the Final File lands.
- BrightDrop production end (October 2025) comes from trade coverage of the IIHS results, not from GM directly; verify before stating as GM fact.
- FMVSS 127's legal status (abeyance, freeze) is as reported by Nelson Mullins in 2026; the rule could change before the September 2029 deadline.
