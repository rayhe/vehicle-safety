Thanksgiving Is America's Deadliest Holiday. The Killer Isn't the Drive Home.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 26, 2026

Thanksgiving is the deadliest holiday on American roads, not because of the traffic but because of the reunion. Between 2019 and 2023, drunk drivers killed 868 people during Thanksgiving holiday periods, more than one-third of everyone who died on the road in those windows.[1] AAA expects 81.8 million Americans to travel at least 50 miles for Thanksgiving this year, a record.[6] The roads will be full, and that is not the problem.

868
People killed by drunk drivers during Thanksgiving holiday periods, 2019-2023

Travel coverage misses the split. Wednesday night, the famous Blackout Wednesday, has the reputation. Bars fill with college kids home for the holiday and old friends back in town, and every year the warnings go out about the biggest drinking night of the year. But a five-year analysis of federal crash data puts the deadliest drunk-driving day of the holiday week on the Saturday after Thanksgiving, not the Wednesday before.[7] Reputation tracks the drinking; death tracks the drive home, after the reunion has dispersed and the designated-driver plan has quietly expired. Federal tallies count the holiday from Wednesday evening to Monday before dawn, a window that catches both nights.[3]

Most Thanksgiving coverage treats the holiday as an interstate story: congestion windows, worst times to leave, which hour to hit I-95. That framing gets the mechanism wrong, because the fatal trips are short, local, and familiar. A 23-year-old drives two miles to a childhood friend's house on roads he has known since he was sixteen, at 1 a.m., after a night that started as catching up and ended as a blackout. Drivers ages 21 to 34 make up the largest share of Thanksgiving drunk-driving deaths, and on Thanksgiving Eve 2023, a third of the drivers killed were drunk.[2] Nobody in that statistic was on a road trip.

Fairness demands the other side of the ledger. Thanksgiving travel may be safer per mile than an ordinary week, since the surge is mostly daytime family highway driving, and the holiday's death toll is high partly because 81.8 million people are moving at once. Counted in bodies, Thanksgiving looks like a massacre; counted in rates, an individual trip may be unremarkable. The reply is in the share, not the count: alcohol's share of deaths rises from 29 percent on an average day to 35 percent during Thanksgiving week.[7] Something about the week itself, not just its volume, changes the risk. The excess is countable: at a 29 percent baseline, a 500-death holiday period would expect about 145 alcohol deaths, while the observed 35 percent share implies roughly 30 more, every single year, as an arithmetic estimate from published shares rather than a finding from crash records.

So plan for two nights, not one, and set the sober ride before the first drink on Wednesday and again on Saturday, because Saturday is when the data says the risk peaks and the vigilance runs out. If you are hosting, the highest-risk guests are the 21-to-34-year-olds sleeping in their childhood bedrooms, so offer the couch, not the keys. Buckle up on every trip, including the two-mile one, since the National Safety Council estimates belts save 157 Thanksgiving lives a year, with 111 more if everyone buckled.[5] And before the holiday drive, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. A free fix beats a tow on I-95.
