5,340 People Died in Large-Truck Crashes in 2024. 83% of Them Were Not in the Truck.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: the federal government keeps a body count for large-truck crashes, and in 2024 that count was 5,340 people, of whom 920 were in the truck.[1] Everyone else was in the way: the sedans next to it, the pickups in front of it, the crosswalks it rolled through. If you want the safest seat in a large-truck crash, the data has a clear answer: inside the large truck. You are welcome to sit with that.

4.8 to 1
The ratio of deaths outside the truck (4,420) to deaths inside it (920) in 2024 crashes involving large trucks, per NHTSA's DOT HS 813 816.

NHTSA's full accounting breaks the toll into three piles: 3,753 occupants of other vehicles, 920 truck occupants, and 667 nonoccupants, meaning pedestrians and bicyclists who met a freight vehicle with no sheet metal negotiating on their behalf.[1] For every truck occupant who dies, roughly five other people die with them. Same story on the injury side: three-quarters of the 161,201 people hurt in large-truck crashes were not in the truck either. A semi is not a participant in traffic so much as a weather system that other people get caught in.

California sharpens the picture into a regional bruise. Of the 391 Californians killed in large-truck crashes last year, 68 were pedestrians or bicyclists: 17.4 percent, against a national baseline of 12.5 percent.[3] Only Florida runs hotter on share (19.5 percent); only Texas beats California on raw count (73). Long urban arterials and sprawl give the truck more pedestrians to find. State lines mean nothing to the machine; land use is what decides.

And here is the part that scrambles the villain narrative. Four percent of large-truck drivers in fatal crashes had a BAC at or above .08, compared with 25 percent of car drivers and 25 percent of motorcyclists.[1] The person piloting 80,000 pounds is roughly six times less likely to be drunk than the person beside them, and less likely to be driving on a suspended license too. Crashes happen anyway, overwhelmingly on weekdays (76 percent) and in daylight work hours. Danger here is not the midnight drunk but the Tuesday afternoon delivery schedule.

Call it arithmetic, not malice, and the call is mostly fair. Large trucks are five percent of registered vehicles and ten percent of all vehicle miles traveled, and every one of those miles is shared with other traffic.[1] The dead are disproportionately outside the truck because almost everyone on the road is outside the truck. In two-vehicle fatal crashes, both vehicles were simply proceeding straight 42.4 percent of the time: ordinary traffic, ordinary people, an 80,000-pound mismatch. Nobody had to be evil. Mass times momentum did the work, and it bills by the pound.

One more caveat: the 2024 figures are FARS Annual Report File counts, preliminary and subject to revision; the injury numbers are sample estimates, not a census. Remember the California anomaly is a share, not a rate: the state has more urban miles and more pedestrians, which mechanically inflates the nonoccupant slice, and the federal file gives no state-level truck mileage denominator to convert shares into rates. BAC figures blend tested and imputed values, and commercial drivers are tested more consistently, which colors the 4-versus-25 comparison. And "large truck" means anything over 10,000 pounds GVWR, buses and motorhomes excluded. Read the fine print before you read the moral.

So what do you do with a toll that is mostly other people's physics? Give trucks actual room. Never linger alongside a trailer; if you cannot see its mirrors, the driver cannot see you, and the person missing from the mirror is statistically you. Never cut in front of a large truck and brake: in 6.3 percent of two-vehicle fatal truck crashes, the truck was already stopped in the road when the other vehicle arrived.[1] For pedestrians and cyclists, treat turning trucks like weather and give them the intersection. In California and Florida, nearly one in six large-truck deaths is a person on foot or on a bike. No drunk driver required. What it needs is a Tuesday.
