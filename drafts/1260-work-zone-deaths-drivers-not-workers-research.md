# Research: Work Zones Kill Two People a Day. Almost None of Them Are Workers.

## Angle
Every spring, National Work Zone Awareness Week tells drivers to slow down to protect the people working on the road. Worthy message, wrong protagonist. Federal data for 2024 shows 850 people killed in work zones, and the overwhelming majority were not workers at all: 673 were drivers and passengers, 177 were pedestrians (a category that includes workers but also everyone else on foot). National Safety Council's breakdown puts drivers at 523 deaths (62%), passengers at 150 (18%), pedestrians at 156 (18%). Separately, Bureau of Labor Statistics data cited by NSC puts worker-pedestrians struck in work zones at roughly 54 per year. In other words, the campaign framed as worker protection is, in body-count terms, mostly about protecting the people driving through.

## Kill test
Passes. Zero prior work-zone articles in the queue (title grep: 0 hits). Fresh data peg: FHWA released the 2024 FARS-based numbers for National Work Zone Awareness Week in spring 2026. Novel angle: the victim inversion (drivers, not workers) plus two structural facts that explain it: fatal work zone crashes grew 46% from 2010 to 2024 against 20% for fatal crashes overall, and 45% of fatal work zone crashes happen on interstates, freeways, or expressways versus 16% of fatal crashes overall. This is a high-speed, driver-killing phenomenon wearing a worker-safety costume.

## Caveats to state in the article
- "Pedestrians" in FARS work zone data is not synonymous with "workers": it includes anyone on foot in or near the zone. The worker-specific figure comes from BLS occupational data, a different system, so the 54-per-year figure is an estimate, not a FARS cross-tab.
- 2024 figures are from the FARS Annual Report File, subject to revision as late reports arrive.
- Zone-type split has a large "unknown type" bucket (272 of 850), so type-level claims are soft.

## Actionable takeaways
- Slow down before the cones, not at them: nearly half of fatal work zone crashes are on high-speed roads, where the speed differential at the taper is the killer.
- Treat the merge like a zipper early: late merges and sudden lane changes at the taper are the classic work zone crash setup.
- If you drive for work through construction corridors daily, your exposure is the risk factor; no app or vehicle feature substitutes for dropping 10 mph before the first sign.
