By The Numbers

America’s Roads Got 4% Safer in 2024. Maine Got 31% Deadlier.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again. NHTSA’s final 2024 data puts American traffic deaths at 39,254, down 4.3 percent from 41,025 in 2023, with the fatality rate falling to 1.19 deaths per 100 million miles traveled.[1] That is the best road-safety headline in years, and it is also a lie by averaging, because underneath it, sixteen jurisdictions, fifteen states plus the District of Columbia, got deadlier while the country got safer.[2]

Mississippi’s traffic death rate per 100,000 residents (25.6), divided by Rhode Island’s (4.7). Same country, same year, same federal traffic laws.

Start with Maine, whose death toll went from 135 to 177 in a single year, a 31 percent jump, the largest percentage increase in the country. That is 42 more deaths, decided by 167 fatal crashes. Motorcyclists accounted for 35 of those 177 deaths, nearly one in five, against a national share closer to one in six.[2] One bad summer on two wheels can move a small state’s whole year, and in 2024, it did.

Maine’s crashes also look different from the national pattern. Sixty-five of its 167 fatal crashes were single vehicles hitting a fixed object, 38.9 percent against 27.8 percent nationally, while non-collision crashes like rollovers were only 14, or 8.4 percent against 21.9 percent nationally.[2] Maine’s problem reads less like traffic and more like cars leaving the road and finding a tree, which is a road-design and speed story, not a congestion story.

Minnesota added the most bodies of any state that got worse: 477 deaths against 402 the year before, up 19 percent, or 75 more people, and New Jersey added 65, rising to 670. Nebraska and Alaska both jumped double digits in percentage terms. None of these are statistical typos, because FARS is a census, not a sample, and every one of those deaths is a record in a federal file.[2]

Raw counts flatter big states and punish small ones, so run the rates instead. Mississippi recorded 25.6 deaths per 100,000 residents in 2024, Rhode Island 4.7. Those rates sit on very different counts, 753 deaths in Mississippi against 52 in Rhode Island, which is why the small-state caveats later in this piece matter. Arkansas sat at 19.5, New Mexico at 19.2, while Massachusetts managed 5.1 and New York 5.5, against a national average of 11.5. Mississippi alone runs at more than double that, and a Mississippian’s per-capita risk of dying on the road was 5.4 times a Rhode Islander’s.[3]

Maybe the per-capita gap is just a driving gap. Rhode Islanders drive less, and a rate per resident punishes states where everyone is behind the wheel all day. Control for miles instead and Mississippi still wins the wrong prize: 1.81 deaths per 100 million miles, against 0.59 in Massachusetts and a national 1.19, a 3.1-times gap on the friendlier denominator. Arizona climbs to 1.74 on this measure, South Carolina to 1.67. Mississippi is the rare state that tops both rankings at once, which means its roads are dangerous per person and per mile, and no choice of denominator rescues it.[3]

Which denominator you pick changes the villain list. Arizona looks merely bad by population, 16.2 deaths per 100,000, then jumps to second-worst in the country once you divide by miles driven, 1.74 per 100 million.[3] Per-capita measures the risk of living somewhere, per-mile measures the risk of driving there, and a state can fail one test while passing the other, which is why Mississippi topping both at once is the number that should worry you most.

None of this erases the national improvement. Thirty-three states got better, Georgia shed 207 deaths in a single year, Rhode Island fell 27 percent, Wyoming 26. NHTSA’s early estimate for 2025 puts the national toll at 36,640, down another 6.7 percent, with a rate of 1.10 that would be the second-lowest ever recorded.[4] Three straight annual declines after the pandemic spike is a real trend, not a blip, and the people who built it deserve the credit.

Every state’s danger has a different shape. Kentucky matched Georgia’s 13 percent improvement, falling to 707 deaths from 814. On the person-type table, the District of Columbia lost 18 pedestrians out of 47 total deaths, more than one in three, Hawaii lost 35 pedestrians out of 102, and Florida lost 642 motorcyclists, one in five of its 3,138 deaths.[2] A single national number hides all of that, which is exactly why the state table is worth reading instead of the headline.

Before anyone declares Maine’s roads broken or Mississippi’s beyond saving, the caveats matter, and they are not small. A 31 percent jump in Maine is 42 deaths, and a single crash that kills four people is nearly a tenth of the whole year’s increase in a state that small. Rates punish rural states where residents drive long distances on two-lane roads far from trauma centers, and they say nothing about speed limits, belt use, or the share of old pickup trucks in the fleet. One year is not a trend, and Minnesota’s 19 percent jump could be noise, weather, or a bad winter, and 2025 might hand it all back. All true.

What this count cannot do is explain itself. FARS records who died, where, and in what kind of crash. It does not record why a state’s toll moved, and no regression in this article controls for the things IIHS itself lists as confounders: vehicle mix, travel speeds, licensure rates, traffic laws, emergency care, weather, terrain.[3] Maine’s motorcyclist share is a clue, not a cause. Treat the rankings as a map of where to look, not a verdict on why.

So what do you do with a map like this? If you live in Mississippi, Arkansas, or New Mexico, understand that your baseline risk is roughly double the national rate before you ever pick a car, and pick the car accordingly: the safest vehicle in the IIHS ratings matters more where the roads are worst. If you are moving states, the change in your road risk can dwarf the change from switching models. Check your state’s rate at IIHS’s state-by-state page before you check a car’s star rating. And if you ride a motorcycle in Maine, the 2024 numbers are talking directly to you: 35 riders died, one in five of the state’s total, and that share is the number to watch in 2025.
