One in Three Cars Has Headlights IIHS Calls Poor or Marginal. At Cadillac, It's Four in Five.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 21, 2026

Before you sign that lease, memorize this: your car has a safety rating that appears on no window sticker, in no dealer brochure, and in approximately zero used-car listings. IIHS scores headlights on how far they actually throw light on a real road, minus a penalty for blinding oncoming drivers, and across 1,643 rated vehicles 542 come back Poor or Marginal. One in three.[1]

33%
Share of IIHS-rated vehicles with Poor or Marginal headlights, 2016–2026 model years

The five-star crash score you shopped with says nothing about any of this. Stars come from NHTSA. Headlights are graded by a different organization on a different scale, and a car can hold five stars while lighting the road like a birthday candle. IIHS chief research officer David Zuby put it bluntly a decade ago: if you're having trouble seeing behind the wheel at night, it could very well be your headlights and not your eyes.[2]

This is the only safety test where a third of the market flunks and nobody talks about it. It's also the only one measuring a system you use every single night. Roughly half of all traffic deaths happen in the dark or in dawn and dusk conditions, which is why the rating matters far beyond its obscurity.[2] Cars whose headlights earn Good for visibility are involved in 19% fewer nighttime single-vehicle crashes and 23% fewer nighttime pedestrian crashes than cars with Poor-rated headlights, according to an IIHS study of police-reported crashes.[3]

Group the failures by brand and the list doesn't look like any other safety ranking you've seen. Cadillac: 79% of its 28 rated vehicles come back Poor or Marginal. Chevrolet: 77%. GMC: 76%. Jeep: 62%. Chrysler: 60%. The worst brands in America for headlights are all American brands, sitting at double the market rate or worse.[1]

Part of the explanation is how the lights got packaged. For years the decent headlight on an American SUV was an option bundled into an expensive trim package, while the base car kept the cheap unit. IIHS rates the hardware actually fitted to the car it tests, so one model can legitimately appear twice with two different results.[1] That is worth knowing when shopping used: the rating you read online may belong to a trim level the car in front of you does not have. Two minutes on iihs.org/ratings with the exact year, model, and trim beats any amount of squinting in the dealer lot after dark.[5]

Now the twist, because there is one, and it is the strangest part of this story. Headlights are the fastest-improving safety metric IIHS has ever measured. When the institute released its first headlight ratings in 2016, exactly one system out of more than eighty earned a Good. By the 2025 model year, 51% did, and the Poor-or-Marginal share fell from 82% to about 16%.[3] ForCar's model-year data shows the same cliff: 24% bad in 2022, 15% in 2023, 14% in 2024, 8% in 2025.[1]

What fixed it? Not regulation. IIHS made a Good headlight rating a requirement for its Top Safety Pick award, manufacturers chased the award, and fixing the lights turned out to be the cheapest route to it.[1] A decade of federal inaction on the same hardware got lapped in four model years by a trophy. Something to remember the next time someone tells you safety can only be mandated from Washington.

But the improvement is concentrated almost entirely in new cars. A 2018 or 2019 vehicle was built before the award rules tightened, and its odds of a Poor rating look a lot more like the old numbers than the new ones. Nothing about the rating appears in a listing, nothing about it changes with mileage, and nothing about it degrades gracefully: it is a property of the design, frozen at the factory.[1] The used market is a time capsule of the worst headlights IIHS ever measured, and the buyer is the last person to know.

So here is the consumer playbook, since nobody else is writing one. If you are shopping used, especially 2016 through 2021, look up the exact year, model, trim, and headlight package on iihs.org/ratings before the test drive, and do the test drive after dark.[5] If the car rates Marginal or Poor, get a dealer headlight aim check and fresh bulbs; mis-aiming alone can cost you shocking amounts of throw distance. If the model was sold with two headlight grades and you are looking at the cheap one, treat the better headlights as a negotiating lever, because upgrading them yourself can cost $800 to $2,000 per assembly for LED units.[4]

Honest accounting of what this piece did not prove: FARS does not encode headlight ratings, so nobody has computed fatality rates per headlight grade; the 19% and 23% figures come from police-reported crashes, which undercount glare involvement since the glare-producing car usually isn't the one that crashes.[3] IIHS tests cars as delivered by the dealer, so your neighbor's aftermarket LED swap, burnt-out bulb, or curb-kissed aim is outside the rating entirely. And the crash reductions are associations: cars with better headlights tend to be owned by people who could afford the better headlights, and wealth buys its own margin of safety.

And the full-strength case against everything above: this is mostly a story about the past. New-car buyers in 2026 face a 9% bad-headlight rate, down from 82% a decade ago.[1] The trim trap is also partly a rational outcome of real cost: LED and adaptive-beam systems add $500 to $2,000 per vehicle, and forcing every base trim to carry them raises prices for the buyers who can least afford it.[4] If you are buying new, the award system already did this work for you. If you are buying used, it did not, and nobody told you.
