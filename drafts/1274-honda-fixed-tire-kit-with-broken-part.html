Honda Fixed Your Flat-Tire Kit. The Fix Was the Broken Part.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2026

You did everything right. A recall letter arrived, you booked the dealer visit, you sat in the waiting room drinking bad coffee while a technician swapped the part, and you drove home believing your car was fixed. It was not. Honda is now recalling 4,804 Accords and CR-Vs a second time, because the replacement tire-repair kits installed under the first recall contained the same defective nozzle that recall was supposed to remove.[1]

4,804
Accords and CR-Vs recalled a second time because their recall repair contained the original defective part

Start with what sits in your trunk instead of a spare tire. Many modern Accords and CR-Vs carry a tire repair kit: a bottle of sealant and a nozzle that pumps it into a flat tire. One version of that nozzle had a one-way valve. Connect the nozzle wrong, pressure builds inside the sealant bottle, and if the relief valve fails to vent it, the bottle cap detaches with enough force to strike whoever is crouched beside the car. That is not a hypothetical failure mode. That is the defect Honda described to NHTSA in June 2026, when it recalled more than a million Accords and CR-Vs in the United States, plus 118,000 in Canada, under campaign 26V366.[2]

So how did the defective part get back into fixed cars? Through the remedy channel itself. Supplier TEK Corporation, which made the nozzles, lacked adequate change-point controls for disposing of pre-countermeasure parts, so old and corrected nozzles ended up mixed in the same warehouse stock and some old ones shipped to dealers as recall replacements.[3] A Honda dealership discovered the mix-up on July 3, 2026. Honda filed the new recall, campaign 26V663, on October 8: 97 days from dealer discovery to federal filing, during which the owners of those 4,804 cars drove around believing a completed repair meant a closed problem.

Run the numbers before reaching for outrage, because they cut both ways. Of the 4,804 vehicles, 2,745 are CR-V Hybrids, 2,058 are Accord Hybrids, and exactly one is a CR-V Fuel Cell EV. Honda estimates about 1% of the population actually carries the defect, which works out to roughly 48 vehicles expected to have a bad nozzle. No injuries have been reported in either campaign. By recall standards, this is small, and the system did catch it: a dealer noticed, an investigation followed, a filing landed.

But small is not the same as unimportant, because the recall remedy is the one safety intervention a consumer cannot second-guess. You can inspect your own tires. You cannot inspect a replacement nozzle sealed inside a kit, and you have no reason to suspect the part a dealer just installed under a federal recall. When the failure happens inside the remedy channel, it lands differently than a failure in the original part: it breaks the one promise the system makes without qualification, which is that the fix is verified by construction. Credit where due, though. This time the remedy deletes the failure mode instead of managing it. A replacement nozzle without a one-way valve cannot build pressure the way the old one did, because the chain loses a required link. That is a better fix than the first one was.

What this does not prove matters too. Honda's 1% defect estimate comes from Honda's own manufacturing records, not from independent inspection of the 4,804 kits, so the true count of bad nozzles could be higher or lower. A filing that reports no injuries is not proof that no one was ever hurt outside the filing, and the documents reviewed do not say how many complaints or warranty claims sat behind the July 3 discovery. Nor does the risk apply to every defective kit: the projectile outcome needs a specific chain, a misconnected nozzle plus a relief valve that fails, and a correctly used kit may never produce it.

Here is what to do. If you own a 2023-2026 Accord Hybrid, a 2023-2026 CR-V Hybrid, or a 2025 CR-V Fuel Cell EV that already had the tire-kit recall done, check your VIN again at nhtsa.gov/recalls or recalls.honda.com. VINs for this campaign became searchable October 17, and owner letters go out November 16, 2026.[1] A completed recall is not a closed recall. And if you get a flat before your dealer visit, point the sealant bottle away from yourself and everyone else while you use it. You already trusted the fix once.
