Jeep Recalled the Same Coil Spring Three Times. On the Third Try, It Stopped Trusting Its Own Mechanics.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

Let's talk about the coil spring. It sits on a rubber isolator on the spring seat, quietly holding up roughly a quarter of your SUV, and nobody thinks about it until it leaves the vehicle at speed. FCA now thinks about it constantly: 328,381 Jeep Grand Cherokees are being recalled because the rear coil springs can walk off their seats and detach while driving.[1] This is the third federal recall for the exact same defect.[2]

The genealogy is the whole story. June 2023: recall 23V413, roughly 345,000 vehicles, dealers told to inspect the rear spring assembly and repair where necessary. Twenty minutes to inspect, up to four hours to fix.[5] January 2026: recall 26V051, and the defect description is the remarkable document. The springs were not correctly installed during the repair performed under the first recall, or the first recall was never completed. Eighty thousand vehicles recalled for the failure of the repair of the repair.[6] Now September 2026: recall 26V562, replacing both prior campaigns outright, and here's the engineering tell. FCA no longer wants an inspection; it wants both lower rear coil-spring isolators replaced on every affected vehicle, with no judgment call from the technician.[4] A visual inspection you could do in twenty minutes failed twice. That is not a parts problem. It is a process problem.

Which brings us to the paradox that makes this vehicle so interesting to me. The Grand Cherokee's FARS fatality rate is 0.51 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles, against a Ford Explorer at 1.54, a Tahoe at 2.49, even the little Cherokee at 1.73.[7] This is one of the safest SUVs in America by the only scoreboard that counts: it does not kill its occupants in crashes. It just sheds load-bearing hardware onto the freeway. Great crashworthiness, questionable assembly quality control, and the two have almost nothing to do with each other. An SUV can be superb at protecting you in a collision and still need three recalls because a rubber pad was installed wrong at the factory. Design and manufacturing are different disciplines, and only one of them failed here.

So what do you do if you own one? If you drive a 2021-2023 Grand Cherokee, Grand Cherokee L, or 4xe without air suspension, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls; the campaign became searchable September 9.[8] Already had the earlier spring recall done? Go back anyway. Your vehicle is included even if a dealer "fixed" it in 2023 or 2026; the new remedy is physically different, isolators out, isolators in, and it is free.[1] Interim letters go out September 29 through October 7, so do not wait for the mail if the rear end starts clunking over bumps. FCA customer service is 1-800-853-1403 if you want to confirm coverage.

Two honest caveats before anyone shares this. First, nobody has been hurt: zero known accidents or injuries across all three campaigns, and Stellantis estimates only 0.1% of the 328,381 vehicles actually carry the defect, which works out to roughly 328 cars. That means around 328,000 dealer visits to fix 328 vehicles, and for nearly every owner the cost is an afternoon, not their safety. Second, my FARS rate comparison leans on 2014-2023 data dominated by the older WK2 generation; the recalled WL generation is barely represented in that window. The 0.51 rate describes the Grand Cherokee family, not precisely the vehicles on the recall list.

And the steelman against this whole article: maybe this is regulatory theater. Zero injuries, a 0.1% defect rate, a vehicle with a genuinely good fatality record, and a remedy that reads like the cheapest way to close an open file. FCA replaced the isolators everywhere because proving which ones were bad cost more than replacing all of them. That is a perfectly rational corporate decision, and a reader could fairly conclude the saga is paperwork wearing a safety costume. My answer: sure, but a twenty-minute inspection failed twice, and the paperwork reveals something real. The fix for a bad inspection regime is not a better checklist. It is a part swap nobody can do wrong.
