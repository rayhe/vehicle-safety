Dallas County’s Deaths Didn’t Budge. Its Neighbor’s Jumped 61%.

Axle McScatter • Data Visualization Editor
November 8, 2026

Dallas County, Texas logged 35,975 crashes in the first nine months of 2026, seventy more than the same stretch of 2025.[1] Deaths went from 211 to 212: one more body. Same roads, same drivers, essentially the same number of crashes, and the same number of people died. Next door in Denton County, deaths jumped 61 percent on the same database, in the same nine months.[3] Dallas is the county that didn’t move.

5.89
Deaths per 1,000 crashes in Dallas County, Jan–Sep 2026 — identical to 2025’s 5.88, while Denton County’s lethality jumped 60% (TxDOT CRIS)

Divide deaths by crashes and the flat line gets stranger. Dallas County recorded 5.88 deaths per 1,000 crashes in 2025, and 5.89 in 2026, unchanged to two decimal places. Denton’s lethality went from 3.09 to 4.96 over the same nine months, a 60 percent rise. Two counties in the same metro, measured the same way from the same database, and one county’s crashes got three-fifths more lethal while the other’s didn’t budge.

Injuries tell a different story than deaths, and they moved the other way. People injured rose from 17,202 to 17,887, up 4 percent, on 70 additional crashes.[1] Injuries per crash rose from 0.479 to 0.497, so each crash injured slightly more people than the year before. Suspected serious injuries actually fell, 1,246 to 1,204. Dallas County crashes in 2026 injured more people, seriously injured fewer of them, and killed the same number: three severity measures moving in three different directions.

Inside the flat total, two opposite stories cancel out. Commercial-vehicle deaths fell from 27 to 18, down a third, on 227 fewer truck crashes.[1] Passenger-vehicle deaths rose from 184 to 194. Trucks got safer in Dallas County while the state’s truck deaths rose 8.1 percent, 406 to 439.[6] The county’s flat death toll is a truck improvement minus a passenger-vehicle deterioration, and the two just happened to net out at plus one.

Put all four DFW counties on one table and Dallas looks less like the steady one and more like the one that was already worst. Denton went from 31 to 50, Tarrant from 151 to 142, Collin from 52 to 49, and Dallas from 211 to 212.[3][4][5] Combined, the four counties went from 445 deaths to 453, up less than 2 percent. But divide each county’s deaths by its crashes and Dallas leads the group at 5.89 per 1,000, ahead of Tarrant’s 5.71, Denton’s 4.96, and Collin’s 4.03. Dallas didn’t get worse, and it didn’t need to, because its crashes were already the most likely to kill.

Quarter by quarter, the danger peaked late: January through March logged 11,565 crashes and 64 deaths, the lightest totals of the year, while April through June had the most of everything at 12,453 crashes and 75 deaths. July through September eased off the second quarter on every measure yet still beat the first by 9 deaths and 661 injuries. Per crash, the third quarter was the most lethal of the three at 6.11 deaths per 1,000, ahead of the second’s 6.02 and the first’s 5.53. Denton’s most lethal quarter was the first, at 6.32 deaths per 1,000 crashes, and Dallas’s was the third, at 6.11, and two counties in the same metro don’t even agree on when the roads are at their worst.

Give the flat-line reading its full weight, because 212 deaths is a terrible number and flat is not good. A county that buries one person every 1.3 days has a chronic problem, not a solved one. The national death rate kept falling in 2026 while Dallas stood still, which is its own kind of failure.[7][8] Small numbers cut the other way too: a single bad weekend can move a county’s quarterly totals, and late-filed reports can still revise these counts upward. But the lethality figure is built on 35,975 crashes, a base large enough that 5.88 to 5.89 is a real flat line, not a rounding trick. Stability is the story, and it is not a compliment.

What you can do with this starts on I-35E, which logged 2,656 crashes in nine months, followed by I-635 at 2,283 and I-30 at 2,087. Grand Prairie’s crashes jumped 11.7 percent while Carrollton’s fell 11.6 percent, so the risk is moving around the county, not shrinking. And the pattern is a severity problem, which means the usual levers are the ones that matter: belts on, speed down, sober behind the wheel. In a county where crashes stayed flat and injuries rose 4 percent, survival is decided after the crash starts, not before. Texas crash data is public through the TxDOT CRIS portal.[9]
