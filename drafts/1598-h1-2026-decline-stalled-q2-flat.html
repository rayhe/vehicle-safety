By The Numbers

The Death Rate Hit a Near-Record Low. Then the Second Quarter Went Flat.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

NHTSA released its early estimates for the first half of 2026 on November 10, and the headline is the kind that gets quoted in press releases.[1] An estimated 16,750 people died in motor vehicle crashes from January through June, down 2.5 percent from the same period in 2025, while Americans drove 0.8 percent more miles.[1] Fatality rate fell to 1.02 deaths per 100 million miles traveled, the second-lowest first-half rate ever recorded, behind only the 1.01 of 2014.[1] Read one quarter further and the celebration curdles: the first quarter fell 5.0 percent, the second quarter fell 0.2 percent, and the second-quarter rate did not move at all.

433
Deaths the second quarter would have avoided if it had matched the first quarter’s 5.0% pace, a labeled estimate from simple arithmetic on NHTSA’s published tables.

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they did not get better. First quarter: 7,715 deaths against 8,120 a year earlier, a 5.0 percent drop, with the quarterly rate falling from 1.05 to 0.99.[1] Second quarter: 9,035 deaths against 9,055, which is 20 fewer deaths on a national scale, a rounding error wearing a press release, and the quarterly rate sat exactly where it was a year ago, 1.05 against 1.05.[1] Every point of the first half’s rate improvement came from January through March, and April through June contributed nothing.

Month by month, the stall has a shape. January fell 8.4 percent, February 4.5, March 2.4, April 5.3.[1] Then May rose 1.5 percent and June rose 3.0 percent, the only two months of the half that went the wrong way, and they are the two months when summer driving starts.[1] May added 45 deaths against May 2025, June added 90, small numbers in absolute terms and the wrong direction at exactly the moment the roads fill up. Four months of improvement followed by two months of reversal is not a trend yet. It is a warning light, and warning lights are my whole beat.

Two pieces of arithmetic put a price on both halves of the story. Americans drove roughly 1.642 trillion miles in the first half, implied by the death count and the 1.02 rate, and at last year’s 1.05 rate those same miles would have produced about 17,243 deaths, so the rate improvement is worth roughly 493 fewer deaths.[1] That is the dividend. Now the stall: the second quarter of 2025 was 9,055 deaths, and a 5.0 percent cut, matching the first quarter’s pace, would have put the second quarter of 2026 at about 8,602 instead of the actual 9,035, a gap of roughly 433 deaths that the first quarter’s pace would have saved and the second quarter did not.[1] Both figures are labeled estimates, simple multiplication on NHTSA’s published tables, not models, and both are the kind of number that survives a second look because there is nothing to hide behind.

State by state, the national decline turns out to be a knife fight. Thirty-five states plus Puerto Rico improved; fifteen states plus the District of Columbia got worse.[1] Illinois alone accounts for 27.3 percent of the entire national decline, falling from 569 projected deaths to 453, a 20.4 percent drop that no other state comes close to matching.[1] Add Tennessee (down 50), Arizona (down 44), Michigan (down 43), and Oklahoma (down 42), and the five biggest improvers sum to 295 fewer deaths, while the five biggest worseners, Georgia (up 60), Pennsylvania (up 57), Kentucky (up 52), North Carolina (up 50), and Ohio (up 37), sum to 256 more.[1] Net of the ten loudest states: 39 fewer deaths. The other 41 jurisdictions delivered the other 386. California, the biggest state in the table, went the wrong way too, up 33 deaths to 1,825, while Illinois carried the headline almost single-handed.

Before anyone prints a percentage, look at the denominator. The District of Columbia posts the scariest number in the whole table, up 128.6 percent, and it is seven deaths becoming sixteen.[1] Nine people. Delaware’s 28.3 percent jump is 46 deaths becoming 59.[1] Percentages lie when the counts are small, which is why the honest version of this story names the counts first and the percentages second, and why West Virginia’s 27.8 percent improvement (133 down to 96) and Hawaii’s 32.4 percent (68 down to 46) deserve the same skepticism in the other direction.[1]

Fairness requires the other side, and it is not small. These are statistical projections, not counted bodies, built from police reports, early notification data, and monthly fatality counts through a time-series regression, and NHTSA says plainly that the 2025 and 2026 figures will be revised when the FARS Annual Reporting File for 2025 arrives.[1] State estimates are modeled shares of regional projections, not state counts, which makes small-state swings noisy by construction. A competing series, the National Safety Council, puts first-half 2026 at 18,460 deaths, roughly 10 percent higher than NHTSA’s 16,750, because it counts a broader set of deaths, and both series agree on direction while disagreeing on level.[4] Nothing in this release breaks the numbers down by crash type, so it cannot confirm or deny whether the pedestrian and cyclist backslide of recent years continued, and nothing in it attributes the decline to any policy, enforcement program, or vehicle technology, including the Pathways to Safer Streets initiative NHTSA’s press release mentions.[2]

Now the counterargument, at full strength: 2.5 percent on a projection is noise, a rounding artifact with a press release attached, and a flat second quarter proves it. There is a real point inside that, because projections get revised and two bad months do not make a trend. But the rate has now fallen across six straight half-year readings from the pandemic peak, 1.33 in the first half of 2021 down to 1.02, and the death count is 16.5 percent below that peak’s 20,055.[1] Six readings in the same direction is not one noisy quarter. The honest version of the objection is narrower and harder to dismiss: the improvement is real, and the pace of it is stalling, and the second quarter is the first piece of evidence that the easy gains may be spent.

What to do with this, practically, starts with geography, because if you live in Georgia, Pennsylvania, Kentucky, North Carolina, or Ohio, your state went the wrong way while the country improved, and that is a question for your state DOT, not a shrug. Watch the nine-months estimate NHTSA releases in late December, because the summer driving season is the test this half-year report cannot run, and a flat third quarter would confirm the stall instead of merely suggesting it.[1] None of this changes what a driver should do on any given trip, because the rate fell for reasons this report does not apportion between enforcement, vehicle tech, and luck, and no single road got safer on its own. What changed is the shape of the good news: steep in the winter, flat by June, and now waiting on summer to tell us which one was real.
