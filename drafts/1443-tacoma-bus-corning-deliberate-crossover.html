The Sheriff Says She Crossed the Line on Purpose. The Lab Says She Was High. The Bus Burned Anyway.

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
November 9, 2026

Six people died on Highway 99W outside Corning, California, on the morning of January 14, 2026.[1] A Toyota Tacoma crossed a broken yellow line into a transit bus, the bus burned, and that was the story for nine months. Then the sheriff’s office rewrote it: the crossing was deliberate, the pickup driver’s blood carried 3,103 nanograms of methamphetamine per milliliter, and five death certificates are being changed to homicide while hers is changed to suicide.[4]

3,103
Nanograms of methamphetamine per milliliter in the pickup driver’s cavity blood. Medicinal use runs 20 to 50. Recreational use tops out around 2,500. (NTSB Human Performance report)

Everyone who knew Christina Sue Whittemore believed she had been sober since 2010. Her husband told investigators she had a substance abuse problem before but had been clean for sixteen years. Her daughter called her a recovering methamphetamine addict who stopped about twelve years ago. Both were describing the same woman the lab was describing, but the lab had better evidence. Medicinal methamphetamine in a living person runs 20 to 50 nanograms per milliliter. Anything above 200 is supratherapeutic. Recreational use tops out around 2,500. Whittemore’s cavity blood read 3,103, above the ceiling of the recreational range, and even allowing for postmortem redistribution, which the NTSB report flags as a real caveat, that number does not come down to anything resembling sober.[2]

Notebooks survived in the Tacoma. Investigators found pages describing marital trouble, money trouble, her husband’s drug use, and previous thoughts of suicide. Her last phone call, with her husband, ended at 9:57 a.m., seven minutes before impact. She left home at 9:44, reached the Gyle Road intersection at 10:03, and crossed the line at 10:04. Carrier records show no active phone use at the moment of the crash. Her daughter believed the Tacoma’s lane departure and collision warnings were switched on, but a warning system cannot stop a decision.[2]

Meanwhile the bus driver was looking the other way. Kelly Margaret Langstaff, 50, had driven the Glenn-Tehama Connection since June, a circular route with regular passengers. The surviving passenger told investigators she was angry at a man sleeping across the first-row seats, kept turning around to look at him, and tapped the brakes to wake him. The last tap came on a bridge half a mile from the crash site. Her friend shouted “look out” a second before impact. Langstaff answered “What?” The bus had no driver-assist features, though none of that may have mattered. At a combined closing speed near 110 miles per hour, the time between the Tacoma crossing the line and impact would have been measured in seconds, and no realistic reaction avoids a head-on at that speed.[2]

What killed the passengers was the fire. Every person aboard the bus had burns over 100 percent of their body. A belted passenger in row three died of chemical asphyxiation and smoke inhalation. The fuel line separated in the collision and gasoline poured onto the exhaust manifold. A dash camera on a truck behind the pickup caught the timeline: a window opened at 84 seconds, the only survivor dropped to the ground, black smoke at 2:14, flames on the roof at 2:43, a passerby dragging the survivor clear at 3:05. Fire trucks were not visible until 9:32, and suppression started at 11:37. One person got out while four did not, and they had about ninety seconds.[3]

And the Tacoma itself is not the story. At 0.80 deaths per 100 million miles, it is one of the safest pickups in a decade of FARS data, safer than the Silverado at 1.25 and the F-150 at 1.04. Its belts held, its airbags fired, its event recorder captured the crash. Head-on collisions are only 14 percent of American traffic deaths, but three-quarters of them happen on rural roads and three-quarters on undivided two-lane highways, exactly the road this bus was on. Paint and reflectors were the only thing between the lanes. Centerline rumble strips are the proven treatment for exactly this failure, and this stretch did not have them.[5][6]

Two cautions before this story earns full trust. The NTSB has not issued a probable cause, and the sheriff’s finding of intent is a law-enforcement conclusion, not the board’s. Methamphetamine at high doses scrambles cognition and perception, and a measured level cannot say whether the driver was in the early or late phase of its effects. A drug-impaired drift across a centerline is also consistent with the physical evidence. The honest version is that the crossing was either a decision or a drug, and the sheriff has chosen one.[2]

What to do with this depends on where you sit. If your county runs cutaway buses on two-lane highways, ask about rumble strips and ask whether the buses have anything more than lap belts. If you ride one, learn where the emergency windows are before the bus moves. The only survivor went out a side window in 84 seconds. If someone you love drives after addiction, the check that matters is a test, not a timeline. Sixteen years of believed sobriety ended in a lab result nobody in her family saw coming. And if you drive a 2021 Tacoma, the truck is fine, so check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls anyway.
