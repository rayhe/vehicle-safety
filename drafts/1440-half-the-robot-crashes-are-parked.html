NHTSA's Robotaxi Crash Database Just Set a Record. Half the Cars in It Were Parked.

Axle McScatter • Data Visualization Editor
November 8, 2026

March 2026 was the worst month ever for robotaxi crashes in the federal database. NHTSA's Standing General Order file logged 145 automated-driving-system incidents in a single month, the highest total since reporting began in July 2021, and the quarterly count kept climbing after that, 355 in the first quarter of 2026 and 388 in the second.[2] Read the file one column further and the record falls apart. Half the robotaxis in it were not moving when they got hit.

50.4%
Share of the 1,538 ADS incidents in NHTSA's Sept 15, 2026 release in which the robotaxi was stopped or parked; 51.0% were at exactly 0 mph

I ran the numbers on the full release, 1,538 distinct incidents from June 2025 through August 2026, and the speed column reads like a parking-lot log. Companies described the ADS vehicle as stopped or parked in 775 incidents, and 785 of them were traveling at exactly 0 mph.[2] Another 381 were rolling at 1 to 9 mph, which puts 1,166 of 1,538, or 75.8 percent, under 10 mph. Only 20 incidents in the entire file involved a robotaxi going 45 mph or faster, a share of 1.3 percent, and 127 of the crashes happened in parking lots.[2] Airbags deployed inside the ADS vehicle itself in just 29 of the 1,538 cases, which is what you would expect from a dataset where the most common event is a human driver tapping a stationary car.[2]

Meanwhile the rate runs the other way, and not by a little. Waymo reported 69 crashes in Los Angeles County in 2024, 219 in 2025, and 186 so far in 2026, a count that roughly tripled while its crash rate per million miles fell from about 57 in 2023 to 13.7 in 2024, 6.7 in 2025, and below 6 so far in 2026.[3] Statewide in California the count nearly doubled, 304 in 2024 to more than 590 in 2025, over the same stretch when the rate fell by an order of magnitude.[3] Waymo says that in 40 percent of the vehicle-to-vehicle and single-vehicle collisions it reported in Los Angeles through June 2026, the change in velocity was under 1 mph, which the company says typically means dents or scratches.[3] An independent check agrees with the direction: IIHS found Waymo's driverless vehicles involved in 68 percent fewer crashes per mile than human drivers across four cities, and 71 percent fewer in Los Angeles specifically.[3]

Fatalities tell the same story in miniature. Only 154 of the 1,538 incidents alleged any injury at all, and just two were fatal, a rate of 0.13 percent.[2] In both fatal cases the robotaxi was a bystander. Near Tempe in September 2025, a Waymo slowed to turn right while yielding to a pedestrian, a motorcycle clipped its rear-left corner at 8 mph, and the rider fell into the road where a passing car struck and killed him.[2] In Dallas in August 2026, a Waymo slowed for a pedestrian in the opposite lanes, an oncoming SUV hit the pedestrian, and the pedestrian struck the Waymo's rear-left area while it moved at about 5 mph.[2] Across the entire five-year file, from July 2021 through September 2026, the total ADS death count stands at three.[4]

Give the finding its proper discount before believing it, because the stopped-or-parked share does not prove the robots are innocent. A robotaxi that brakes hard for a hazard a human driver would have rolled past still created the conflict, and "stopped" includes vehicles stopped in live traffic lanes where a person would have kept moving. Waymo's per-mile improvement is real but measured in geofenced, fair-weather, exhaustively mapped territory, and the sub-1-mph delta-v figure is Waymo's characterization of its own crashes. NHTSA itself warns the file has no miles-driven denominator, that a company with better telemetry will report more crashes simply because it sees more of them, and that the June 2025 rule change added a property-damage threshold, which means the 2025-26 monthly series is not directly comparable to the years before it.[1] Waymo is also 82.8 percent of the file, so file-wide statistics are mostly Waymo statistics with a long tail of rounding errors attached.

What to do with a database like this depends on which number you are shown. If a headline tells you robotaxi crashes hit a record, ask for the denominator before believing it, because raw counts in this file track fleet size, not danger, and NHTSA says so on its own page. Judge operators by crashes per million miles, and demand the same per-mile number from every company before ranking them, since the file gives you no way to compute one yourself. If you drive near robotaxis, the most common "crash" in the federal file is a human driver hitting a stopped or slow-moving robotaxi, often from behind, so the scenario to watch for is the robot braking for something you cannot see, not the robot speeding. And anybody comparing 2024 robotaxi crash counts to 2026 counts without mentioning the June 2025 reporting change is either sloppy or selling something, because the threshold for what counts as a reportable fender-bender changed in between.
