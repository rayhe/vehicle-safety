The Gap

GM Recalled 29,347 Cadillacs Because a Key Fob Can Close the Window. Nobody Complained.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

Thirteen millimeters, roughly the width of your thumb. On 29,347 Cadillac Optiqs, that is the exact distance during which the window forgets it is supposed to feel your fingers.[1]

13 mm
Final travel where the anti-pinch logic failed on key-fob express-up

But here is the "how": it only happens when you close the window with the express-up button on the key fob, from across the parking lot, not looking at the car.[2]

Meanwhile the remote-close feature that sold you the fob is now the hazard, and the one safety system that exists for this exact moment was asleep.[3]

Inside that door sits the body control module, the little computer that runs the windows, which simply never applied its obstruction-detection logic to the key-fob express-up path. Press the switch inside the door and the anti-pinch works. Press the fob from ten feet away and it does not.[4] Somebody at GM coded two code paths, tested one, and shipped both.

And yet, remarkably, zero complaints. GM told the agency it was not aware of a single one.[1] No injuries, no angry forum threads, no viral video. This is a pure noncompliance recall, filed because the windows do not meet FMVSS 118, the federal standard for power-operated window systems, which has required stop-and-reverse behavior on closing windows for decades.[5]

Now, the contrarian reading of this is obvious and I will state it at full strength: if nobody complained and nobody was hurt, the system caught a paperwork-grade defect and that is how regulation is supposed to work. Remote-close is an opt-in convenience feature, so until the patch lands, do not use it and the problem solves itself. A software update fixes it, many cars can take it over the air, and the whole thing costs owners nothing but a few minutes.

Fine. But the thirteen millimeters keep me up at night. The standard was written around a physical obstruction test performed from inside the car, with a person at the switch, and the remote-close path sailed right past the assumptions the standard was built on. That is how a modern car ends up violating a rule written before key fobs existed: not by engineering failure, by interface sprawl. The car learned a new trick that the safety check never followed.

What to do with this

Own a 2025, 2026, or 2027 Cadillac Optiq, built between July 2024 and September 2026? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. The recall is NHTSA campaign 26V612; GM calls it N262567230. The remedy is a free body-control-module software calibration, available over the air if your car accepts wireless updates, or at any Cadillac dealer. GM started building the fix into production on September 22; owner notification letters go out November 9.[4] Until it is patched, use the switches inside the doors, not the fob.

What this does not prove: the absence of complaints is not proof that nobody was pinched. FARS captures only fatal crashes, so a window-pinch story leaves no statistical footprint anywhere; this article is built on recall documents, not crash data. How often owners actually use remote express-up is unknown, so the real exposure could be large or tiny. And this is one model's software bug, not evidence that remote-close features are broadly broken across the industry.
