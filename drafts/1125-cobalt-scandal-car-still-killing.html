GM Paid $900 Million for the Ignition Switch. The Cobalt Kept Killing.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. Between 2014 and 2023, the Chevrolet Cobalt killed 1,540 Americans, an average of 154 a year, in a car General Motors stopped building in 2010.[1] You remember the Cobalt, the car with the 57-cent ignition switch.

5.1
deaths per 100 million miles, the sixth-worst fatality rate of 337 models in FARS, roughly 4.4 times the national average

February 2014: GM recalled some 800,000 Cobalts and Pontiac G5s after admitting the ignition switch could kill the engine mid-drive and take the airbags with it.[2] Engineers had known about the defect since 2005.[2] What followed was the largest auto scandal of the decade: roughly 30 million cars recalled worldwide, a $900 million forfeiture to the Justice Department, and a compensation fund that paid out for 124 deaths, most of them drivers under 25.[2] The switch got fixed, the headlines moved on, and the Cobalt did not stop killing people.

FARS, the federal database that counts every traffic death in America, logged those 1,540 Cobalt deaths across 1,907 crashes in the decade starting 2014.[1] On a per-mile basis, only five nameplates rank worse: the Veloster, the Tracker, the Land Cruiser, the Mustang, and the Maxima.[1] That is the company the Cobalt keeps now. And the timeline is the tell, because the recall happened in 2014, at the very start of that window, which means nearly all of these deaths came after the fix.

328
deaths from the 2006 model year alone, the deadliest single model year and also the Cobalt's peak sales year

Nobody is saying the ignition switch is still killing people; GM replaced those switches. Of the 1,540 deaths, 328 came from the 2006 model year, and 2006 was also the Cobalt's peak sales year, so part of this is pure arithmetic: more cars, more crashes.[1] But the rest is sociology. Back when it was new, the Cobalt was one of the cheapest cars in America, and now it is a $2,000 Craigslist special, which means the surviving fleet is driven disproportionately by the young and the broke. FARS toxicology data shows 22.4 percent of drivers in fatal Cobalt crashes were impaired, above the 20 percent baseline.[1] That is not a defect; that is the beater effect, and the Cobalt is its valedictorian.

Scandal victims skewed overwhelmingly under 25, and the Cobalt's secondhand victims are the same demographic: a teenager's first car is a $2,000 Cobalt because a $2,000 Cobalt is what exists.[2] If you are shopping the bottom of the used market for a new driver, the data has an opinion. The Corolla and the Civic each kill at less than half the Cobalt's rate per mile, so spend the same two grand on one of those instead.[1] And if you are still driving a Cobalt yourself, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, keep the keychain light, and understand that the switch was never the whole story.[3]

Now the honest counterargument: selection bias. Surviving Cobalts are driven by the highest-risk drivers on the road, and the FARS rate uses estimated miles rather than odometer readings, which adds roughly 15 percent of slop for old, low-volume cars.[1] None of that changes the ranking, because sixth-worst is sixth-worst. GM spent $900 million admitting the Cobalt was a death trap, and sixteen years after the assembly line stopped, the car is still auditioning for the title.
