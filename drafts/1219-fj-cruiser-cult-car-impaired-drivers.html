The FJ Cruiser Earned Top Crash-Test Scores. One in Four of Its Drivers Was Impaired.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 20, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are 265 of them for this one vehicle, the Toyota FJ Cruiser attracts drivers who treat the drive home as an extreme sport. Of FJ drivers in fatal crashes between 2014 and 2023, 25.3% tested positive for alcohol or drugs, landing this beloved retro off-roader 16th out of 307 models in the federal impairment standings, shoulder to shoulder with the Corvette.[1]

25.3%
Share of FJ Cruiser drivers in fatal crashes testing positive for any impairment, vs. 20.0% dataset average

Nobody expected this from a Toyota. The FJ Cruiser is a reliability icon, a cult object that peaked at $80,000 for pristine examples in 2022, and a rig the IIHS rated Good in frontal and side crash tests.[2][3] It is the vehicular equivalent of a golden retriever. Yet its fatal-crash drivers are impaired at rates 26% above the national average: alcohol alone at 19.6%, drugs at 10.9%. Compare the family. The RAV4 sits at 18.4%, the Highlander at 16.4%, and the Land Cruiser, the FJ's own ancestor, at a monastic 8.9%. Something about the retro bodywork selects for a wilder clientele.

0.43
FJ Cruiser fatality rate per 100M VMT, rank 165 of 286: dead average, despite 16th-worst drivers

Here is where the numbers get genuinely strange. For all that driver misbehavior, the FJ's fatality rate is 0.43 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles, a perfectly average 165th out of 286 models. The machine keeps its occupants alive at an average clip while being piloted by a cohort that ranks 16th-worst in sobriety. That gap between driver behavior and vehicle outcomes is the real story. Toyota's crash engineering is quietly compensating for a driver population that behaves like it owns a sports car.

Now the counterargument, stated at full strength, and it deserves the space. A 265-driver toxicology sample is moderate, and FARS only records fatal crashes, so this is not a census of FJ drivers but a census of FJ drivers already having the worst day of their lives. The vehicle's adventure positioning means more campsite-adjacent driving, where alcohol is simply more present, and FJs are often weekend toys driven hard by enthusiasts rather than daily commuters. Maybe the FJ doesn't attract reckless people so much as it attracts people who use it recklessly.

What the data cannot tell us matters too. FARS toxicology screens are a subset of fatal crashes, not universal testing, and a positive screen does not prove impairment caused the crash. Our rate calculations use VMT estimates rather than odometer readings, which introduces real uncertainty for a low-volume, often-garaged vehicle like the FJ. Treat the rankings as signal, not verdict.

Shopping the 2026 used-FJ market, where clean examples have finally slid back to $15,000–$35,000? Buy the vehicle, not the myth.[4] Its crash-test scores and reliability record are real, but its clean safety numbers are the engineering's achievement, not the community's. Check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before handing over cash, inspect for the rust that kills old FJs faster than any crash, and remember that no ladder frame compensates for a drunk driver, yours or anyone else's.[5]
