## Kill test: PROCEED
Daylight saving ends Sunday, November 1, 2026 — 19 days out. The site has covered deer collisions at fall-back (#829), Halloween kid-pedestrian deaths (#1096), the 2024 pedestrian divergence (#1155), and IIHS headlight ratings (#963) — but never the IIHS daylight-saving study itself, and never its most unsettling frame. Queue greps for "daylight saving," "fall back," "time change" return only #829 (deer). The novel angle: the clock change is a *fatality transfer* — the same hour of darkness that saves vehicle occupants (+(-)7.1% occupant deaths) kills pedestrians and cyclists (+13%). Nobody in the queue framed it as one mechanism moving deaths from inside the car to outside it.

## Primary sources (verified Oct 13, 2026)
1. **IIHS study: Woods, Weast & Monfort, "Time changes affect traffic deaths," Journal of Safety Research, July 2025** — FARS 2010-2019, five weeks before/after each time change, targeting hours whose light levels changed. Fall-back: fatal pedestrian/cyclist crashes +13%, motor-vehicle-occupant fatal crashes -7.1%. Spring-forward reverses the pattern. Net over both changes (2010-2019): 29 more occupant deaths, 26 fewer pedestrian/cyclist deaths. Reported via IIHS release mirrors: https://www.fi-magazine.com/news/time-changes-affect-traffic-deaths and https://www.autodealertodaymagazine.com/news/time-changes-affect-traffic-deaths. Parent: https://www.iihs.org/topics/fatality-statistics.
2. **NHTSA Administrator/Harkey testimony context — David Harkey, written testimony to U.S. Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation, April 10, 2025** (daylight-saving hearing): less than a quarter of trips begin at night, but nearly half of motor-vehicle-occupant deaths and 77% of pedestrian deaths occur in the dark. "Whatever you decide to do about the clock, I hope you will also consider actions to keep pedestrians and bicyclists safe, especially after sundown." Via municipal summary: https://cms8.revize.com/revize/franklincountyny/Document_Center/Public Safety/Traffic Safety Board/Did You Know/2025/Twice Yearly Time Change Not Major Factor in Traffic Safety.pdf?t=202505111119280&t=202505111119280
3. **Sullivan & Flannagan (UMTRI), DST-transition natural experiment** — comparing fatal crashes at the same clock hours under different ambient light: pedestrian scenarios showed nighttime fatal-crash risk as high as 7x daytime risk. Summarized: https://www.workerscompensation.com/daily-headlines/daylight-saving-time-and-workplace-injuries/ (also restates the Woods +13%/-7.1% finding).
4. **NHTSA FARS 2024 (this site's #1155 research):** 7,080 pedestrians killed (-3.9% vs 2023), 71,635 injured (+5%); a pedestrian killed every 74 minutes; 76% of pedestrian deaths in the dark; alcohol (BAC .01+, driver and/or pedestrian) in 43% of fatal pedestrian crashes.
5. **Clock fact:** U.S. daylight saving ends 2:00 a.m. Sunday, November 1, 2026 (first Sunday of November; Arizona and most of Hawaii do not observe). Verified via TZ=America/Los_Angeles date.

## Verified facts
- DST ends Nov 1, 2026. The 5-7 p.m. weekday commute window flips from civil twilight to full dark overnight; work/school schedules do not move — the routine stays, the environment changes.
- The effect is asymmetric by design: evening light is removed, morning light is added. Pedestrian exposure concentrates in the evening (after-school activities, evening commute, dog walks); driver exposure is spread across the day.
- IIHS/Harkey themselves hedge the overall magnitude: "the time switch has little net effect on overall traffic fatalities" and "the twice-yearly time change is not a major factor in America's ongoing road safety." The pedestrian slice is where it bites.
- Absolute stakes: 7,080 pedestrian deaths (2024); +13% on the ~5-week post-change window is roughly dozens of extra deaths — small against 7,080, catastrophic if it's your kid crossing at 5:45 p.m.

## Original contribution (this article's novel findings)
1. **The transfer frame:** one policy lever (the clock) simultaneously cuts occupant deaths (-7.1%) and raises pedestrian/cyclist deaths (+13%). Framed as a transfer, not a wash: the car protects its own better when the sun is down early, because fewer driver-error fatal crashes happen in the dark? No — the honest mechanism per IIHS: occupant deaths fall because darkness suppresses driving exposure (people drive less / differently); pedestrians keep walking the same routes and get hit in the dark. The protection of the cabin and the vulnerability of the sidewalk diverge.
2. **The commute-window arithmetic:** the evening 5-7 p.m. window carries peak pedestrian-vehicle mixing (after-school, commute, errands) and flips from marginal visibility to zero-visibility overnight. No infrastructure adapts; no speed limit changes; the same crosswalk, the same hour, seven times the risk (Sullivan & Flannagan upper bound).
3. **Compounding with #963's finding:** one in three cars on the road has headlights IIHS rates Poor or Marginal. Darkness + a poor-headlight fleet + a dark commute = the fall-back multiplies a pre-existing defect.

## Limitations (must state in article)
- IIHS study covers 2010-2019; pedestrian deaths have surged ~80% since 2009, so the +13% share may not map linearly to 2026 volumes.
- Arizona and Hawaii don't observe DST; their November pedestrian data dilutes the national effect (and Arizona's high pedestrian death rate is a reminder darkness matters year-round).
- Effect sizes are relative (+13% of a 5-week window), not apocalyptic absolutes; the annual pedestrian total is dominated by year-round darkness exposure (76-77% dark), not the one-week change.
- Causation is ambient-light association; sleep disruption and behavior confound the occupant side (IIHS's own theory).

## Strongest counterargument (must state at full strength)
IIHS and Harkey say it plainly: the time change is "not a major factor" in overall road safety — the net is roughly a wash, and darkness exists every November regardless of what the clock says. Permanent DST would just move the dark to the morning school run instead of the evening commute. The honest enemy is not the clock; it's that three-quarters of pedestrian deaths happen in the dark year-round, headlights are often poor, and crosswalks are under-lit. Blaming November 1 risks mistaking the trigger for the disease.

## Actionable insights
- Drivers: the week after Nov 1, drive the 5-7 p.m. window like it's 9 p.m. — slow 5 mph on arterials, high beams where legal, and assume every crosswalk is occupied. Check headlight aim and lenses before November (IIHS: 1 in 3 cars rates Poor/Marginal).
- Pedestrians: wear something reflective or light-colored after 5 p.m. starting Nov 2 (Harkey's own recommendation); cross at lit intersections — #1155 found 73% of pedestrian deaths happen outside intersections.
- Parents: after-school activities now end in the dark — walk routes change on Nov 2 even though the schedule doesn't.
- Fleet/delivery managers: last-mile stops land in darkness; brief drivers on the +13% window.
