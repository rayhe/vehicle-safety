The Robotaxi Crash Reports Said No Injuries. The Police Reports Disagreed.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 15, 2026

On September 14, federal safety regulators opened an audit into the crash reports filed by Avride, the robotaxi company running driverless rides through Uber in Dallas, and the reason is the kind of thing that makes a detective reach for the second cup of coffee: NHTSA compared the company's federal filings against the actual police reports and found injuries the paperwork didn't mention. Six crash reports, six report IDs, and a pattern the agency described with the bureaucratic equivalent of a raised eyebrow.[1]

6
Standing General Order crash reports NHTSA cited in audit query AQ26003, each with injuries missing or downgraded relative to police records.

Here is what the opening resume says, in plain language. Several of Avride's filings stated the company was not aware of any injuries when it submitted them. Some of those same filings said police reports of the crashes were available. NHTSA went and got the police reports, including for crashes where Avride hadn't listed police reports as a data source at all, and the police paperwork indicated potential injuries to occupants of the Avride vehicles, or occupants of the other vehicles, or both. In a separate flourish, some reports initially stated that an injury was reported or alleged, and the updated versions of those same reports later stated that no injuries were alleged. Injuries went in, and then the injuries went out.

Context matters, because Avride was already on the agency's radar. Back in May, NHTSA opened a preliminary evaluation after 16 crashes in Dallas and Austin between December 2025 and March 2026, every one of them with the automated driving system engaged and a trained safety monitor sitting behind the wheel.[2] The failure modes read like a driving test from which the student should have been excused early: lane changes into the paths of other cars, failure to slow or stop for traffic ahead, striking stationary objects partially blocking the road. NHTSA's assessment was that the system showed "excessive assertiveness and insufficient capability," which is regulator for driving like it owned the road before it had earned the keys. Avride, a Yandex self-driving spinoff now owned by Nebius running about 200 Hyundai Ioniq 5s, said it had reported everything as required and implemented fixes per incident.[3]

Now the part that should bother readers of this site specifically. America has exactly one national record of self-driving crashes: the Standing General Order database. Our own decade of FARS data has no field for any of it, no automation checkbox, nothing, which means the SGO filings are the single federal window into what the robotaxis are doing out there. This audit just found smudges on that window. And even if every report were perfect, the database still couldn't tell you the one number that matters: the crash rate. IIHS pointed out in July that companies aren't required to report the miles they drive, so there is no denominator, and without a denominator there is no rate, only a pile of numerators arguing with each other.[4] A crash database that undercounts injuries and can't compute rates is less a safety record than a rumor with a filing deadline.

What to actually do

Treat every robotaxi safety statistic you hear from an operator as unaudited until this audit resolves, because right now that is literally what it is. You can read the SGO database yourself at nhtsa.gov, and the six cited report IDs (31101-13612 and company) are downloadable if you want to read the paperwork yourself. Watch whether AQ26003 closes quietly or escalates, and note the calendar: audit queries have a way of becoming the opening chapter of something longer. If you ride in Dallas, the practical advice hasn't changed since May, which is that the safety monitor is still in the front seat for a reason.

What this doesn't prove

An audit query is a compliance review, not a defect finding, and NHTSA has accused Avride of nothing. Avride's defense has real structure: the SGO's five-day reporting clock practically guarantees that early filings get revised as facts arrive, police "potential injuries" are preliminary by nature and frequently revised downward, and every one of the 16 crashes happened under supervision, not in driverless operation. Those downgrades could be honest information updates rather than anything darker, and the audit may close with no action. A narrower summary is still damning: the only national database tracking robotaxi crashes cannot currently be trusted to count the injuries, and it was never able to compute the rate. Both halves of the fraction are broken: the numerator is under audit, and the denominator was never collected.
