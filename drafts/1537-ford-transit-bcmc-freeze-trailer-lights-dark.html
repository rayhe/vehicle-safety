Ford’s New Transit Vans Can Go Dark While Towing. The Reset Is Pulling the Battery.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 19, 2026

Before you hook up a trailer to a 2026 Ford Transit, check the lights. Then check them again, because Ford just recalled 60,956 of these vans over a software bug that can freeze the computer running the lights, and when that computer freezes, the trailer goes dark with no warning at all.[1]

6
Systems one frozen body control module switches off at once (trailer lighting counted as one system): trailer taillights and reverse lamps, interior lighting, air conditioning, 12-volt outlets, the DC/AC inverter, and the anti-theft horn.

Ford calls the part Body Control Module C, one of the computers that runs the van’s electrical features. Inside its software, an interrupt service routine was set to the wrong priority level. That sounds like the kind of detail only an engineer would care about, until you hear what it does: the wrong priority can send the module’s microcontroller into an endless loop, and the module locks up. Not degraded, not glitchy: locked until somebody disconnects the 12-volt battery. A 2026 work van needs its battery pulled to get its lights back.[3][6]

NHTSA’s filing is blunt about why this matters. Trailer lighting is not a convenience feature. It is federal equipment, required under FMVSS 108, and a van towing a dark trailer at night is a van other drivers cannot see from behind. Ford’s own words in the filing put it plainly: a loss of lighting can reduce trailer visibility to other drivers, increasing the risk of a crash. That is the whole case, and it fits in one sentence, because the risk is not complicated: a dark trailer at highway speed at nighttime, with everybody in the lane behind you guessing where your trailer ends.[1]

Here is the part that should bother anyone who owns one. Ford found this in August, while reviewing a warranty claim for an unresponsive control module. By October 2, the company had counted 124 warranty claims, 53 field reports, and one customer report across North America, with 118 warranty claims and 47 field reports in the U.S. alone. Ford filed the recall on November 2, and owner letters went out November 16. So the timeline runs about three months from Ford’s August discovery to a mailed letter, and the fix that is already sitting at dealers, free, right now, has an over-the-air twin that does not arrive until January 2027. If you tow for a living and you wait for the over-the-air update, you are driving into the darkest two months of the year with a module that can freeze mid-trip.[4][5]

None of this is about the Transit as a vehicle. Say that clearly, because the numbers demand it. Across a decade of FARS data, the Transit posted 178 deaths and a fatality rate of 0.14 deaths per 100 million miles, one of the lowest rates among all 337 models in this site’s dataset, and far below its full-size rivals: the Chevrolet Express posts 0.92, the GMC Savana 0.54, and the Ford E-350 2.51. Its drivers in fatal crashes show 13.6 percent impairment, below the fleet average, which is what a work vehicle driven on the clock looks like. This is a safe van with a software problem, not a dangerous van, and the distinction matters because safety ratings measure crash structure, not whether the lights stay on. A safe van that goes dark behind a trailer is still dark.[7]

Ford’s year supplies the context, and it is not flattering. Ford has filed 53 recalls in 2026 by one November count, including a 741,195-vehicle rollaway recall filed in the same week whose fix instructions do not arrive until 2027. It is also the second recall on the 2026 Transit itself: an earlier campaign covers wireless rear seatbelt reminder buckles that can fail to show a change in buckle status. Two recalls, two different electrical systems, same model year, same van. Somebody at Ford should be asking what else in the 2026 electrical architecture was signed off in a hurry.[2][8]

Fairness first, because the counterargument here is real: zero crashes, zero injuries. A total of 178 reports across 61,000 vans, and a free fix already available. Most owners will never see this freeze, and the trailer-light failure needs the van to actually be towing when the module happens to lock up. Ford caught it through its own warranty review and filed the recall. That is a company doing the recall system roughly the way it is supposed to work, and this article should not pretend otherwise. But the counterargument stops at the conditional, and the conditional is the story. A dark trailer at highway speed at night needs exactly one unlucky freeze to become a rear-end crash, and FMVSS 108 exists because trailer lighting is not optional equipment. Zero crashes so far is luck with a deadline on it.

What to do: if you own a 2026 Transit, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 26V727 right now. Do not guess from the build date. Affected vans were built February 25, 2025 through May 20, 2026, but not in VIN order, so the date range cannot clear your van. Book the dealer software update this week: it is free, it is available now, and it is the only fix that exists before January. If you tow, do a trailer-light walk-around before every tow until the update is installed, because the freeze gives no warning. And if the module does freeze on the road, know where the 12-volt battery is before you need it, because that is the reset. Ford customer service is 1-866-436-7332. NHTSA’s Vehicle Safety Hotline is 1-888-327-4236.
