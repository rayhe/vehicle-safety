Waymo Answered 12 Questions About Hitting a 9-Year-Old. Every Answer Is Redacted.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 12, 2026

On January 23, at 8:30 on a school morning in Santa Monica, an empty Jaguar I-Pace running Waymo's fifth-generation driver struck a 9-year-old girl crossing 24th Street. She had darted from behind a parked Suburban into the drop-off chaos, moving at a rapid pace between queued cars, and the robot detected her the instant she emerged, braked hard, and still made contact at under 6 mph. She got up, walked to the curb, and went home with minor injuries. That is the good version of this story. Here is the other one: federal investigators asked Waymo 12 questions about how its cars behave around schools, Waymo answered all 12, and the public is allowed to read zero of them.[1][2]

Do the math and the robot looks innocent. Seventeen miles per hour is about 25 feet per second. Scrubbing to under 6 mph under hard braking consumes roughly 14 feet of pavement, which means the child was visible to the machine for about half a second, and no human driver on Earth stops in half a second. It reacted faster than biology allows, and physics collected its toll anyway. Waymo's own modeling says a fully attentive human would have struck her at 14 mph, more than double the robot's impact speed.[3] By every measure of reaction time, the machine was the better driver that morning. Which is precisely why the crash itself is not the interesting part of this story. The speed is, and that morning the Waymo was doing 17 in a 25-mph zone, forty feet past the end of an adjacent 15-mph school zone, at 8:30 a.m. during drop-off, in a corridor containing a queue of five stopped cars, a crossing guard, and other children.[4] Legal? Yes. But the question NHTSA actually opened its preliminary evaluation to answer, case PE26001, is whether the car "exercised appropriate caution" near an elementary school during drop-off hours.[5] Investigators asked how Waymo sets speeds in school zones, whether it has received complaints about its cars near schools, and for documentation of the software that decides. Those answers exist, filed with the federal government, and you cannot read them.

Meanwhile the marketing department is having a great year. Waymo says it is committed to being "transparent when incidents occur." It reported the crash the same day, called 911, and its first national ad campaign, aired during a World Cup match, claims the system is ten times safer than a human driver.[6][5] All of that can be true at the same time as this: the only documents that would tell a parent how the robotaxi decides to drive past their kid's school are black bars. NHTSA posted the first batch of redacted answers in August only after TechCrunch asked where they were.[1] Transparency, it turns out, is available upon request, heavily redacted, and four months late.

Now the defense, stated properly, because the universe is not a courtroom and the robot deserves its hearing. A nine-year-old sprinted midblock from behind a tall SUV directly into a lane of moving traffic. That is the textbook unavoidable crash, the kind that kills children under human drivers every week. It detected her instantly, braked harder than any human could, and turned what its own models call a 14-mph human impact into a 6-mph bump with minor injuries. Waymo called 911 and cooperated with both federal probes. As for the redactions, every automaker claims confidential business information in defect investigations: Ford does it, GM does it, Tesla does it. Congress wrote the exemption precisely for this kind of material. Black bars are not an admission of anything; they are a routine Tuesday at the Office of Defects Investigation. If anyone deserves blame for that morning, it is the double-parked queue at school drop-off, a chaos no driver, carbon or silicon, was ever going to fully solve.

What this article did not prove: that Waymo's school-zone protocol is unsafe, because the protocol is the redacted part. That the redactions hide wrongdoing, because we cannot see them either. That the crash was avoidable at any speed a reasonable driver would choose, because half a second is half a second. NHTSA has not found a defect, and the NTSB's final report is roughly a year out. Waymo's "14 mph human" number comes from Waymo's own model, peer-reviewed but company-cited, and you should hold it the way you hold any number handed to you by an interested party.

So here is what you can actually do. NHTSA's PE26001 file is public; watch whether the agency closes it quietly or upgrades it to an engineering analysis, because that decision is the signal and the redactions are the noise.[5] Teach your kids never to cross between queued cars at drop-off, robot or no robot. And the next time a robotaxi company tells you it is ten times safer than you, ask yourself which half of that sentence is marketing and which half is a trade secret. Right now, legally, you are only allowed to hear one of them.
