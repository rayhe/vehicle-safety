NHTSA’s Number Is Finally Out. The Safety Streak Survived Q2 by 20 Deaths.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 13, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They did not get better, and this time the numbers are NHTSA’s own. An estimated 16,750 people died in motor vehicle crashes in the first half of 2026, down about 2.5 percent from the 17,175 projected for the same period in 2025, according to the agency’s early estimate published November 10.[1] Fatality rate: 1.02 deaths per 100 million miles driven, down from 1.05, the second-lowest first-half rate since at least 2014. Americans drove 12.9 billion more miles and died less often doing it.

0.2%
How much Q2 2026 deaths fell versus Q2 2025. Q1 fell 5.0%.

Good news, on the headline. Now split the half in two. Q1 2026: 7,715 deaths, down 5.0 percent. Q2 2026: 9,035 deaths, down 0.2 percent. Every bit of the half-year improvement happened in the first three months. In the second three months, the improvement was 20 deaths out of 9,055, a rounding error with a press release attached.[1]

Run that deceleration as a rate and it reads worse. Q1 improved 5.0 percent; Q2 improved 0.2 percent. Ninety-six percent of the improvement rate evaporated between quarters. A year ago, NHTSA’s first-half 2025 estimate showed deaths down 8.2 percent, the largest first-half decline since 2008. This half showed 2.5 percent. Whichever series you prefer, the improvement is roughly a third of what it was twelve months ago.

And it was not broad-based. Eight of ten NHTSA regions improved, but 15 states plus the District of Columbia got worse in the first half of 2026, against 35 states plus Puerto Rico improving. Texas posted the largest death count in the country, an estimated 1,851, essentially flat against 1,856 a year earlier, with a fatality rate of 1.19 against the national 1.02. California recorded 1,825 deaths, Florida 1,439.[3]

Context matters, because the streak is still technically alive. NHTSA’s Q1 report counted 16 consecutive quarterly declines running since Q2 2022. Q2 2026 keeps that streak intact, by 20 projected deaths. A streak that survives by 20 deaths is a streak on paper. It is not momentum.

One more series, because two independent counts saying the same thing is worth more than either alone. National Safety Council estimates, covered here in September, put first-half 2026 deaths at 18,460, down 2 percent, with a rate of 1.12. NSC counts differently, deaths within 100 days of a crash, private roadways included, where NHTSA counts 30 days on public roads only, which is why its total runs about 1,700 higher. Different methods, different levels, same verdict: the decline has slowed to roughly 2 to 2.5 percent from 8 to 13 percent a year ago.[4]

Here is the counterfactual, labeled plainly as arithmetic and not a forecast. Had the first half of 2025’s 8.2 percent NHTSA pace held, the first half of 2026 would have come in near 15,767 deaths. Actual: 16,750. About 983 more people died than the old pace implied. That gap is the cost of the stall, measured in the only currency this column trades in.

Two footnotes from the revision trail, because revisions are data too. NHTSA’s Q1 report put the first quarter at 7,770 deaths, down 4.3 percent. This release revises Q1 to 7,715, down 5.0 percent. Q1 actually looks slightly better on revision, which means the stall is entirely a Q2 story, not a restatement of old numbers. Similarly, the first half of 2025 was 17,140 in the earlier release and is now projected at 17,175. Small revisions, same direction.[1]

What to do, concretely. First, stop reading the national headline as a local forecast. Q2 was flat nationally and 15 states plus DC got worse; check your own state’s trend before assuming the improvement covers you. Second, if you drive in Texas, the math is blunt: 1,851 deaths, flat year over year, at a 1.19 rate against a 1.02 national rate, roughly 17 percent more risk per mile than the country as a whole. Drive like the streak is over, because in your state it may be. Third, mark late December on the calendar. NHTSA’s next release covers the first nine months of 2026, and that number, not this one, will say whether Q2 was a pause or the end of the streak.[1]

Limitations, stated plainly: all first-half 2026 numbers are preliminary statistical projections, not final counts, and they will be revised when the FARS Annual Reporting File for 2025 lands later this year and the final files arrive next year. Q2’s 0.2 percent is a projection measured against a projected Q2 2025 base of 9,055, so the “20 deaths” margin sits inside the noise of the estimation method; the honest phrasing is effectively flat, not exactly 20. NSC and NHTSA count different things and their totals must never be mixed in a single sentence without naming the series. State-level half-year estimates carry far more noise than the national number; do not write a single-state verdict off a projection.

Counterargument, at full strength: a 2.5 percent decline is still a decline, the rate is the second-lowest ever recorded for a first half, deaths fell while miles rose, and the streak is intact. A single quarter of near-flat performance after the largest first-half drop since 2008 could be regression to the mean. You cannot fall 8 percent every half-year forever, because the denominator keeps shrinking and the easy wins, fleet turnover and post-pandemic normalization, get used up. That reading is mostly fair, and this article grants it. The counterweight is the within-half math: the improvement did not just slow, it nearly stopped in Q2, and the breadth is narrowing, with 15 states plus DC moving the wrong way. A deceleration that steep, while miles driven kept rising, is a materially different regime than the 2022–2025 run, and the next release will tell us whether it was a pause or a turn.
