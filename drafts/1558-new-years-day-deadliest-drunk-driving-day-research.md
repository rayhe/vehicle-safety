## Story in one line
On an average New Year's Day, 141 people die on American roads, 56% more than an ordinary January day, and January 1 has beaten its ordinary-day comparison in 50 of 50 years, because the killing is not spread across the holiday at all: 41% of the day's fatal crashes happen between midnight and 4 a.m., and 54% involve a drinking driver.

## Verified facts
1. **The 50-year record (Hazardly, FARS 1975-2024, read directly).** On an average New Year's Day, 140.7 people die on American roads, 7,033 in 50 years. That is 56% more than an ordinary day in January that falls on the same weekday (90 deaths). New Year's Day was deadlier than an ordinary day in 50 of 50 years. The worst was 1981, when 197 people died on January 1. In 2024, the newest year in the record, 126 died, against 76 on an ordinary Monday that January. Since 2015, the average is 129.3, still 41% above an ordinary day. Method: every fatal crash on a public road in the 50 states and D.C.; a death counts on the date of the crash; "ordinary day" is the average of the other days in the same month and year on the same weekday, excluding other holidays. 2024 is a first release and may be revised.
2. **The week around it.** Average deaths, three days before to four days after, over 50 years: Dec 29: 97, Dec 30: 102, Dec 31: 113, Jan 1: 141, Jan 2: 86, Jan 3: 94, Jan 4: 92, Jan 5: 91. An average day of the year sees 114. New Year's Day is the worst day of its week, and the day after it (86) is the safest of the eight. The spike is one day wide.
3. **The clock.** The deadliest hour on New Year's is 2 a.m. to 3 a.m. 41% of the day's fatal crashes happen between midnight and 4 a.m., against 17% on all days. The Callahan/1Point21 analysis of FARS 2015-2019 found more than twice as many crashes and fatalities on New Year's Day between midnight and 4 a.m. as on New Year's Eve between 8 p.m. and midnight.
4. **The drinking share.** 54% of fatal crashes on New Year's involved a drinking driver (police-reported, 1982-2020), against 34% across all days in those years. Police reports undercount alcohol, so both true shares are higher; the gap is the point. Callahan/1Point21 (FARS 2015-2019): January 1 had 232 alcohol-related fatal crashes out of 544 total fatal crashes (43%), 255 people killed in alcohol-impaired crashes over five New Year's Days, and an 89% higher risk of a fatal drunk-driving crash than an average day. NSC: alcohol-impaired drivers are involved in 34% of New Year's traffic fatalities and 37% of Christmas fatalities, against a 30% annual average.
5. **The NSC forecast frame.** NSC estimates 429 people may die on U.S. roads during the New Year's Day 2026 holiday period (6 p.m. Wednesday, December 31, 2025 to 11:59 p.m. Sunday, January 4, 2026), 4.25 days, 90% confidence interval 339-529, slightly above the six-year average of 423 for holidays of the same length. The average over the last six 4.25-day New Year's periods is 12% higher than comparison periods (423 vs. 379). Estimated nonfatal medically consulted injuries: 48,900 (CI 38,600-60,300). NSC underestimated the actual New Year's deaths in 2023 by 5.3%, inside the confidence interval.
6. **The three-holiday tie.** NSC estimates, all 4.25-day periods: Thanksgiving 2026: 428 deaths (CI 372-487). Christmas Day 2025: 431 deaths (CI 372-493), slightly below the six-year average of 450. New Year's Day 2026: 429 deaths (CI 339-529). Three holidays, three estimates, a three-death spread. The NSC Christmas and New Year's estimates were published together in NSC's holiday-season release: "431 traffic fatalities during the Christmas holiday period and 429 during New Year's."
7. **The shape comparison (Hazardly, same 50-year census).** Average deaths and change vs. an ordinary day: Fourth of July 160 (+30%), New Year's Day 141 (+56%), Christmas Eve 134 (+19%), Halloween 130 (+5%), Thanksgiving Eve 130 (+31%), Labor Day 123 (+27%), Thanksgiving Day 121 (+15%), Memorial Day 117 (+27%), New Year's Eve 113 (-1%), St. Patrick's Day 107 (+5%), Christmas Day 100 (-12%). July 4 kills more people in absolute terms; January 1 has the largest percentage spike of any holiday in the table. Christmas Day is the only holiday in the table that runs BELOW its ordinary day.
8. **The national trend is improving; the spike is not.** NHTSA: an estimated 11,904 people died in alcohol-impaired driving crashes in 2024, about 30% of all traffic fatalities, one life every 44 minutes, and a third straight annual decline (2022: 13,524; 2023: 12,429; 2024: 11,904). December 2023 alone saw 1,038 drunk-driving deaths, one of the highest single-month totals in 15 years (NHTSA via DK Law). The January 1 average since 2015 (129.3) is below the 50-year average (140.7), but the +41% spike against an ordinary day persists.
9. **Where the spike is worst (Hazardly, states with 60+ holiday deaths).** Deaths on New Year's Day vs. each state's own average day, 1975-2024: Wisconsin 1.53x (153 deaths on the day), Texas 1.50x (748), Arizona 1.43x (188), Nevada 1.42x (61), Florida 1.41x (552), Massachusetts 1.41x (100), Louisiana 1.40x (173), California 1.37x (789). Texas and California dominate the raw counts; Wisconsin has the worst ratio.
10. **Local FARS baseline (fars_output.js, 2014-2023, computed this run).** 490,736 drivers in fatal crashes; 98,348 any-impaired (alcohol-positive at any BAC, or drug-positive), a 20.0% share. Definition warning: this is any-impairment (BAC > 0 or drug-positive), not NHTSA's alcohol-impaired (BAC 0.08+) definition behind the 30% annual figure. The two shares must not be compared as if they were the same measure.

## Original contribution: the tie, the record, and the window
- **Three holidays, one toll, two shapes.** 428, 431, 429. The NSC's three winter-holiday death estimates are within three of each other, and the press treats them as three versions of the same story. They are not. Christmas Day itself kills 100 people on average, 12% FEWER than an ordinary day. New Year's Day kills 141, 56% MORE. The Christmas toll is spread across travel days; the New Year's toll is a spike on a single date. Same body count, opposite geometry, and the difference is one night of drinking.
- **50 of 50.** January 1 beat its ordinary-day comparison in every year from 1975 to 2024. Fifty years, fifty spikes, zero exceptions, across the safest decade in the record (the 2010s) and the deadliest (the early 1980s). No other holiday in the Hazardly table carries a perfect record, and the Fourth of July, which kills more people in absolute terms (160 vs. 141), manages only +30% against its ordinary day. January 1 is not the deadliest day in absolute terms. It is the most reliably deadlier-than-normal day in the calendar.
- **The four-hour window.** 41% of the day's fatal crashes happen between midnight and 4 a.m., against 17% on all days. The deadliest single hour is 2 a.m. to 3 a.m. The actionable unit of this story is not "New Year's Day." It is four hours, and really one hour. Everything the article tells the reader to do is about those four hours.
- **The baseline improved; the spike did not.** Alcohol-impaired deaths fell three straight years to 11,904 in 2024, and the January 1 average since 2015 (129.3) sits below the 50-year average (140.7). But the spike against an ordinary day is still +41% since 2015. The country got better at not drinking and driving on ordinary days. It did not get better at not drinking and driving on January 1.
- **The day after is the safest day of the week.** January 2 averages 86 deaths, below the yearly daily average of 114 and the lowest of the eight days around the holiday. The danger does not bleed into the new year. It arrives at midnight, peaks at 2 a.m., and is gone by sunrise on the 2nd. That is a drinking story, not a winter-driving story, and it is why the article's advice is about the night, not the season.

## What this article must NOT claim
- That the NSC's 429 estimate applies to the coming New Year's Day, January 1, 2027. It covers December 31, 2025 to January 4, 2026. The 2027 estimate has not been published. The article looks ahead to January 1, 2027 (a Friday) using the 50-year record and the most recent estimate, and says so.
- That Christmas is "safe." Christmas Day averages 100 deaths, 12% below an ordinary day, but Christmas Eve runs +19% (134), and the Christmas period estimate is 431 deaths. The safe day is one date inside a dangerous week.
- That the 54% drinking-driver share means alcohol caused 54% of the deaths. It is police-reported involvement in fatal crashes, 1982-2020, and police reports undercount alcohol. Involvement is not causation, and the share is the gap (54% vs. 34%), not a verdict on any single crash.
- That the local FARS 20.0% any-impaired share contradicts NHTSA's 30% alcohol-impaired share. Different definitions (BAC > 0 or drug-positive vs. BAC 0.08+), different windows. Do not compare them.
- That July 4 is safer than January 1 because its percentage spike is smaller. July 4 averages 160 deaths, more than January 1's 141. The article's claim is about reliability and concentration, not absolute body count.
- A specific 2027 death forecast. The article gives no number for January 1, 2027. It gives the 50-year average (141), the recent average (129.3 since 2015), and the most recent NSC period estimate (429), each labeled with its window.
- That rideshare caused the post-2015 decline. The decline coincides with rideshare saturation, but no source in this file establishes causation. Mention it only as a hypothesis, and only once.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)
The whole premise can be read as an artifact of a low baseline. An ordinary January day is one of the safest days of the year (90 deaths, against a 114-per-day yearly average), because January has short days, cold weather, and less travel. Measuring January 1 against that low bar inflates the percentage: +56% sounds apocalyptic, but it is 51 extra deaths on a day when 90 people die anyway, and July 4 kills 19 more people than January 1 in absolute terms without anyone calling it the deadliest day of the year. The post-2015 average (129.3) is falling, alcohol-impaired deaths have fallen three straight years, and the 2024 count (126) is the lowest January 1 total in the modern record's recent years. The honest version of this story is narrower than the headline: one night a year, a few thousand very drunk drivers get on the road between midnight and 4 a.m., and the rest of the country's driving keeps getting safer around them. The spike is real, but it is a spike on top of a shrinking baseline, and the trend lines all point the right way.

## Limitations
- NSC's 429 (New Year's 2026) and 431 (Christmas 2025) are statistical projections, not counts; the 2025-26 actuals will not be final until FARS data arrives in 2027. The 2027 holiday estimates have not been published.
- Hazardly's "ordinary day" method excludes other holidays and compares within month and weekday; it controls for seasonality but not for weather, travel volume, or enforcement intensity on the holiday itself.
- The 54% drinking-driver share is police-reported involvement, 1982-2020; police reports undercount alcohol, and the field is missing for some years. The 43% figure (Callahan/1Point21, 2015-2019) uses a different window and a different denominator (fatal crashes, not deaths).
- The local FARS file (2014-2023) has no day-of-year field, so the January 1 analysis relies on Hazardly's FARS build, not the site's own dataset. The site's own numbers appear only as the aggregate toxicology baseline (20.0% any-impaired).
- State ratios (Wisconsin 1.53x, Texas 1.50x) are ratios against each state's own average day over 50 years; they do not adjust for population growth, urbanization, or changes in drinking law over the period.
- The 2 a.m. to 3 a.m. deadliest-hour finding is a share of fatal crashes by hour, not a death count by hour, and it aggregates 50 years; bar-closing times and last-call laws have changed over that span.

## What to do (actionable)
- The window is midnight to 4 a.m. on January 1, peaking 2-3 a.m. If you are driving at all that night, be home before midnight or stay put until after 4 a.m. The data says the day after is the safest day of the week (86 deaths), so waiting it out works.
- If you drink, book the ride before the first drink, not after the last one. Rideshare surge pricing peaks 12:30-2:30 a.m., exactly the deadliest hours; a pre-booked car or a designated driver booked in advance costs less than a surge fare and removes the decision from the drunkest hour of the year.
- Hosts: stop serving by midnight, keep car keys in a bowl by the door, and offer the couch. The 2 a.m. hour is when guests who "feel fine" leave. They are the statistic.
- If you are the sober driver on the road between midnight and 4 a.m., assume the car ahead of you is impaired: extra following distance, no passing on two-lane roads, and treat every green light as a suggestion until you have checked the cross traffic.
- The 21-to-24 age group has the highest share of drunk drivers in fatal crashes (NHTSA). If your kid is 21-24 and going out on New Year's Eve, the ride home is the present that matters.
- Check your own state: Wisconsin, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, and Florida run the worst January 1 ratios in the country. If you live in one of them, the spike is not abstract. It is your roads.
