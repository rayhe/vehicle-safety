# Research: 993-ram-3500-other-driver-death-rate

## Angle
The deadliest vehicle in America isn't dangerous to its driver. It's dangerous to everyone else. IIHS's July 30, 2026 driver-death-rate release (only the second to compute "other-driver death rate") shows the average car kills more strangers than owners: 53 other-driver deaths vs 37 driver deaths per million registered vehicle years. The Ram 3500 crew cab long bed 4WD sits atop the kill-others list at 204. The flip: very large pickups are the SAFEST class for their own drivers (24) and the deadliest to others (110). Safety is asymmetric, and the five-star rating on the window sticker measures only one direction.

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. First-of-kind second-edition metric, covers ~all nameplates 2023 MY equivalents.
- Novel on site? Yes. #985 covered the Rio driver-death angle; #936 decomposed FARS death rates. Zero site coverage of the other-driver dimension. Everyone wrote about the Rio at 170; nobody wrote that the truck at 204 kills four times the average stranger.
- Original contribution: threat ratios (other-driver/driver death rate) computed per class and per model: very large pickups 4.6x, F-150 SuperCrew 2WD 14.1x, minicars 0.35x. Cross-list analysis (both-lists, low-low, internal-asymmetry).

## Methodology notes
- FARS death counts; registration from Mobility Global. Adjusted for driver age and sex. NOT adjusted for speed, daily mileage, or driving habits. Minimum 100,000 registered vehicle years or 20 deaths. Class averages more reliable than individual models (IIHS's own caveat). Only driver deaths counted (no passengers/pedestrians in either metric).

## Threat ratios (original calculation)
- Fleet: 53/37 = 1.43x (your car is 43% deadlier to strangers than to you)
- Very large pickups: 110/24 = 4.6x
- F-150 SuperCrew 2WD: 99/7 = 14.1x
- Minicars: 55/158 = 0.35x (killed far more than they kill)
- Midsize wagons: 27/27 = 1.0x (symmetric)
- Large cars: 107/104 = 1.03x

## Strongest counterargument
Heavy-duty pickups are work trucks. They tow, they haul, they rack up commercial miles on rural two-lane roads where head-ons happen and speed differentials are high. IIHS doesn't adjust for mileage or speed, and HD pickups plausibly lead both. The other-driver metric also counts crashes where the other driver was at fault (a drunk crossing the center line into a Ram 3500 counts as a Ram 3500 other-driver death). And the Tundra CrewMax (driver rate 0) proves a full-size pickup can score zero — so it's not pure mass; design, buyers, and use patterns matter.

## Limitations
- No VMT normalization: rates are per registered vehicle year, not per mile. High-mileage work trucks are penalized.
- Age/sex-adjusted only; speed, alcohol, mileage unadjusted.
- Individual-model estimates have wide confidence intervals (Malibu 116, CI 39-194).
- Partner-vehicle vintage not disentangled (IIHS itself hypothesizes older crash partners drive the 53 > 37 gap).
- Driver deaths only: pedestrians, cyclists, and passengers are invisible in both metrics.
- Commercial fleet use (contractors, fleets) inflates HD pickup exposure.

## Actionable takeaways
1. Look up YOUR car's other-driver death rate at the IIHS table, not just the driver rate or star rating. The star rating measures one direction; the other-driver table measures the other.
2. Small-car owners: your biggest asymmetric threat is a large pickup, not another small car. Minicar driver rate 158 vs other-driver 55 says the danger flows one way.
3. The Tundra CrewMax (driver 0) and F-150 2WD (driver 7) show big trucks don't have to be killers by design — the F-150 protects its own while its 99 other-driver rate shows compatibility is the unsolved half.
4. Regulators: no US standard grades front-end crash compatibility with other vehicles. NHTSA's five-star program has no other-driver metric at all.
