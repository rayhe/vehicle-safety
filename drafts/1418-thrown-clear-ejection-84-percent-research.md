# Research: Thrown Clear — 84% of Ejected Occupants Don't Survive (#1418)

**Slug:** 1418-thrown-clear-ejection-84-percent
**Journalist:** Mia Crumplezone (Safety Engineering Editor — technical but accessible; gets excited about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds)
**Kicker:** Investigation
**News peg:** NHTSA, Traffic Safety Facts, "Occupant Protection in Passenger Vehicles: 2024 Data," DOT HS 813 840 (October 2026) — fresh fact sheet with a dedicated ejection section; plus NHTSA's "Overview of Motor Vehicle Traffic Crashes in 2024," DOT HS 813 791 (April 2026).

## Angle
The seat belt's real job isn't cushioning you in the crash. It's keeping you inside the car. NHTSA's October 2026 occupant-protection fact sheet puts the sharpest number in the entire federal safety literature on one line: in 2024 fatal crashes, 84 percent of passenger-vehicle occupants who were totally ejected from their vehicles were killed. And the belt is almost absurdly good at preventing exactly that: only 1 percent of belted occupants in fatal crashes were totally ejected, against 24 percent of the unrestrained. A 24-to-1 containment gap from a strap of webbing.

Ejection is still one in five vehicle-occupant deaths. FARS counted 5,072 ejected vehicle occupants killed in 2024 (excluding motorcyclists) out of 24,473 total — 20.7%. Light-truck occupants eject at a higher rate than car occupants (22.7% of light-truck occupant deaths involved ejection, vs 16.1% for passenger cars). The good news buried in the same data: ejected passenger-vehicle occupant deaths fell 537 in 2024, an 11% drop — the steepest improvement of any category in the 2024 overview, against a 4.3% fall in total deaths.

### The containment gap (DOT HS 813 840, 2024 fatal crashes, known restraint use)
- Totally ejected occupants who were killed: **84%**
- Belted occupants in fatal crashes who were totally ejected: **1%**
- Unrestrained occupants in fatal crashes who were totally ejected: **24%**
- Belt effectiveness vs fatal injury: **-45%** front-seat passenger car, **-60%** front-seat light truck (Kahane 2015; NHTSA 1984)
- Frontal air bags vs fatalities: **-14%** unbelted, **-11%** belted (air bags are supplemental; not designed for all crashes)

### 2024 vs 2023 (DOT HS 813 791)
- Ejected passenger-vehicle occupant deaths: **-537 (-11%)** — steepest category drop in the report
- Rollover passenger-vehicle occupant deaths: -380 (-5.7%)
- Unrestrained passenger-vehicle occupant deaths: -775 (-7.4%)
- Total traffic deaths: 41,025 → 39,254 (-4.3%)
- Context: 22,713 passenger-vehicle occupants killed in 2024; 9,758 unrestrained (48% of known restraint use); 59% of pickup drivers killed were unrestrained (vs 47% car, 46% SUV, 42% van); 60% of killed occupants aged 21–34 were unrestrained.

## What this means / actionable
- The belt's primary job is containment. The 1%-vs-24% ejection split is the argument in one number — and it holds for back-seat riders too (58% of second-row occupants killed were unrestrained, vs 47% front row).
- Pickups are the worst combination: highest unrestrained share among killed drivers (59%) and the vehicle type most likely to roll over and eject. If you drive a pickup, the belt matters more, not less.
- Ejection risk is why "short trip, low speed" is not an excuse: ejection doesn't need highway speed, it needs an unbelted body and a sudden stop or roll.
- Check: buckle every seat, every trip, including the back seat; never put the shoulder belt behind your back or under an arm (a misrouted belt can't contain you); keep kids in the back seat until at least 13.

## Limitations
- FARS only captures fatal crashes. The 84% figure is "of totally ejected occupants in fatal crashes" — it does not mean 84% of all ejections are fatal, because non-fatal crashes aren't in the file. State it that way.
- The FARS Encyclopedia table combines total and partial ejection; the 84% figure is total ejection only. Don't mix the two denominators.
- Restraint use was unknown for 11% (2,553) of passenger-vehicle occupants killed in 2024; all restraint percentages are "based on known restraint use."
- 2024 data is the Annual Report File (ARF), subject to revision when the final file publishes.
- Vehicle classification changed in 2020 (vPIC); pre-2020 comparisons need the caveat.

## Strongest counterargument
Ejection correlates with the worst crashes. Occupants get ejected in rollovers and high-speed impacts that would likely have been fatal anyway — so the 84% kill rate partly measures crash severity, not ejection alone. The belt's 1%-vs-24% gap is the cleaner causal signal, but even there, unrestrained occupants differ systematically from belted ones (younger, more likely impaired, more likely speeding, more likely at night — 56% of nighttime killed occupants were unrestrained vs 41% daytime). The honest claim: belts prevent ejection, and ejection is nearly always fatal when it happens in a fatal crash — not that a belt would have saved every one of the 5,072.
