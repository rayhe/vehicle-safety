The Wheels Came Loose in October: 589 Cars, 3 Recalls, and Porsche's 1-in-5 Miss

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 30, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. Three NHTSA recall campaigns, filed within ten days of each other in October, covering 589 vehicles from two manufacturers, and every one of them is about fasteners coming loose. Wheels, in two of the three cases; the third is suspension bolts, which your humble data editor considers close enough to count.

19.6%
Share of the true affected Porsche population that the 2024 center-lock recall missed: 548 of 2,799 cars

Start with the expensive one: on October 16, Porsche filed campaign 26V682 for 548 model-year 2024 911 and 718 sports cars, because the single center-lock wheel bolt, the racing-derived fastener that replaces five lug nuts with one very serious nut, can fracture and let the wheel detach.[1] NHTSA classifies this as a Do Not Drive recall, the agency's most urgent category, which is a remarkable sentence to read about a GT3 RS. The twist is that Porsche already recalled these bolts once. Campaign 24V809, filed in October 2024 and amended that December, covered 2,251 vehicles for the identical failure, traced to one batch of out-of-spec bolts from German supplier Hirschvogel, with analyses confirming lead and bismuth contents outside the specification.[2][6] Then two more center locks fractured, one in May 2026 and one in August, and the second investigation found what the first missed: unidentified material movements inside the supplier's logistics chain, which meant the 2024 traceability exercise had gaps.[3] Porsche's own math puts 548 additional cars in the suspect population, from a six-week production window in the summer of 2024.[3]

Here is the number I could not stop staring at: 548 missed cars against a 2,251-car first recall gives a true affected population of 2,799, which means the original investigation missed 19.6 percent of the cars it was supposed to catch, nearly one in five. The recall needed a recall, and the only reason anyone knows is that two more bolts broke in the real world.

Now the cheap ones: on October 21, Hyundai filed 26V693 for 17 vehicles, 2026 Venue, Elantra, Elantra Hybrid, and Elantra N, all built between August 10 and 27 at the Ulsan plant in South Korea.[4] The wheel lug nuts may loosen and let the wheel detach, because a temporary manual torquing process did not torque them. The supplier of the hub-nut assembly is Samjin Nut of Cheonan, a company name I will never tire of typing. Filed alongside it was 26V692, the twin: 24 more vehicles, 20 Elantras and four Tucson Hybrids, with improperly tightened suspension strut fasteners from the same plant and the same supplier, where torque data was also recorded incorrectly.[5] Hyundai has since implemented new torque monitoring and sign-off procedures, which is the corporate equivalent of buying a torque wrench after the shelf collapses.

Two failure modes, one month, and a price spread from Hyundai's cheapest crossover to Porsche's most track-obsessed 911. The common thread is not the component; it is the verification. Porsche's paperwork said the bad batch was contained, and it was not, while Hyundai's paperwork said the bolts were torqued, and they were not.

Now the strongest case against everything I just wrote, stated fairly. 589 vehicles is a rounding error against 16 million annual US sales, and every one of these defects was caught by the system working as designed: field incidents triggered investigations, investigations triggered recalls, recalls triggered free repairs. Porsche's second investigation is arguably diligence, not failure; a company that re-audits its supplier's delivery notes and voluntarily expands a recall is doing the thing we ask companies to do. Hyundai's 17 lug-nut cars were built in a two-week window and caught within two months. If your takeaway is that wheels are falling off everywhere, the data says otherwise: this is three small, contained, quickly caught lapses, and the plural of anecdote is not data, even when I am the one doing the anecdoting.

What this tally does not prove: these are manufacturer-reported "potentially affected" counts, not confirmed defective vehicles, and the true number of bad bolts and loose nuts is lower, possibly much lower. My 19.6 percent miss rate treats the amended 24V809 population as the true baseline, so if the 2024 recall over-included cars, the miss rate is overstated, and if more VINs are still hiding in Hirschvogel's delivery notes, it is understated. Porsche published no injury data for the two 2026 incidents, and Hyundai reports no known crashes or injuries for either of its campaigns. Finally, this ledger covers October 2026 filings only; it is a snapshot, not a trend, and one month does not make a pattern no matter how nicely the numbers line up.

If you own a 2024 911 or 718 with center-lock wheels, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls right now; the affected VINs have been searchable since October 20, and if yours is on the list, park the car.[7] Do not wait for the owner letter, which is not scheduled until December 15. If you bought a 2026 Elantra, Venue, or Tucson Hybrid built at Ulsan, same drill: VINs searchable since October 23, free re-torque at the dealer, letters due December 19. And the general lesson, which costs nothing: recall letters trail filings by roughly two months, so never wait for the mail; check the VIN.
