Existential Dread

Your Tiguan Can Stall, Go Dark, and Lose Its Backup Camera in a Single Software Reset. All 49,958 Have the Bug.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 20, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: Volkswagen recalled 49,958 model-year 2025 Tiguans because a single software error can reset the body control module while you are driving, and when it does, the ignition switches off, the engine stalls, the warning lights die, the exterior lighting dies, and the backup camera goes blind, all from one glitch in one computer.[1] Your car has a nervous system now. This is what happens when it has a seizure.

100%
Share of the recalled Tiguans estimated to carry the defect, per the recall report

Pause on that number, because it inverts everything you know about recalls. A recall usually means a lottery: some supplier's Tuesday-afternoon batch of bolts might be bad, and the automaker drags in a few hundred thousand cars to find the unlucky one percent. On the very same day VW announced this Tiguan recall, it also recalled 208,724 older vehicles for a steering-rack bolt that might corrode, with an estimated defect rate of one percent,[2] while the Tiguan's number is one hundred percent. A bad bolt is a coin flip; bad code ships in every unit, because every unit runs the same code. When the defect is software, the recall population is the defect population, and there is no lucky majority.

Then there is the architecture question, which is the part that should keep you up at night. The body control module is the switchboard for the car's body electrical systems, and this one reset takes out propulsion, lighting, warnings, and the federally mandated rear camera in a single event. NHTSA's campaign record says the defect leaves these vehicles noncompliant with four separate Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards at once: 101 for controls and displays, 102 for the shift-lever and starter interlock, 108 for lamps, and 111 for rear visibility,[1] which means four federal standards fall to a single module. And consider the scenario the engineers surely rehearsed in the meeting where someone said the quiet part out loud: the engine dies at 65 mph on an unlit highway, you reach for the hazards, and the hazards are exterior lighting, which is controlled by the module that just reset. The thing you instinctively grab when everything fails is wired through the thing that failed.

Steel-manning the other side, because dread without fairness is just theater: software recalls are the good kind of recall. A dealer reflashes the BCM, the bug is gone permanently, no parts to manufacture, no 99 percent of healthy cars torn apart for nothing. VW also caught this itself; the campaign record cites no crashes, no injuries, no deaths, only the word "may," doing heroic work in a federal filing.[1] The cascade I just described is theoretical. It has to stay theoretical, because the fix exists and it works, and that is genuinely the system functioning as designed.

But notice what "the system functioning as designed" now requires: your car is a computer that can brick its own drivetrain, its lights, and its legally required safety equipment in one uncommanded reset, and the only reason nobody died is that the manufacturer found the bug first. The margin between a routine software update and a multi-car pileup on a dark interstate was not engineering redundancy. It was luck with a deadline. Somewhere in Wolfsburg, a QA engineer is having a very good week, and 49,958 American drivers will never know how close the coin came to landing the other way.

What to do with this: if you own a 2025 Tiguan, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls right now; VINs have been searchable since September 17, and the mailed owner letters do not go out until November 6.[1] Do not wait for the envelope. And file this one away as a general driving lesson for the software-defined era: if any modern car stalls at speed, your first move is hazards, but know that in a body-computer failure the hazards may not answer, so coast for the shoulder instead of stopping in the lane assuming you are visible, because visibility itself may be one of the casualties. When you shop for your next car, ask the uncomfortable question nobody at the dealership can answer: which of my safety systems share a single computer, and what happens when it reboots?
