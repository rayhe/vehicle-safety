A Jury Handed Him $1 Billion Over a Seatbelt. Then the Legal System Hit Undo.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 18, 2026

On November 11, 2017, Francis Amagasu tried to pass a car on a Bucks County road, lost control of his 1992 Mitsubishi 3000GT, hit three trees, and rolled. He was belted. His head found the roof anyway, fracturing his cervical spine. Quadriplegic. His 14-year-old son walked away with minor injuries.[1]

Six years later a Philadelphia jury awarded the family $1,009,969,395.32, including $800 million in punitive damages, over the 3000GT's seat belt and roof design. Last December, Pennsylvania's Superior Court threw the whole thing out. This week, Mitsubishi settled. Amount undisclosed. Nobody admitted anything.[2]

the verdict that died on a jury instruction: $800M of it was punitive damages, vacated because jurors were never told the one legal doctrine that mattered

The case rested on a small, terrible detail: the 3000GT's belt had a rip-stitch web loop designed to tear in a crash, trading four inches of slack for a gentler stop of your chest. In a rollover, slack is not gentle. It is four extra inches of travel toward a roof offering roughly three inches of head clearance. The loop ripped, the experts said; the body traveled; the head found the roof.[3]

Here is the paradox that ate the verdict whole: the belt did not cause the crash, and Amagasu's own lawyers never claimed it did. That triggers the crashworthiness doctrine: when the defect didn't start the accident, the jury must weigh which injuries a safer design would actually have prevented. Mitsubishi asked for that instruction. The trial judge refused, instead telling the jury a defective-from-sale belt made Mitsubishi liable for all harm from the restraint system. A 49-page Superior Court opinion called that refusal indefensible and ordered a new trial.[4]

Read that again: one of the largest auto liability verdicts in American history was awarded under a theory the plaintiffs never argued and the law did not allow. Mitsubishi's experts had testified the crash was violent enough to put his head in the roof with or without the web loop. Jurors never got to choose between the two versions, because the judge handed them a simpler question than the law permits.

So Mitsubishi won the appeal, then paid anyway. Companies do not settle after winning unless the retrial math is worse. In exchange for a vacated billion, the Amagasus took an undisclosed sum and an end to nine years of litigation. Justice was not so much produced as every alternative to it was exhausted.

Buried under the legal theater sits a consumer lesson. Nearly every defect case you will read about is a crashworthiness case: the car didn't cause the crash, it caused the second collision, your body against its interior. Energy-management belts are tuned for frontal crashes; roof clearance was an afterthought on 90s sports cars. Shopping a low-slung coupe? Check your seated head clearance and the belt design: three inches of roof plus a load-limiting belt is the exact combination this case fought over. And meeting federal standards is a floor, not a finding: the 3000GT met or exceeded every standard in effect when built, and it still cost a man his mobility and Mitsubishi a fortune.[5]
