There Are Two Ways for a Car to Kill You: Seventeen Models Do Both

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
November 9, 2026

Every death rate on this site is two numbers multiplied together, and nobody had bothered to pull them apart until now. Run FARS 2014–2023 across 337 models, 191,193 deaths in 336,202 fatal-crash involvements, and the fleet averages out to 1.6 fatal-crash involvements per 100 million miles, with 0.569 deaths per involvement, for a death rate of 0.91 per 100 million miles.[1] Two axes. One measures how often a car finds itself in a crash somebody dies in. One measures how often somebody dies once it does. Most cars are bad at one of them. Seventeen of the 91 models with enough crashes to judge are bad at both.

How often a Toyota Land Cruiser ends up in a fatal crash per mile, versus the fleet average — yet only 0.347 people die per Land Cruiser fatal crash, well below the 0.569 fleet average. (NHTSA FARS 2014–2023)

Start with the worst quadrant, because it earns the name. Chevrolet Cobalt: 6.32 fatal-crash involvements per 100 million miles, four times the fleet, and 0.808 deaths per involvement, which means four out of five Cobalt fatal crashes kill somebody. Multiply them and you get a death rate of 5.10, more than five times the fleet average, built from 1,540 deaths in 1,907 crashes. Hyundai Veloster sits beside it with the highest death rate in the entire dataset, 8.54, because it crashes 7.9 times as often as the fleet per mile and still kills in two out of three of those crashes. Ford Mustang: 5.4 times the crash frequency, 0.696 lethality, 2,739 dead. Chevrolet Impala: 4.6 times the frequency, 3,774 dead, the biggest body count in the quadrant after the Accord. Chevrolet S-10 pickup: 4.0 times the frequency and 0.755 lethality, the rare truck that is dangerous on both axes at once. Nissan Maxima, Toyota Solara, Chevrolet Camaro, Ford Ranger, Chevrolet Trailblazer, Ford Taurus, Ford Focus, Honda Civic, GMC Envoy, Buick LeSabre. Seventeen names. Every one of them crashes more often than the fleet and kills more often per crash. That is not one problem; it is two problems that happen to share a badge.[1]

Now the axis most people never see, because a single death rate hides it. Chevrolet Cavalier killed 0.857 people per fatal crash, 1,225 deaths in 1,429 crashes, the highest lethality of any model with 500 or more fatal crashes, followed by Dodge Neon at 0.856, Buick LeSabre at 0.823, and Chevrolet HHR at 0.801. Read those numbers slowly, because when a Cavalier is in a fatal crash, somebody dies roughly six times out of seven. Yet the Cavalier's overall death rate is only 2.43, barely two and a half times the fleet, because it crashes just 1.8 times as often per mile. Its rate looks moderate; its crashes are not. Same story for the Neon at 1.50 and the Grand Am at 1.18, a car whose 0.774 lethality is buried under a crash frequency so ordinary that the rate barely registers. Single-number rankings flatter these cars, but split the rate and the flattery stops.[1]

Then the quadrant that breaks the intuition entirely. Toyota Land Cruiser ends up in a fatal crash 11.3 times as often per mile as the average car. Eleven times. Its death rate is 6.27, the third-highest in the dataset, and every instinct says the truck must be a deathtrap. It is not, at least not in the way the rate suggests. Only 0.347 people die per Land Cruiser fatal crash, barely three-fifths of the fleet average of 0.569. Chevrolet Tracker tells the same story at 8.9 times the crash frequency and 0.549 lethality. Ford E-350, GMC Yukon, Chevrolet Tahoe, Chrysler 300, same pattern, different degrees: these vehicles find the crash constantly and walk away from it constantly. Their problem is frequency, not fragility, so fix the frequency and the rate collapses. Fix the structure and almost nothing changes, because the structure was never the problem.[1]

One car sits alone in the fourth quadrant, and it deserves a paragraph to itself. Kia Forte gets into a fatal crash at 0.37 times the fleet rate. It almost never shows up in this dataset. But when it does, 0.668 people die per crash, 17 percent above the fleet, for a death rate of just 0.40. It is the only model with 800 or more fatal-crash involvements that is rare in the crash and deadly in it. Could be a statistical edge case, or a crash-mix artifact, a higher share of single-vehicle high-speed events hiding inside a small sample of 904 crashes. Either way, it is the strangest cell in the whole table: a car that almost never appears in a fatal crash, yet one of the more lethal ones when it gets there.[1]

For completeness, the quadrant where you want to live. Ram 1500: 0.37 fatal-crash involvements per 100 million miles, 0.341 deaths per involvement, death rate 0.13, the lowest of any model with 800 or more fatal crashes, and Toyota RAV4 at 0.39 and 0.498, rate 0.19. Kia Sorento, Honda Pilot, Hyundai Tucson, Hyundai Santa Fe, Jeep Compass, Toyota Highlander, all under half the fleet on crash frequency and under 90 percent of the fleet on lethality. Nobody in this group is interesting, and that is the point. Interesting is what the other three quadrants are for.[1]

IIHS ran its own version of this split in July 2026, and the corroboration is worth noting. Its driver death rates, built from registrations rather than estimated miles, put the Kia Rio at the top with 170 driver deaths per million registration years against a fleet average of 37, and the Ram 3500 at the top of the other-driver list at 204 against an average of 53. IIHS also noted that the other-driver rate runs higher than the driver rate partly because the cars being hit tend to be older and less protective, which is the same exposure-versus-protection split this table draws, measured from the other side of the collision.[2]

Fairness requires the other side, stated at full strength, because this split is arithmetic, not destiny. A Cobalt's 0.808 lethality does not mean the Cobalt is built out of tissue paper. It means Cobalt fatal crashes disproportionately happen at high speed, on rural roads, with unbelted occupants, in single-vehicle events, and the crash mix may be doing most of the killing while the sheet metal takes the blame. A Mustang's 5.4-times crash frequency is a driver-selection effect as much as a vehicle effect: the car attracts the driver who will find the ditch, and if you swapped the drivers, the quadrants would move. VMT estimates are shakiest exactly where the numbers look wildest, low-volume models like the Land Cruiser and Tracker, so the top of the crash-frequency ranking should be read with one eyebrow raised. And the Forte singleton might be noise wearing a lab coat. None of that makes the table useless. It makes the table a map of where risk lives, car plus driver together, rather than a verdict on the metal alone.[1][3]

So what do you do with two numbers instead of one? If you are shopping used, look for the quadrant, not the score: low on both axes beats a good crash-test rating on one. Ram 1500, RAV4, Pilot, Tucson, Santa Fe, Highlander, Sorento, Compass. If you already own a both-bad car, a Cobalt, a Veloster, a Mustang, an Impala, an S-10, a Maxima, the risk is doubled and the fixes split the same way: the belt is the cheapest intervention on the lethality axis, and speed is the cheapest intervention on the frequency axis. Forte owners get the same advice with a sharper edge. And whatever you drive, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change. A recall will not move your car to a better quadrant. An open airbag or brake recall will make a bad quadrant worse.[4]

Limitations: Crash frequency here means fatal-crash involvements per 100M VMT, not all crashes; FARS only records fatal crashes, so a model with many non-fatal crashes and few fatal ones will look safer on this axis than it is. VMT figures are estimates from NHTS and public sales data, least reliable for low-volume models (Land Cruiser, Tracker, Veloster), so the top of the crash-frequency ranking carries the widest uncertainty. Both axes conflate driver and vehicle: crash frequency reflects driver behavior and crash mix as much as vehicle dynamics, and lethality reflects crash speed, road type, and belt use as much as structure. Quadrant thresholds (2× fleet frequency, 1.1× fleet lethality) are analytic choices, not regulatory lines; the 800-crash floor excludes small-sample models. See methodology for the full caveats.
