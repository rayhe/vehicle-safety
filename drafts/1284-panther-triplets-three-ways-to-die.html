The Gap

Same Platform, Three Different Ways to Die.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 26, 2026

Ford stopped building Panther-platform sedans in September 2011, when the last Crown Victoria rolled out of the St Thomas Assembly plant in Ontario. Fifteen years later, the three cars that shared that chassis are still dying at wildly different rates: the Mercury Grand Marquis was involved in 1,516 fatal crashes between 2014 and 2023, and 1,153 people died, at a rate of 2.29 deaths per 100 million miles.[1] Its Lincoln sibling, the Town Car, killed 260 people at 0.86 per 100 million miles. Same frame, same 4.6-liter V8 in their later years, same full-size body-on-frame sedan formula, and a 2.7-fold gap in death rates.

Grand Marquis death rate divided by Town Car death rate, 2014-2023

Between the two extremes sits the car everyone actually remembers: the Crown Victoria, with 881 deaths at 1.46 per 100 million miles. Police departments and cab fleets ran Crown Vics into the ground for decades, which makes its middle-of-the-pack rate interesting on its own. If hard fleet use explained everything, the workhorse should top the list, and it does not. Impairment does not explain the spread either: Grand Marquis drivers in fatal crashes tested impaired 18.5 percent of the time, against 22.3 percent for Crown Victoria drivers and 20.4 percent for Town Car drivers.[2] That is a 3.8-point spread trying to carry a 166 percent rate gap, and it collapses under the weight.

Now flip the table over and count not deaths per mile but deaths per fatal crash, meaning the share of fatal involvements in which the car's own occupant died. Town Car jumps to the top at 80.5 percent: 260 deaths across 323 fatal involvements. Grand Marquis follows at 76.1 percent, and the Crown Victoria, the car built for pursuits and cab shifts, trails at 65.3 percent. Read both rankings together and the story inverts. By rate, the Town Car looks like the safe Panther. By what actually happens inside the car when a crash turns fatal, it is the worst of the three.

Before crowning or condemning any of them, admit what this comparison cannot do. Same platform never meant same life: Crown Vics lived as cop cars and taxis, Town Cars lived as chauffeured livery cars, and Grand Marquis buyers skewed older, which means older, frailer bodies in the same crashes. Mileage estimates for discontinued cars carry real uncertainty, and fatal-crash data says nothing about the far larger pile of nonfatal crashes. Control for driver age, mileage, and crash context, and this 2.7-fold gap might shrink to almost nothing. FARS can describe the gap, but it cannot assign blame for it.

None of that makes the numbers useless to a buyer. If you are shopping the bottom of the used market, do not let the Town Car's 0.86 rate talk you into calling it safe: when one is in a fatal crash, its occupant dies four times out of five. Judge any Panther on condition, tires, brakes, and lights, check IIHS ratings for the exact model year, and run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands. These cars are 2011 technology at best, and no badge on the trunk changes that.
