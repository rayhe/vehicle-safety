We Ranked Every Carmaker by Death Rate. Tesla Won by a Mile. Chevrolet Finished Last.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 23, 2026

I took ten years of federal fatal-crash data, all 337 nameplates, and grouped them by the badge on the grille. One question: per mile driven, which automaker’s cars kill the fewest people? The answer is Tesla, at 0.06 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles. Last place among major automakers is Chevrolet at 1.37, a 23-fold gap.[1]

The obvious objection first, because you were already thinking it: Tesla’s fleet is young. Every Tesla on the road was built after 2012, and the Model 3 and Model Y, which make up 91 percent of Tesla’s fleet in this data, launched in 2017 and 2020. Meanwhile the average American car is 12.6 years old.[4] New cars carry electronic stability control, automatic emergency braking, and two decades of structural progress that a 2008 Cobalt simply does not have. Some of this gap is youth, and some of it is money: Tesla buyers skew wealthy, and wealth predicts survival independent of the badge.

Youth does not explain all of it, because I checked whether the mileage estimates were doing the work, and the implied driving comes out near 12,000 miles per vehicle per year for every make on the list, Tesla included.[1] The gap survives the audit. Chevrolet’s problem is also specific rather than general: its trucks are unremarkable (the Silverado rates 1.25, near the national middle), while its dead sedans do the damage. The Cobalt rates 5.10 and the Impala 5.00 with 3,774 deaths. Chevrolet plus Ford account for 70,859 deaths, more than a third of the entire dataset. Nobody’s showroom looks like its FARS footprint, but Chevy’s is a graveyard of 2000s sedans.

Now the part Tesla’s critics will clip: brand-level fatality rate is not Autopilot safety. NHTSA has open investigations covering roughly 2.9 million Teslas over Full Self-Driving failures at traffic controls, and the agency has identified at least 13 fatal crashes involving Autopilot.[5] A brand can top this ranking while a specific assistance feature kills people in edge cases, and both things are true at once. Note also the Model S, Tesla’s oldest and fastest car, rates 0.50, more than eight times the brand average. Youth cuts both ways, and as the fleet ages, expect the number to rise.

For anyone cross-shopping used cars, the badge matters less than the birth year and the model: a 2018 Model 3 (0.05) and a 2012 Impala (5.00) sit roughly a factor of one hundred apart per mile. When a brand brags about safety, ask for the fleet age and the sedan mix, not the marketing. Whatever you drive, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change.[6]

What this does not prove, stated plainly. FARS records deaths, not crashes and not injuries, so a brand can post a low fatality rate while crashing often if its cars protect occupants well. The rates use VMT estimates rather than odometer readings, which adds uncertainty for low-volume makes. Wealth, geography, and driver age all confound this ranking, and none of them are controlled for here. This is a cross-tabulation, not a controlled experiment. It is, however, a cross-tabulation nobody had run.
