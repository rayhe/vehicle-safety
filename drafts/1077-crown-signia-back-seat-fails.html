The Back Seat Failed the Test Your Car Was Supposed to Ace

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 5, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a frontal crash, because the 2026 Toyota Crown Signia just flunked them. Last year this wagon was an IIHS Top Safety Pick[9]; this year it earned no award at all, though the sheet metal did not change. What changed is that the test finally put a dummy in the seat where your kid rides, and the dummy did not fare well. According to IIHS’s own real-world research, belted rear-seat passengers in modern vehicles face a 46 percent higher risk of fatal injury than belted front-seat passengers.[2]

46%
Higher risk of fatal injury for belted rear-seat passengers vs. front-seat passengers in model year 2007 and newer vehicles (IIHS)

Here is the mechanism, and it is brutally simple. In the Crown Signia’s updated moderate overlap test (IIHS test CEF2621), the driver dummy scored Good on every single measure: head, neck, chest, legs, restraint kinematics. But the rear dummy, sized like a small woman or an average 12-year-old, submarined: the lap belt slid off the pelvic bone and rode up onto the soft tissue of the abdomen during maximum deceleration. That gave the car Marginal rear-passenger kinematics, Acceptable rear chest, and Marginal overall. One seat, one belt, one award gone.[1]

Before you blame Toyota, look at the pattern. Both the 2026 Chevrolet Traverse and the GMC Acadia were knocked off the Top Safety Pick list this year with merely “acceptable” results in the same updated test, and the 2026 BMW 3 Series managed “acceptable” there too. The 2026 Hyundai Palisade earned Top Safety Pick+, but only for vehicles built after November 2025, when Hyundai revised the rear seat belt buckles. Buy an earlier-build Palisade and you are ineligible for the badge on the window sticker.[4][5][7]

This is not a mystery of physics; it is a ledger of priorities. IIHS updated its signature test in 2022 specifically because front-seat safety had sprinted ahead while the rear stood still. Up front you get crash tensioners that tighten the belt the instant a crash begins, plus force limiters that spool webbing out to spare your ribs. In the back, fewer than half of new vehicles carry any advanced restraint system at all. Marcy Edwards, the IIHS engineer who led the test update, calls adapting proven front-row tech to the rear “an opportunity to make big gains in a short time.” Mazda proved her right: the CX-5 went from Poor to Good in this exact test after Mazda added rear pretensioners and load limiters.[2][6]

So here is what you actually do with this. If you are shopping for a 2026 family vehicle, ignore last year’s award badges and look up the updated moderate overlap rating for the specific model year you are buying, on iihs.org. Ask the dealer whether the second-row belts have pretensioners and load limiters, not just the front. If you already own a Crown Signia, Traverse, Acadia, or a pre-November-2025 Palisade, know that your most vulnerable passengers sit in the least-improved seats in the car, and keep lap belts low across the hips, where they belong.
