# Research — #946: The Sedan Is America's Deadliest Body Style. The SUV Is Its Safest.

## Angle
Class-level death accounting from FARS 2014–2023 (337 models, 191,193 occupant deaths).
Nobody on the site has ranked body styles by **deaths per registered vehicle** or
**deaths per mile driven** — the two denominators that answer "which body style is
most dangerous to own/drive." The occupant-death-ratio (aggressivity) angle is
thoroughly covered (who-dies-weight-class-hierarchy, self-protective-kill-ratio,
aggressor-vehicles); this is the mirror question: absolute occupant risk per unit
of exposure.

## Core finding
| Class | Deaths | Share of deaths | Share of fleet | Deaths/1,000 veh | Per 100M VMT |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sedan | 89,127 | 46.6% | 32.5% | **1.60** | **1.39** |
| SUV | 46,442 | 24.3% | 39.6% | **0.68** | **0.55** |
| Pickup | 41,593 | 21.8% | 20.3% | 1.20 | 0.90 |
| Van | 7,897 | 4.1% | 6.0% | 0.77 | 0.65 |
| Sports Car | 6,134 | 3.2% | 1.7% | 2.15 | 2.69 |

- Sedans: **2.34x** the SUV death rate per registered vehicle; **2.53x** per mile driven.
- Sedans are 32.5% of the registered fleet but 46.6% of deaths. SUVs are 39.6% of
  the fleet but only 24.3% of deaths.
- If sedans matched the SUV per-vehicle death rate: 89,127 − (fleet_sedan × 0.684/1000)
  = **51,077 fewer deaths over the decade ≈ 5,100/year**.
- Sports cars are the true outlier (2.15/1,000 veh), but they're 1.7% of the fleet —
  a niche risk, not a population one.

## Confounders checked and rejected
- **Impairment:** class-level driver impairment is nearly identical — Sedan 20.4%,
  SUV 19.5%, Pickup 20.1%, Van 18.1%, Sports Car 22.5% (driver-weighted from
  FARS_TOXICOLOGY). Drunk drivers don't explain a 2.3x gap.
- **Fleet age:** death-weighted median model year of the death vehicle — Sedan 2007,
  SUV 2006, Pickup 2004. Sedans' death vehicles are *newer* than pickups' and about
  even with SUVs'. Age runs the wrong way to explain the gap.
- **Exposure (miles):** the gap survives per-mile normalization (2.53x), so it's not
  just that sedans are driven more.

## What likely explains it
Mass. The site's own weight-class-hierarchy piece and IIHS's size/weight research:
below ~4,000 lbs, each 500 lbs of curb weight buys the occupant a large reduction
in death risk. Sedans cluster below that line; SUVs cluster above it. The cruel
symmetry: the mass that shields SUV occupants is the same mass that endangers
everyone around them (see occupant-pedestrian-zero-sum, aggressor-vehicles).

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? Yes — inverts the dominant "SUVs are the danger" narrative
  from the occupant's perspective, with a 2.3–2.5x ratio across two independent
  denominators and a 5,100/year excess-death quantification.
- Novel angle? Yes — no prior story computed class-level per-vehicle or per-mile
  death rates; existing class pieces used occupant death *ratio* (a different metric).
- **Verdict: PROCEED.**

## Limitations (for the article)
- "Deaths" = occupant deaths of the listed make/model (FARS vehicle.csv DEATHS field);
  "crashes" = fatal crashes involving the model. Rates use estimated VMT, not odometer
  readings — ±15% uncertainty for low-volume models.
- FARS captures fatal crashes only (~36k deaths/yr vs ~6.7M total crashes). A class
  with low fatality rates can still have high injury rates.
- Demographic confounders (sedans skew younger and cheaper; driver age/income not in
  this dataset) are not controlled. Impairment and fleet-age were checked and don't
  explain the gap, but unmeasured confounders remain.
- The excess-death figure (5,100/yr) is arithmetic, not causal: it assumes the sedan
  fleet could attain SUV-class mass/protection without changing who drives sedans.
- Says nothing about danger *to others* — pickups/SUVs remain far more lethal to
  pedestrians and crash partners (see this site's aggressivity coverage).

## Strongest counterargument
Mass is doing honest work here, not marketing. A 4,500-lb SUV genuinely protects its
occupants better than a 3,000-lb sedan in a two-vehicle crash — the physics is real
and the IIHS data agrees. "Buy the SUV" is also terrible societal advice: the same
mass externalizes risk onto pedestrians, cyclists, and sedan drivers, and above
~4,000 lbs the buyer's own marginal benefit nearly flatlines while the danger to
others keeps climbing. The article must say both.
