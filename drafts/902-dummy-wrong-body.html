America’s Newest Crash Test Dummy Weighs 108 Pounds. The Average American Woman Weighs 172.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 14, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. First the belt pretensioner fires, then the airbag deploys, while your body, riding Newton’s laws forward, loads the restraint system. Now here is the part that should embarrass everyone in this industry: the federal government has zero crash test dummies that look like the average American woman, whose body mass index sits at exactly 30.0, the CDC obesity threshold. After almost 15 years of research and development, the new advanced female dummy NHTSA is about to bless weighs 108 pounds and stands 4-foot-11, a BMI of 21.8. A body almost nobody in this country has.

108 lbs vs. 172 lbs
THOR-05F, the dummy of the future, vs. the average American woman it will never represent

Exquisite and grim. NHTSA’s 2026 Report to Congress says the agency is pushing hard to get the THOR-05F fifth-percentile female dummy and the WorldSID-05F side-impact dummy into federal rules, with Congress “anxiously awaiting” their arrival. In the same breath, NHTSA published a new injury study finding that a BMI of 30 or higher is “frequently associated with significantly higher injury risk, especially in frontal crashes” (the exact crash the new dummy was built to evaluate). Engineers built a better test device for the wrong body, and the data proving it came from the same building.

How did we get here? Partly by industry stall: in June 2025 the Alliance for Automotive Innovation lobbied NHTSA to hold off, arguing the THOR dummies were “still largely in a developmental phase,” and that September the agency agreed to delay its five-star safety rating overhaul, the one that would have folded pedestrian automatic emergency braking into the scores. Humanetics CEO Chris O’Connor’s line from the whole saga is the most honest sentence anyone has uttered: “We can’t even detect these injuries in a crash test dummy because the sensors aren’t there.” Current federal dummies don’t reflect physiological differences between male and female bodies, and they don’t even carry sensors in the lower legs, per the GAO, which is awkward because the lower legs are one of the injury types where women face the greatest excess risk.

U.S. adults age 20+ classified as obese (CDC, Aug 2021–Aug 2023) — a population with no federal test dummy at all

Now, full strength to the other side, because the data demands it. NHTSA’s own study found the male-female fatality gap collapsed from 18% to just 2.9% for 2015-through-2020 vehicles. Modern restraints, pretensioners, and smarter airbags largely closed the gender gap without a single new dummy. IIHS argues the money is better spent on virtual human-body models you can morph to any shape for the cost of compute, and the Insurance Institute currently has no plans to change the dummies in its own tests. Automakers have a point that rushing an immature device into 49 CFR part 572 could lock in bad injury criteria. And to be blunt: a heavier dummy is not automatically a better dummy. Biofidelity is about how the flesh and bones respond in the first 150 milliseconds, not the number on the scale.

That structural problem isn’t limited to women: the modern 50th-percentile man is about 30 pounds heavier than the federal male dummy, too. Nobody in the test matrix resembles the bodies actually rolling through American traffic. For decades the test matrix measured progress against a 1970s man and called it universal; now the fix measures progress against a 1970s woman and calls it equity. Same mistake, new mannequin.

What you can actually do, since the dummies won’t save you: for larger occupants, the specific failure mode is submarining, sliding under the lap belt in a frontal crash, because the belt cannot restrain a body it wasn’t shaped for. Keep the lap belt flat and low across the hips, never the belly, and skip the aftermarket belt adjusters and comfort padding, which corrupt the restraint geometry further. Shop 2020-and-newer model years, where the gender injury gap has measurably narrowed, and weight the IIHS moderate-overlap and side ratings, which are most sensitive to female-body injury risk. One policy lever exists: NHTSA will propose adding THOR-05F to part 572 after its 500-test validation round finishes, and that rulemaking takes public comment. It is the only input mechanism on this issue that has ever existed. Use it.

Limitations: FARS does not record occupant BMI, so the BMI-injury-risk link comes from NHTSA’s NASS-CDS/Crash Investigation Sampling System study, not our own data. And the 2.9% residual gender gap in 2015-2020 vehicles suggests much of the injury disparity is driven by restraint technology and vehicle age, not dummy design — the strongest evidence the dummy fight may be the wrong battle.
