The Two Safest States for Drunk Driving Make Every Convicted Drunk Driver Blow Into Their Car

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
June 29, 2027

According to the toxicology reports — and there are a lot of them — the safest place in America to be a drunk driver is New Jersey. A new analysis of five years of federal crash data puts the Garden State at the bottom of the national drunk-driving death table: 1.33 drivers killed over the limit per 100,000 licensed drivers. Montana sits at the top at 7.17. Do the division and a Montana driver is more than five times as likely to die drunk behind the wheel as a New Jersey driver.

Now look at the policy column. New Jersey passed Ricci's Law in December 2019: every DUI conviction, first offense included, means an ignition interlock — a breath tube wired into the ignition. The 2020–2024 study window is essentially the first full observation period of that law. New York sits at #2 (1.45) and has run the same play since 2010 — Leandra's Law mandates an interlock for every DWI conviction. These are the two states that make every convicted drunk driver blow into their car. They are also the two safest states. Coincidence is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.

Massachusetts takes #3 with the same medicine in a repeat-offender dose: Melanie's Law, on the books since 2005, forces interlocks on repeat offenders with a lifetime lookback — every prior OUI counts, no matter how old. The evidence behind the hardware is unusually strong: a CDC review of 15 studies found interlocks cut re-arrest rates by a median of 67 percent while installed, and all-offender laws are associated with 15 percent fewer alcohol-involved crash deaths — roughly 915 lives a year.

Before you carve this into a monument, read the fine print. New Jersey is the most densely populated state in the union; Montana is 48th. This ranking measures geography as much as policy: transit access, trip length, road speed. Utah — first state to a 0.05 BAC limit — just got a rigorous 2026 evaluation finding no detectable drop in alcohol-involved fatal outcomes. Statutes deter; hardware prevents — but only while it's installed: the CDC found re-arrest rates revert once the device comes out. Montana's problem may be culture and enforcement, not the absence of a mandate. The honest read: the ranking mostly measures geography, and the interlock correlation is the most actionable thing on top of it.

So here's the playbook, straight from the box score. First, check whether your state requires interlocks for first-time offenders — fewer than half do. If yours doesn't, take that gap to your legislators; the evidence base is as close to settled as traffic safety gets. Second, if you live in a Montana-class state — rural, long drives, no transit at 1 a.m. — get a keychain breathalyzer and budget for rideshares, because New Jersey's infrastructure doesn't exist where you are. Third: you don't need a conviction. Interlocks install aftermarket for about $75 to $100 plus a monthly fee — two orders of magnitude cheaper than a DUI, and the device mechanically prevents the exact crash this ranking measures. It doesn't care about your intentions, and that's the whole point.
