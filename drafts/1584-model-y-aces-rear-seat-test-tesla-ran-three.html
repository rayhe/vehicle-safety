The Gap

The Best-Selling EV Just Aced the Test That Sank the RAV4. Tesla Ran Three of the Four Crashes Itself.

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Editor
November 25, 2026

On September 3, 2026, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety handed its highest award to a car it had barely finished testing. The redesigned 2026 Tesla Model Y earned Top Safety Pick+, the institute's top tier, with Good ratings in every crash test that counts, both crash prevention tests, and both headlight variations. That is the news, and the interesting part is which test decided it. In 2026, a Good rating in the updated moderate overlap front test is required for either IIHS award, and that test, the one that puts a dummy in the back seat for the first time, is the test that sank the Toyota RAV4, the Honda CR-V, and the RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid this year. Model Y passed it with the rear dummy scoring Good on every single measure.[1]

Start with the test itself, because the award is really about one dummy. In 2022, IIHS added a second Hybrid III dummy to the moderate overlap front test, seated directly behind the driver and sized like a small woman or an average 12-year-old. Institute research says belted rear-seat occupants in model year 2007 and newer vehicles face a 46 percent higher risk of fatal injury than belted front-seat occupants. Not because the back seat got more dangerous. Because the front seat got pretensioners, load limiters, and airbags while the back seat kept the same belts it had in 2007. Fewer than half of new vehicles carry advanced restraints in the rear. Marcy Edwards, the IIHS senior research engineer who developed the updated test, calls adapting proven front-seat technology to the rear an opportunity to make big gains in a short time. Four years after she said it, the test finally has teeth: for 2026, a Good rating in the updated moderate overlap is the price of admission to either award, and cars that used to collect trophies on front-seat performance alone are now failing in public.[6]

Run four family SUVs through that test and the segment splits in half. Model Y: Good overall, rear passenger restraints and dummy kinematics Good, rear chest Good, with the Subaru Forester matching it and the Toyota RAV4 Hybrid managing only Acceptable. Honda CR-V: Poor, with the institute noting that the rear dummy's shoulder belt moved too far upward toward its neck, compromising the restraint. RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid, tested separately from the hybrid: Marginal, with rear-seat restraints and dummy kinematics Poor, in the same test, the same year, with the same dummy. Every one of these cars protects its driver well. What separates the winners from the losers is the row where your kids sit.[2][5]

Now the part Tesla would rather you skip. Three of the four crash tests behind this award were conducted by Tesla, not by IIHS. Driver-side small overlap (test ID VTN2608), passenger-side small overlap (VTP2609), and the updated side test (VTS2608) all carry the same note on the IIHS ratings page: ratings assigned by the Institute based on a test conducted by Tesla. That is not a secret, and it is not a scandal. It is the verified manufacturer testing program, running since 2006, under which automakers submit video, photos, intrusion data, and dummy sensor traces from production-representative vehicles, IIHS scores the data itself, and IIHS runs occasional audit tests to check the homework. What makes the Model Y case worth stating plainly is the shape of it. Those three tests are exactly the three crash tests IIHS allows manufacturers to submit. A fourth test exists, and it is the one that matters.[2][3]

IIHS policy, published in August 2025, is blunt about the boundary: the institute does not accept manufacturer tests for the moderate overlap front 2.0 evaluation or for headlights, with no exceptions and no audit shortcut. That means the updated moderate overlap result on the Model Y page, test ID CEF2625, is an IIHS result, produced in the institute's own lab. Its rear dummy took the hit with no head contact, a chest index of 32, and shoulder belt tension of 4.2 kilonewtons. All Good. Front-seat numbers on that page are Tesla's, and back-seat numbers are IIHS's. And the back seat is the seat the award is about.[2][3]

Put plainly, the award survives its own disclosure. If Tesla had run the moderate overlap test, the award would rest on the manufacturer's word, and it does not. One test in four is independent, and it happens to be the test that decides the award and the test where the Model Y's rivals keep failing. That is not a coincidence worth ignoring, and it is not a loophole worth pretending does not exist. It is the system working the way IIHS designed it: let manufacturers certify the tests they have run for twenty years, keep the new test in-house, and let the rear dummy sort the field.

Real-world data backs the lab up, and the numbers are almost indecent. In our FARS data covering 2014 through 2023, the Model Y posts a fatality rate of 0.03 deaths per 100 million miles traveled, on 57 deaths across a fleet of 1.75 million vehicles. That is the lowest rate in our 337-model dataset. RAV4 sits at 0.19, more than six times higher. Forester sits at 0.26, nearly nine times higher. CR-V sits at 0.53, almost eighteen times higher. Model Y drivers are also unusually sober for a fatal-crash population: 17.6 percent any impairment in the toxicology data, with 13.2 percent alcohol and 7.3 percent drug positive. A car that protects its occupants in the lab and carries the lowest death rate on the road is a rare combination. It is also a combination that flatters the buyer as much as the car. Model Y owners skew newer, wealthier, and better equipped with driver assistance than the average driver, and the FARS data covers the pre-redesign car, model years 2020 through 2023, not the Juniper that just won the award.[4]

Two marks on the page are not Good, and both are instructive. Seat belt reminders earned Marginal, because the front alert lasts only 8 to 89 seconds and the second row has no startup status alert at all, while child seat anchors earned Acceptable. Neither counts toward the award, which is why the award looks the way it does: IIHS decided years ago that nagging chimes and LATCH hardware would not gate the top tier. But the reminder rating is the one blemish Tesla could fix with software alone. Lengthen the chime, add a second-row startup alert, and the page goes clean without a single bolt turning, though Tesla has not said it will. The institute's page is the only pressure that exists, and it is not much.[2]

None of this makes the award bulletproof, and the case against taking it at face value deserves its full weight. Three of four crash tests were run by the seller. Detailed data behind those tests is submitted confidentially and destroyed after IIHS assigns the rating, so no outsider can re-run Tesla's numbers. FARS rate covers a different car, the pre-redesign Model Y, and a different population, the early-adopter buyers who could afford a $50,000 electric SUV in 2021. Award applies only to 2026 and 2027 model years, so a used 2023 Model Y is a different vehicle with a different record. And a Top Safety Pick+ is a lab result, not a promise: the safest car in the test can still be totaled by a drunk driver running a red light, and the Model Y's own crash prevention tests, Good as they are, were run against IIHS targets on a closed course, not against a texting driver drifting across the center line. None of that makes the award wrong; it makes it bounded. Trust the moderate overlap result completely, and trust the other three as much as you trust the program.[2][3]

For context, the Model Y was not the only winner that day. The redesigned 2027 Kia Telluride earned Top Safety Pick+ in the same September 3 round, though only for vehicles built after June 2026. That build-date qualifier is the tell for the whole 2026 award year. For 2026, IIHS made a Good rating in the updated moderate overlap a requirement for either award, not just the plus, and the cars that fell off the list this year, the Crown Signia, the Traverse and Acadia, the RAV4, the CR-V, all fell off at the same place: the back seat. The Model Y and the Telluride are the cars that fixed the back seat first. Everyone else is now doing the same homework, a year late.[1][7]

What to do with this. If you are shopping for a family SUV and anyone ever sits in the back seat, check the updated moderate overlap rating specifically. Not the old test, not a 2025 award, and not a five-star NHTSA rating, which does not put a dummy in the rear seat during frontal crashes at all. Model Y and Forester earn Good there, the RAV4 earns Acceptable, the CR-V earns Poor, and the RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid earns Marginal. That single rating is the difference between the 2026 award winners and the rest of the segment, and it is the only rating on the page that measures what happens to your kids. If you own a 2026 RAV4 or CR-V, the weak point is the rear seat in a frontal crash: keep lap belts low on the hips, put the most vulnerable passengers in the best-protected row, and do not let a dealer show you a 2025 Top Safety Pick badge for a car that does not hold it in 2026. If you are buying a used Model Y, the award applies to 2026 and 2027 cars only, so check the model year on the IIHS ratings page before you pay for the badge. And if you already own a 2026 Model Y, buckle up in the back seat anyway, because the reminder chime that is supposed to nag you about it lasts eight seconds on a good day, and the car that aced the crash test will not remind you to survive it.
