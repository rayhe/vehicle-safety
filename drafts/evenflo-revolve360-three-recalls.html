Evenflo Has Recalled the Same Car Seat Three Times in One Year. The First Fix Was Tape.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
July 24, 2026

Evenflo’s Revolve360 rotating car seat was supposed to be the clever one. Swivels 360 degrees so you can load your toddler without wrenching your back, converts from rear-facing to forward-facing. It has now been recalled three times in twelve months, covering more than 383,000 units across three separate failure modes.

383,000+
Revolve360 seats recalled in 12 months: foam, crash compliance, and headrest shift

Start the clock in July 2025. Transport Canada flagged the Revolve360 Slim for failing chest acceleration requirements when installed rear-facing with the seat belt and tether.[1] Eighteen thousand seats, pulled because a child restraint did not restrain the child’s chest within regulatory limits during a crash.

Two months later, September 2025: 324,000 Revolve360 Slim seats recalled in the U.S. because children could reach behind the cushion, pick off pieces of headrest foam, and choke on them.[2] Eleven reports filed, and eight of those children had already swallowed the foam. Evenflo’s remedy for a choking hazard in a child safety device was to mail parents a strip of adhesive tape.

Tape. For a car seat.

Then this week, July 2026: another 59,661 Revolve360 seats recalled because the headrest can shift position during a crash when raised to its two highest settings, discovered during dynamic testing with a three-year-old-sized crash dummy.[3] This time the remedy is listed as “under development.” If your child is tall enough to need positions 10 or 11, Evenflo says to stop using the seat entirely and call their hotline during business hours, Monday through Friday.

Three recalls, three distinct mechanical failures, one product line: a crash compliance failure in Canada, a choking hazard patched with tape in the U.S., and now a headrest that moves when it matters most, with no fix ready, leaving parents of taller toddlers with a seat they bought specifically so their growing kid could keep using it.

If the company name sounds familiar, it should. In February 2020, ProPublica published an investigation revealing that Evenflo had marketed its Big Kid booster seat as “SIDE IMPACT TESTED” while internal test videos showed child-sized dummies being thrown completely out of their shoulder belts during simulated T-bone collisions, a motion Evenflo’s own lead engineer admitted under oath could cause catastrophic head, neck, and spinal injuries or death in a real child.[4] Evenflo’s passing standard for those tests, as a company technician explained in a deposition, was whether the dummy ended up on the floor or the seat broke apart. That was it.

A five-year-old girl named Jillian Brown suffered what doctors call internal decapitation in a 2016 crash while strapped into a Big Kid booster, weighing just 37 pounds. A congressional investigation followed, concluding that Evenflo’s self-designed test standards were “grossly inadequate” and that the company’s safety marketing was “unfair and deceptive.”[5]

Congress mandated NHTSA to create side-impact testing standards for child seats back in 2000, in the TREAD Act. Twenty-six years later, those standards still do not exist.[6]

What to do

If you own a Revolve360 of any variant, check your model number against all three recall campaigns at nhtsa.gov/recalls. For the July 2026 headrest recall, model numbers start with CS260212 and include Brookline, Lenox, Holden, Nantucket, Danvers, and Chelsea colorways. If your child currently uses headrest positions 10 or 11 in forward-facing mode or position 11 in rear-facing mode, stop using the seat in those positions immediately and contact Evenflo at 1-800-233-5921.

More broadly: rotating car seats add mechanical complexity that fixed-shell seats do not have, and that complexity creates failure modes. Three recalls in a year from a company with this track record is not a coincidence; it is a pattern, and your child is not a beta tester.

Limitations

This article tracks the recall history of one product line from one manufacturer. It does not claim rotating car seats are inherently less safe than fixed-shell seats across the industry, and other manufacturers’ rotating seats may have clean records. The ProPublica investigation concerned a different product (the Big Kid booster), and Evenflo has stated its seats meet or exceed applicable federal standards. NHTSA recall reports confirm no injuries have been reported in connection with the July 2026 headrest recall specifically.

Counterargument

Evenflo could reasonably argue that multiple recalls demonstrate a functioning safety system: the company is identifying issues through testing and voluntarily reporting them. That is true, but a functioning recall system and a functioning product are different things, and parents shopping for a seat that will protect a three-year-old in a crash should not have to parse that distinction three times in one year.
