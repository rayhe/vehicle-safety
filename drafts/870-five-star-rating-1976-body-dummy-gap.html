Your Five-Star Safety Rating Was Earned by a 1976 Body

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 10, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. The crash test dummy that earned your car's five-star safety rating is shaped like the median American man of 1976: five-foot-nine, 171 pounds.[1] Today's median man stands the same height and weighs 193 pounds. Thirty pounds of American has appeared since the dummy was cast, and no federal test accounts for a single one of them.

40.3%
of American adults are classified as obese; the federal dummy's BMI is 25.2.

Dr. Jingwen Hu of the University of Michigan ran the same 35-mph barrier crash four times, each with a different body.[1] His 1976 man met the airbag perfectly, exactly as designed. A short woman with a BMI of 40 drove her knees into the steering column. A six-foot-three man folded around the wheel and struck his head on the dashboard. One crash, one car, three injury outcomes, because every restraint in the vehicle was tuned for exactly one of the four bodies.

Federal rules require new cars to protect two bodies: an average-sized adult male and a small-sized adult female, both in the front seat.[1] Nobody taller, nobody heavier, nobody among the 72 percent of adults above a healthy BMI. Humanetics builds the dummies that could fix this, 150 sensors each, and sells them to automakers testing cars for other countries. Asked why American regulations don't use them, the company's CEO was blunt: "there's just little incentive to use them."

NHTSA's own endorsed replacement, the THOR dummy, was supposed to enter federal testing in 2019.[1] It slipped to 2022, then 2027, and the current proposal is the fourth quarter of 2029: a full decade of slippage, while Europe's NCAP adopted THOR dummies back in 2020. Along the way the auto industry's trade group told NHTSA the new dummies remain "largely in a developmental phase" and proposed sticking with the old ones, and in September 2025 the agency delayed its five-star program updates at the industry's request. NHTSA's 2026 report to Congress finally urged "swift implementation." The Center for Auto Safety calls the federal program "on life support."

Your car is not unsafe because of this. Traffic deaths hit a record low of 36,640 in 2025, and the old dummy still catches plenty of real danger.[1] But a five-star rating certifies protection for two body types, and yours probably isn't one of them. Until the 2029 update arrives, if it survives the next delay, cross-shop the IIHS Top Safety Pick list, which modernizes its tests far faster than the feds.[2] Sit upright, keep the belt low across your hips, and don't recline into the airbag's path. Fifty years of American bodies changed; the test never did.
