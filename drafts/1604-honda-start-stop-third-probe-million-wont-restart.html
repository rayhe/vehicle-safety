Your Honda Shuts Its Engine Off to Save Gas. 701 Owners Say It Never Turned Back On.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

Picture the scene, because 701 Honda and Acura owners have lived it. You stop at a red light, your engine cuts out exactly as designed, saving a thimbleful of fuel while you wait, and then the light turns green, you lift your foot off the brake, and nothing happens. No cough, no crank, no restart, just a silent three-row SUV sitting in a live intersection while the cars behind you discover how patient they are not. On November 13, 2026, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Engineering Analysis EA26007 into that exact failure, covering an estimated 1,033,632 model-year 2024–2026 Honda Odyssey, Passport, Ridgeline and Pilot vehicles, plus the Acura MDX.[1][2]

Federal probes into the same Honda start-stop failure since 2022

Three probes, same symptom, four and a half years. That count is the story, because a single investigation is a question and three is a pattern. NHTSA first opened a Preliminary Evaluation in June 2022, covering 194,731 Pilots from 2016 to 2020, after 1,045 reports. It upgraded the case to Engineering Analysis EA25004 in March 2025, widening the net to about 2.2 million vehicles across six nameplates. Now the newest cars get a case of their own, and the reason NHTSA split them off is the most interesting sentence in the whole file: the 2024–2026 vehicles appear to have different root causes and respond differently to repairs.[4][8]

Read that again slowly, because Honda spent three years fixing this failure in the old cars, and federal regulators looked at the new cars, saw the same failure, and concluded the old fix does not apply. Same symptom, different cause, different repair, and a regulator does not write that sentence lightly, nor does it write it unless the evidence forced its hand.

Run the complaint math, because the numbers refuse to flatter anyone. EA26007 opened with 701 complaints against roughly 1,033,632 vehicles, about one complaint for every 1,475 cars. EA25004 opened with 1,348 reports against about 2,209,466 vehicles, about one in 1,639, which works out to nearly identical rates. Redesigned transmissions, new model years, a fresh round of service bulletins, and the failure shows up at the same frequency anyway. Whatever changed under the hood between the old cars and the new ones, and plenty did, the 2024 Pilot runs a second-generation 10-speed automatic with shift-by-wire where the older investigation describes a nine-speed, it did not change the odds of getting stranded at a light.[4]

And stranded is the right word, because of who drives these cars. A Pilot is not a weekend toy, and neither is an Odyssey, a Passport, or an MDX. These are the default answers for American families who need three rows of seats, which means the drivers behind the wheel stop at school crossings and left-turn lanes all day long, in traffic, with kids in the back. A no-restart at a red light is not a driveway inconvenience. It is a dead vehicle in a live lane, and the only thing standing between that driver and the car behind them is a stranger's brake pedal.

So far, the new file lists no crashes, no injuries, no fires, no deaths. Say that plainly, because it matters, and then say the rest of it. Sibling file EA25004, the older probe into the same symptom, includes four crashes, two of which resulted in injuries.[6] Absence of harm in the new case is a snapshot, not a verdict, because investigations open with complaints and close with data.

The fix that shrinks the feature

Honda has been chasing this failure since 2023, when it issued service bulletins 23-008 and 23-009 with a two-stage countermeasure, stage one a fuel-injection software update, and if that did not cure the problem, stage two replaced the starter assembly and starter relays and adjusted the valves, depending on the model.[2][5] NHTSA upgraded the case to an Engineering Analysis in March 2025 anyway, because reports kept arriving, many from owners who said the countermeasures had already been done. A repair program that does not stop the reports is not a repair program; it is a waiting room.

One Honda bulletin from that era deserves a closer read than it got. Service Bulletin 23-027, for the Odyssey, explained that when the 12-volt battery nears the end of its life, the engine can resist turning more than the starter can push at certain piston positions. A stop-start restart is harder than a morning cold start: while the engine sits off, the battery keeps running the climate control and the screens with no alternator help, and then the starter has to spin a hot engine that stopped wherever it happened to stop. Park a piston near the top of its compression stroke and the starter is pushing against full compression with a tired battery behind it. Honda's fix, in part, was more software that reduced how often idle-stop engages.[4] Stop and think about what that means. Part of the remedy for the fuel-saving feature was to use the fuel-saving feature less.

Two weeks before NHTSA opened EA26007, Honda issued fresh service bulletins, 26-090 for Honda and 26-019 for Acura, addressing start/stop no-restart problems, including difficulty starting in hot ambient temperatures. Honda also told dealers there is a shortage of replacement parts, so vehicles already experiencing the problems get priority for repairs.[3] Credit the timing: a manufacturer issuing new bulletins before the probe opens is a manufacturer acting, not stalling. But a parts shortage means the priority queue is also a waiting list, and everyone else waits for the part that fixes the car that already stranded them once.

Meanwhile, the feature itself fights the owner: Auto Idle Stop has an OFF button, and pressing it works, for that drive. Start the car again and the system switches itself back on, so every single trip begins with the same small ritual or with the engine shutting itself off at the first red light. Honda built a system that cannot be left off and cannot always be restarted, and there is no permanent off, so owners get the worst of both.

The strongest case against this article

Seven hundred complaints across a million cars is 0.07 percent. No crashes, no injuries, no deaths in this file. When the engine fails to restart, the owner restarts it or gets a jump, and the car goes back to being a car. Honda issued new service bulletins two weeks before the probe opened, which is exactly what a responsible manufacturer does, and it told Reuters it is aware of the investigation and will continue cooperating with NHTSA. A third probe into the same symptom could just as easily be read as the system working: complaints arrive, the agency opens a file, the manufacturer cooperates, and the slow machinery of defect investigation grinds forward. Fair. Genuinely fair.

But that defense answers a question this article is not asking. Nobody is claiming 701 complaints are a public health emergency. What those complaints prove, stacked across three federal cases over four and a half years, is that a defect survived its own repair program and reappeared in the redesigned cars at the same rate, for reasons the regulator admits are different. A failure that outlives its fix is not a closed file. It is a pattern, and patterns are what investigations are for.

What this article does not prove

EA26007 is an investigation, not a recall and not a defect finding. NHTSA has made no determination that the start-stop system is defective, and this article makes none either. Population of 1,033,632 comes from Reuters, citing NHTSA; the agency's own opening resume gives no vehicle count. Complaint counts differ by source: 701 per the NHTSA opening resume, 700 per Reuters, which rounded. "Different root causes" is NHTSA's characterization in the opening resume, and the agency has not published what those causes are, so any claim about which part fails in the 2024–2026 cars would be invention. Complaints are self-reported and undercount real failures, so the one-in-1,475 rate I calculated is a floor, not a ceiling, and the true failure rate is higher by an unknown amount. Finally, the 2023 two-stage fix applied to the older population, and whether it helps the new cars is exactly what NHTSA says is in doubt.

If you own a 2024–2026 Pilot, Odyssey, Passport, Ridgeline or MDX, press the Auto Idle Stop OFF button every time you start the car, because the system re-enables itself at every ignition cycle and there is no set-and-forget. If your engine has ever failed to restart after auto stop, file a complaint with NHTSA at nhtsa.gov or through the Vehicle Safety Hotline at 1-888-327-4236. EA26007 exists because 701 owners did exactly that; the complaint count is what opened the case, and your complaint is what keeps it honest. Ask your dealer about Honda service bulletin 26-090 or Acura 26-019, issued October 30, 2026. Honda says replacement parts are short and vehicles already experiencing the problem get priority, so get on the list before yours strands you, not after. And if you are shopping for a used 2024 or 2025 Pilot or MDX, know that it sits in two open federal investigations at once. Check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, ask the seller whether the 2023 countermeasures were performed, and know that NHTSA says those countermeasures may not fix the new cars anyway.
