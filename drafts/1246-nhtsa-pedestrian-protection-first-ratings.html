The Gap

NHTSA Finally Rates Cars on How They Hit Pedestrians. 102 Models Passed. Not One Full-Size Pickup.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
December 10, 2027

The government has spent fifty years rating how well your car protects you. On September 25, it finally rated how well it protects everyone else. 102 model-year 2027 vehicles earned the first-ever NHTSA pedestrian-protection checkmark, a federal grade for what happens to the person your car hits instead of the person inside it.[1]

60%
Minimum score to earn the checkmark: 21.6 of 36 points, a D-minus by classroom standards.

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. Federal technicians smash child and adult headforms into the hood and windshield, slam an upper-leg impactor into the hood's leading edge, and drive a full lower-limb rig into the bumper. Head impacts count for half the points, the legs for the rest. It is the first time the 5-Star program has measured the outside of the car, and the numbers deserve a close read.[2]

NHTSA set the passing bar at 60% while Europe demands 70%. That ten-point gap is the difference between a test that pressures automakers and one that flatters them, and NHTSA only published the 102 vehicles that passed. No scores, no rankings, and no list of who failed. You cannot tell a 21.6 from a 36.0, even though one car could be roughly two-thirds more protective than the other. A rating that hides its own math is a recommendation engine for nothing.[3]

Now look at who is missing. GHSA data show SUVs and pickups account for more than half of pedestrian fatalities. Ford's lone entry on NHTSA's list is the gas-powered Explorer, a unibody crossover. General Motors has nothing on it, and neither does Stellantis. Not one full-size pickup from any of the three Detroit brands earned the checkmark, which means the vehicles deadliest to pedestrians are the least represented in the first pedestrian ratings.[2][5]

What this means for you

Shopping for a 2027? Check nhtsa.gov/ratings for the pedestrian-protection checkmark, then check your exact trim, because NHTSA warns that not every trim of a listed model qualifies. A five-star occupant rating says nothing about what the car does to a person outside it. If your car predates 2027, no version of this rating exists for it, and the bluntest predictor of pedestrian harm remains front-end geometry: tall, stiff, and flat kills. And mind the calendar: this checkmark is a pilot that expires after model year 2027, with the new NCAP rating system beginning on the 2028s. Buy the rating, but know you are buying version 1.0.[1][3]

What it does not do

The tests use standardized impactors at fixed lab speeds, which model a limited set of strike geometries, not the turning car or the multi-impact pileup. NHTSA selected the models it assessed; absence from the list may mean untested rather than failed. This rating covers crashworthiness only, what happens on impact; whether the car brakes first is scored in a separate driver-assistance program covering ten 2027 models. And the real-world predictive power of a brand-new test is unproven; Europe's longer-running program is suggestive, not proof.[2][4]

The strongest case against it

The auto industry has a point. John Bozzella of the Alliance for Automotive Innovation says NHTSA rates new safety tech too slowly and too rarely to help buyers decide, and a binary checkmark published 21 months after the final rule does not rebut him. Engineers can also reasonably argue the test rewards softer front ends with real trade-offs in occupant protection, weight, and aerodynamics. Biggest of all: the test grades what happens when the car hits someone, not whether it stops. Most protective of all is the front end that never reaches the pedestrian. On that score, Europe has graded cars for years while Washington printed its first checkmarks last month.[2]
