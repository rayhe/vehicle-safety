## Story in one line
Between 2015 and 2024, deaths among 35-to-44-year-olds rose 32.8 percent, the fastest growth of any age group and 41 percent of the entire national increase, while the groups everyone worries about (21-to-24-year-olds, down 9.2 percent) actually got safer.

## Verified facts

1. **The totals.** 39,254 people died in U.S. traffic crashes in 2024, down 4.3 percent from 41,025 in 2023. The fatality rate fell to 1.19 per 100 million VMT from 1.26. It was the third straight year-to-year decrease in both deaths and rate since 2021. In 2015 the total was 35,484. Over the decade, deaths rose 3,770, or 10.6 percent. (NHTSA DOT HS 813 791; PlainRoadSafety FARS rebuild.)
2. **The age table, 2015 vs 2024 (fatal-person rows, FARS final files).** 25-34: 6,344 to 6,921 (+577, +9.1%). 35-44: 4,707 to 6,252 (+1,545, +32.8%). 55-64: 4,856 to 5,246 (+390, +8.0%). 45-54: 5,304 to 4,963 (-341, -6.4%). 65-74: 3,140 to 4,128 (+988, +31.5%). 75+: 3,097 to 3,891 (+794, +25.6%). 16-20: 3,147 to 3,276 (+129, +4.1%). 21-24: 3,464 to 3,146 (-318, -9.2%). Under 16: 1,345 to 1,235 (-110, -8.2%). Unknown age: 80 to 196 (+116). (PlainRoadSafety, from NHTSA FARS final national person files.)
3. **The 2024 one-year moves point the same way.** In 2024 vs 2023, fatalities in crashes involving young drivers (ages 15-20) fell 370 (-6.6 percent), while fatalities in crashes involving older drivers (65+) rose 115 (+1.4 percent), the only category NHTSA lists as increasing besides SUV occupant fatalities (+112, +1.6 percent). Alcohol-impaired-driving fatalities fell 478 (-3.9 percent) to 11,904; speeding-related fatalities fell 625 (-5.2 percent); unrestrained occupant fatalities fell 775 (-7.4 percent). (NHTSA DOT HS 813 791.)
4. **The 65+ fact sheet (final data).** 8,019 people 65 and older were killed in 2024, 20 percent of all traffic fatalities, up 1 percent from 7,911 in 2023. The 65+ population was 61.2 million (18 percent of the U.S. population). The 65+ fatality rate per 100,000 population was 13.11, down 2 percent from 13.33 in 2023. Over 10 years, 65+ fatalities rose 29 percent while the number of older licensed drivers rose 36 percent. Older drivers were 23 percent of all licensed drivers in 2024, up from 18 percent in 2015. There were 54.4 million licensed drivers 65+, up 36 percent from 2015. Of 55,620 drivers in fatal crashes in 2024, 8,484 were 65+ (15.3 percent). (NHTSA DOT HS 813 839; Book of Odds summary of Table 2.)
5. **The rate that undercuts the senior panic.** Drivers 65 and older were involved in fatal crashes at 15.60 per 100,000 licensed drivers in 2024, the lowest rate of any age group. Within 65+, the rate was highest for drivers 85 and older: 29.69 for men, 10.91 for women. (NHTSA DOT HS 813 839 Table 2, via Book of Odds.)
6. **The 2025 context.** NHTSA estimates 36,640 traffic deaths in 2025, down 6.7 percent from 2024, with a rate of 1.10 per 100M VMT, the second-lowest in recorded history. (NHTSA press release; already covered by #1469 — context only.)

## Original contribution: the decade decomposition

**88.2 percent of the increase sits in two groups.** The national death toll rose by 3,770 between 2015 and 2024. The 35-to-44 group added 1,545 (41.0 percent of the increase). Everyone 65 and older added 1,782 (47.3 percent). Together: 3,327 of 3,770, or 88.2 percent. Every other age group combined added 443 deaths, net. That is the whole story in one number: the decade's growth is a middle-aged story and a senior story, and almost nothing else.

**The biggest group is not the fastest-growing group.** 25-to-34-year-olds are still the largest single group (6,921 deaths in 2024), but they grew only 9.1 percent, slower than the national 10.6 percent, and their share of all deaths fell from 17.9 percent to 17.6 percent. The group with the biggest body count is treading water. The group with the momentum is 35-to-44, whose share rose from 13.3 percent to 15.9 percent.

**The bracket that improved.** 21-to-24 fell 9.2 percent and 45-to-54 fell 6.4 percent. The two cohorts immediately on either side of 35-to-44 both got safer in absolute terms. Whatever is happening to 35-to-44-year-olds is not a tide lifting every working-age boat; it is specific to that band.

**One in five deaths is now 65 or older.** The 65+ share rose from 17.6 percent (6,237 of 35,484) to 20.4 percent (8,019 of 39,254). But the rate story runs the other way: older licensed drivers grew 36 percent while older deaths grew 29 percent, and 65+ drivers have the lowest fatal-crash involvement rate per licensed driver of any age group (15.60). The senior death count is rising mostly because there are more seniors driving, not because each senior got more dangerous. That distinction is the article's honesty check, and it is also what makes the 35-to-44 number the sharper mystery: no equivalent exposure denominator is published for that group in the sources used here.

## FARS cross-check
Repo FARS dataset (2014-2023) has no age dimension (it is by make/model/model-year), so the age decomposition cannot be cross-tabulated against vehicle models from repo data. Stated plainly in the article: the age table comes from the full FARS person files (all persons killed, all person types), not the repo's model subset. The repo's 191,193 deaths over 2014-2023 cover passenger-vehicle occupants in the modeled fleet, roughly half the national fatality count over the same span; do not present repo numbers as national totals in this article.

## Kill-test notes / what NOT to claim
- Do NOT say 35-to-44-year-olds became 32.8 percent more dangerous as drivers. These are counts of people killed at each age (victims of all person types: drivers, passengers, pedestrians, cyclists), not rates, and not driver-only. No exposure denominator (population, licensed drivers, VMT) by age group is in the sources used here for 35-to-44, so per-person risk cannot be computed from this table. Say the count and the share rose; say the rate question is unanswered.
- Do NOT present the 65+ increase as proof seniors got more dangerous. The fact sheet's own numbers cut the other way: licensed older drivers grew 36 percent against a 29 percent death increase, and 65+ drivers have the lowest fatal-crash involvement rate per licensed driver of any age group. The honest statement is that the senior count is rising mostly because the senior population is rising.
- Do NOT conflate victim age with driver age. The age table counts the age of the person who died. NHTSA's young-driver and older-driver figures count fatalities in crashes *involving* drivers of those ages, a different measure. Keep the two measures labeled.
- Do NOT overclaim the cohort story. The 2024 35-to-44 cohort is largely millennials (born 1980-1989), a bigger cohort than the Gen X cohort (born 1971-1980) that occupied the band in 2015. Cohort size plausibly explains part of the increase; it is a hypothesis, not a measured fact in these sources. State it as the leading explanation to test, not as the answer.
- Do NOT treat 2024 as final. The 2024 figures come from the FARS Annual Report File (ARF), subject to small revision when the Final File is released. The PlainRoadSafety rebuild uses final files through 2023 and the 2024 archive as published; note the ARF caveat.
- Do NOT re-litigate the 65+ divergence as the lead. Three prior articles covered the 2023-to-2024 senior increase from preliminary estimates. This article's lead is 35-to-44; 65+ appears as the second half of the 88.2 percent decomposition and as the rate-based counterweight.
- The "Unknown" age row grew from 80 to 196 (+145 percent). It is small in absolute terms (0.5 percent of deaths) but flag it as a data-quality footnote, not a finding.
- The 2025 estimate (36,640, rate 1.10) is context only and was the subject of #1469. Do not rebuild that article's lead.
