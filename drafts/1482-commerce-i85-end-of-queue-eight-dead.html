Eight People Sat in a Traffic Queue on I-85. A Semi Hit Them at 65 MPH.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 12, 2026

At 4:06 on a Monday afternoon, eight people in a Dodge Grand Caravan were crawling through a traffic queue on I-85 near Commerce, Georgia, at 2 miles an hour. Their belts were buckled. Their kids were in their seats. A semi truck hit them from behind at 64 to 66 miles an hour. Every one of them died.[1]

64–66 mph
The semi's calculated impact speed into a minivan crawling at 2 to 3 mph. Closing speed: roughly 62 mph.

Five impacts followed, in sequence, plus a sideswipe. A 2005 Western Star tractor pulling a 38-foot tank trailer of bulk grain shoved the Caravan into a 2020 Ford Transit cargo van, which was itself crawling at 2.4 mph, and the chain kept going: a 2024 Toyota Tacoma, a 2017 Dodge service truck, and finally a Freightliner Cascadia that sideswiped the Transit as the wreckage scattered across the right lane. Six vehicles. Then the Caravan and the Western Star left the roadway and caught fire, and the fire consumed both of them whole.[1]

Here is what the minivan's own recorder says about its last five seconds. Two to three miles an hour, brake off, a slight acceleration to 7 mph in the final second, the ordinary creep of a driver inching forward in stopped traffic. Then a longitudinal delta-V of 56.0 mph, which is the change in velocity the recorder measured as the rear bumper and cargo area drove into the passenger compartment. Fifty-six miles an hour of instant velocity change is not a crash a 2014 minivan was ever engineered to survive, from any direction, at any price. Nobody builds a family van to take a rear impact at highway speed plus fire.[1]

Seat belts did not fail. Both front latch plates were still engaged in their buckles when investigators pulled the wreckage apart, and the recorder confirmed both front belts were in use. A Graco child safety seat was found partially melted, still affixed to the second-row left seat. Three adults and five children died in that van, including one occupant who was pregnant. And in the Transit ahead of them, the one detail that refuses to leave the report alone: 37 rescue cats, caged in the cargo area, on their way to a facility in Vermont.[1]

Now the truck. It was a 2005 Western Star 4900EX, a Class 8 tractor with a Detroit Series 60 engine, and the NTSB states it plainly in the factual report: the tractor, manufactured in 2005, was not equipped with crash avoidance technologies. Whatever video or data recording it carried burned with the cab, so nobody will ever know from a recorder whether the driver braked. What the record does say is that the driver was a 33-year-old from Gainesville, Georgia, holding a valid Class A commercial license since 2019, that his cell phone records show no texts or calls at the time of the crash, and that he told Georgia State Patrol he may have looked at his phone before the crash. Georgia charged him with eight counts of second-degree vehicular homicide, one count of second-degree feticide by vehicle, following too closely, no registration, and failure to exercise due care.[1][3]

Pause on that truck's birth year, because it matters less than you think and more than it should. A 2005 truck had no automatic emergency braking because the technology barely existed for heavy trucks then. But a 2026 truck has no federal requirement for it either. NHTSA finalized automatic emergency braking for passenger cars and light trucks in April 2024, mandatory by September 2029, and that rule covers vehicles up to 10,000 pounds. A Class 8 tractor weighs more than three times that. The separate heavy-truck rule was proposed in June 2023 and never finalized; as of September 2026 the agencies are restarting it as a supplemental proposal, which means years of comment periods before any mandate exists. Buy a brand-new semi today and the government still does not require it to brake itself.[4]

None of this is a verdict, and the article would be dishonest if it pretended otherwise. NTSB has not issued a probable cause finding for this crash. The criminal charges are allegations, and the driver is presumed innocent. A glance at a phone leaves no carrier record, and the truck's own recorder burned, so the distraction question stays open. And the retrofit objection is real: even a finalized heavy-truck AEB rule would not have reached a 2005 Western Star, because the agencies declined to propose retrofits. Maybe the cause is as simple as a driver who stopped watching the road at 65 mph, and no regulation fixes that in a truck already built.[1]

Grant all of that. What survives is the pattern, and the pattern is the story. NTSB opened this investigation with the stated intent of issuing safety recommendations to prevent similar events, which is the agency's way of saying this shape of crash recurs: a stopped line of cars at highway speed, invisible until the gap closes in seconds, with the last vehicle in line serving as the crumple zone for everything behind it. In the Commerce queue, the victims did everything the safety posters ask. They buckled up, they drove sober, they waited their turn in traffic. Their Grand Caravan was the deadliest minivan in the FARS data by body count anyway, 1,782 deaths in ten years at a rate of 1.33, with a lethality of 0.58 deaths per fatal crash against 0.43 for a Honda Odyssey, and none of that is what killed them. What killed them was being last in line when a truck with no automatic braking arrived at full speed.[1][2][5]

What to do with this: if you are last in a queue on an interstate, you are the crumple zone. Keep your foot on the brake, put your hazards on, and watch the mirror instead of the bumper ahead of you, because the threat is behind you and it is moving at 65. Leave an escape gap and an exit path toward the shoulder, not just following distance. If you drive an older minivan, or any older family vehicle, understand that rear-impact protection is the weakest-tested direction in the fleet, and check the rear-crash ratings for your specific model year rather than trusting the frontal stars. If you buy or operate heavy trucks, ask whether the truck has forward collision warning and automatic emergency braking, because the federal government does not require it yet, and fleet buyers are the only mandate that exists right now. And run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because the habit is the point even when the recall is not the story.
