Body Count

Nine Streets in Las Vegas Killed 172 Pedestrians. They All Post 45 MPH.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

On Saturday, November 7, volunteers spread across the Las Vegas Valley with chalk and drew 172 outlines on nine streets, one silhouette for every pedestrian killed on those streets between January 2021 and June 2026.[1] Chalk washes away. Numbers like that do not, and the people who drew them knew it, which is why they picked the busiest roads in the county and made the death toll something drivers had to look at instead of scroll past.

Run the list and a pattern shows up fast. Tropicana, Flamingo, Sahara, Charleston, Decatur, Lake Mead, Las Vegas Boulevard North, Las Vegas Boulevard South, Boulder Highway. Nine streets, nine arterials, and every last one of them posts the same speed limit: 45 mph.[2] Flamingo Road leads the count with 29 dead, Boulder Highway follows with 23, Charleston Boulevard with 22, Lake Mead Boulevard with 15. Four streets, 89 bodies, more than half the total.[3] Flamingo's deadliest stretch runs between Paradise Road and University Center Drive, right by UNLV, which means some of the people crossing that road are students walking to class.

172
Pedestrians killed on nine Clark County streets from January 2021 through June 2026, about one death every 12 days on those streets alone.

October made the pattern worse. Eleven pedestrians died in the valley last month, the deadliest month of 2026, roughly one every three days, and 8 of the first 10 died after sunset.[4] Daylight saving time ended November 1, which moved the evening commute into the dark for the first time this season. An analysis of ten years of federal crash data puts a number on what that costs: pedestrian deaths in the 5 p.m. hour run six times higher in the two weeks after the clocks go back than in the two weeks before.[5] In New Jersey, they quadruple. Las Vegas just spent its first dark-commute week of the year drawing chalk outlines.

Zoom out and the contrast gets stranger. Nationally, the first half of 2026 posted the lowest traffic fatality rate since 2014, 1.02 deaths per 100 million miles traveled, and the country is on pace for a fifth straight year of declines.[6] Pedestrian deaths did not get the memo. America buried 7,080 people on foot in 2024, down 3.9 percent from 7,367 the year before, and a pedestrian was killed every 74 minutes.[7] Bicyclist deaths rose 4 percent to 1,148 last year.[6] In the valley, 2026 pedestrian deaths are down about 8 percent year over year, and the first half of the year saw them fall nearly 40 percent, before they climbed again from July through September.[4] So the long trend bends down and October bends up, and the chalk went down in the gap between those two facts.

None of this is a mystery to the people counting. Erin Breen, who runs PedSafe Vegas out of UNLV's Transportation Research Center and compiled every number in this story, calls the deaths largely preventable through routine changes to street design and enforcement.[1] Boulder Highway already got 11 new crosswalks with rapid flashing lights under the Reimagine Boulder Highway project, and police run pedestrian enforcement stings at dusk.[8] Clark County Commission Chair Michael Naft says better road design makes a real difference and that he will keep pushing the RTC on it.[3] A 17-year-old was hit in a marked crosswalk on Lake Mead Boulevard in February. A man was killed in the same crosswalk in May 2024.[2] Crosswalks help. On a 45 mph arterial at dusk, they are not enough on their own.

The counterargument, at full strength: Metro police say pedestrian error is the leading contributing factor in their jurisdiction, and that cuts against any story that blames the asphalt alone. These nine streets are also the valley's busiest arterials, carrying the most cars and the most foot traffic, and without per-street traffic volumes nobody can say how much of Flamingo's 29 deaths is danger and how much is exposure. Valley pedestrian deaths are down 8 percent this year, the first half fell nearly 40 percent, and Boulder Highway's flashing crosswalks are real infrastructure, not a press release. By that account the chalk outlines are an advocacy tactic from a campaign with a point of view, the 172 count comes from PedSafe Vegas rather than a federal database, and the honest reading is a metro making slow progress with a bad October. That reading has real weight, especially on the trend. But it does not explain why all nine streets share one speed limit, and it does not bring back the 17-year-old in the crosswalk.

What this does not prove: the 172 count and the street-by-street breakdowns come from PedSafe Vegas and UNLV's Transportation Research Center as reported by local news, not from an independent FARS query, and no per-street traffic volume was available, so no per-mile fatality rate can be computed. The 45 mph commonality is a design observation, not a proven causal claim. October's count differs by report date, 10 as of October 27 and 11 for the full month, and this article uses 11. The six-times DST multiplier comes from a New Jersey attorney's analysis of ten years of FARS data, not a peer-reviewed study, though a January 2025 AAA Foundation report points the same way. And "five years" in campaign language covers a five-and-a-half-year window, January 2021 through June 2026, which is the window used for every rate in this story.

What This Means for You

If you walk in the Las Vegas Valley, treat Flamingo Road between Paradise Road and University Center Drive as the most dangerous crossing in the county, and cross at marked, lit crossings after 5 p.m., because that is the hour and the light where the deaths concentrate. If you drive, slow down on the nine arterials after sunset and expect pedestrians at the crosswalks, because that is where they are dying, marked crosswalk or not. And if you live anywhere else, check the speed limit on the arterial nearest your home. If it says 45, you are looking at the same design Las Vegas just outlined in chalk.
