Two of Seven Cars Earned IIHS’s Top Award. Your Telluride Might Not Be One of Them.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 15, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. Seven 2026 and 2027 models rolled through the Institute's gauntlet this month. Only two earned Top Safety Pick+, its highest honor.[3] And one of those two awards comes with fine print you will not find on the window sticker.

2 of 7
models tested this month earned IIHS Top Safety Pick+

The 2027 Kia Telluride and the 2026-27 Tesla Model Y both turned in maximum Good ratings across every crash test and crash-prevention evaluation. Everybody else went home with homework. BMW’s 3 Series managed Acceptable in the moderate overlap test and Marginal for vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention. Its electric sibling, the i4, scored Poor on the standard crash prevention system, with available headlights ranging from Marginal to Poor by trim. GMC’s Canyon crew cab took a Marginal in moderate overlap and a Poor in vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention. Lexus’s IS and Toyota’s Crown Signia both stumbled on the moderate overlap test.[3][4]

Now the fine print. Kia improved the Telluride’s headlights in June 2026. Tellurides built before that date carry Marginal-rated headlights, and the award simply does not apply to them. Same model year, same badge, different safety award. If you are shopping one, open the driver’s door and read the manufacturing date on the jamb sticker. Anything before June 2026 is a different car in the eyes of the people who crash-test them.

Tesla’s Model Y carries its own wrinkle. It earned the institute’s top honor while scoring a Marginal rating on seat belt reminder effectiveness, because belt reminders are not part of the award criteria.[4] Read that again. The machine that calculates the safest cars in America ignores how hard the car nags you to buckle up. Buckle up anyway. Your dummy would.

Look at what keeps failing and a pattern emerges. IIHS’s updated moderate overlap test straps a dummy the size of a small woman or a 12-year-old into the back seat behind the driver, and the back seat keeps getting caught short. IIHS research found that in newer vehicles, the risk of fatal injury is now higher for belted rear occupants than for front occupants, not because the rear got worse but because the front got much better: advanced airbags and sophisticated belts that rarely make it to the second row.[1] The Canyon tells the story in miniature. Good driver protection, then a chest index of 40 and shoulder belt tension of 5.7 kN on the rear dummy, which slid beneath its lap belt exactly the way rear dummies have been doing for three years.[5]

So here is your shopping checklist. One: a Top Safety Pick+ badge is model-year and build-date specific, so verify the jamb sticker. Two: “almost got the award” means the car failed the rear-seat test your kids sit through, so ask the dealer what changed for 2027. Three: crash prevention ratings now decide awards, which means a car can be structurally excellent and still flunk on software. Structural steel is doing its job; the back seat and the code are still catching up.
