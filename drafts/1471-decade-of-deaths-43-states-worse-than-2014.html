The 4% Drop Is Real. The Decade Is Not: 43 States Are Deadlier Than 2014.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 11, 2026

Every headline this year said the same thing: traffic deaths fell 4.3%. True, and also incomplete in a way that borders on dishonest. I ran the numbers back a decade, state by state, and the celebration dissolves on contact: 39,254 Americans died on the roads in 2024, which is 6,510 more than died in 2014. That is a 20% increase over ten years, and only seven states are safer than they were when the decade started. Forty-three are worse.

43
states with more traffic deaths in 2024 than in 2014; only 7 improved

Start with the recovery math, because it is the part nobody printed. Last year's improvement, 1,771 fewer deaths, erased barely a quarter of the decade's growth. At that pace the country needs three more years of identical declines just to get back to 2014, a year nobody remembers as dangerous. Then I ran the numbers again, because that is what you do when the first run hurts, and the map came back worse than the total.

Five of the eight worst decade increases sit in the West: Arizona up 59%, Washington 58%, Oregon 51%, Nevada 43%, Colorado 41%. Three more sit in northern New England: New Hampshire 40%, Maine 35%, Vermont 34%. Two clusters, opposite edges of the continent, same direction. Only seven states improved: North Dakota, Wyoming, Kansas, Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Alaska, Oklahoma. Four of the seven are Great Plains states, which is either a clue or a coincidence, and the data does not say which.

Now the objection that actually matters: more people, more miles. Miles driven rose about 9% over the decade, population about 7%. Deaths rose 20%. Strip out the miles and the story barely softens: the fatality rate went from 1.08 deaths per 100 million miles in 2014 to 1.19 in 2024, up 10%. Roads got more dangerous per mile, not just busier.

Maine deserves its own paragraph. It posted the largest one-year jump in the country in 2024, up 31%, and it is also up 35% over the decade. A one-year spike can be noise; a spike on top of a ten-year trend is a direction.

None of this erases the good news. Three straight years of declines is real, the 2021 peak is more than half unwound, and 2014 was one of the safest years on record, which flatters the comparison. But a recovery that has erased a quarter of the damage is a recovery in progress, not a recovery complete. And the 2024 figures are still the Annual Report File, subject to revision when NHTSA finalizes them, so treat the second decimal as provisional and the ranking as solid.

If you live in one of the eight worst states, the national headline is not about you. Check your state DOT's numbers, not the national press release, and drive like the decade happened, because it did.
