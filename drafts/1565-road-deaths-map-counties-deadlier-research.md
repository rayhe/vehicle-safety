# Research — #1565: The National Death Toll Fell. 183 Counties Got Deadlier Anyway.

**Slug:** `1565-road-deaths-map-counties-deadlier`
**Journalist:** Axle McScatter (Data Visualization Editor — statistical roundups beat)
**Kicker:** By The Numbers
**Date researched:** November 22, 2026

## Angle
The national road-death story of the last four years is a victory lap: deaths peaked in 2021, fell every year since, and NHTSA's early estimate puts 2025 at 36,640, the second-lowest fatality rate ever recorded. A new county-by-county study from Censal Research (published November 5, 2026) shows the victory lap was mostly a press release. Compare 2024 with 2019, the last pre-pandemic year, and the road death rate was HIGHER in 183 of America's 286 largest counties. In the big counties, home to 64% of Americans, the death rate rose 9.5%. In the rest of the country it was flat. The national improvement is real, but it is geographically concentrated: the places where most Americans live got deadlier, and the people least protected in a crash — pedestrians, cyclists, motorcyclists — got killed at rates far above the national trend. The Bronx nearly doubled. Philadelphia rose 54%. Las Vegas rose 48%. The headline number fell. The map did not.

## Verified facts (3+ primary sources)

1. **The county divergence (Censal Research, "The Road Deaths Map," v1.0, November 2026; analysis of NHTSA FARS + Census Bureau population estimates).** 39,254 people died on US roads in 2024, against 36,355 in 2019 (+8.0%, +2,899 deaths). Per resident, the rate rose 4.9%, from 11.0 to 11.5 per 100,000. Of the 286 counties with at least 250,000 residents, 183 had a higher road death rate in 2024 than in 2019 and 103 a lower one. In those large counties, home to 64% of Americans, the death rate rose 9.5%, from 8.3 to 9.1 per 100,000. In the rest of the country the rate was unchanged: 16.1 in 2019 and 16.1 in 2024. Smaller counties still have far deadlier roads per resident; what changed is that big counties caught up part of the way.
2. **The seven statistically clear rises (Censal, same study; false-discovery-rate correction across all 286 counties, q below 0.10).** Cameron County, Texas (Brownsville) +134% (17 to 41 deaths, rate 4.0 to 9.5 per 100,000). The Bronx +103% (27 to 52 deaths, rate 1.8 to 3.7). Philadelphia +54% (91 to 137, rate 5.7 to 8.7). Clark County, Nevada (Las Vegas) +48% (186 to 293, rate 8.3 to 12.3). Shelby County, Tennessee (Memphis) +43% (155 to 218, rate 16.7 to 23.8). Harris County, Texas (Houston) +30% (421 to 583, rate 8.9 to 11.7). Maricopa County, Arizona (Phoenix) +26% (442 to 596, rate 10.1 to 12.8). Harris and Maricopa alone recorded 316 more deaths in 2024 than in 2019. The fall is statistically clear in three: Fulton County, Georgia (Atlanta) -36%, Osceola County, Florida (Kissimmee) -48%, Baldwin County, Alabama -55%.
3. **The vulnerable-road-user split (Censal, same study).** Pedestrian deaths 6,272 to 7,080 (+12.9%, +808). Cyclist deaths 856 to 1,075 (+25.6%, +219). Motorcyclist deaths 5,044 to 6,225 (+23.4%, +1,181). Deaths in speeding-related crashes 9,592 to 11,288 (+17.7%, +1,696). All road deaths +8.0%; population +3%. Every vulnerable group rose faster than deaths overall and far faster than population. Among large counties, St. Louis city had the highest pedestrian death rate in 2024 (7.8 per 100,000; 16 to 22 pedestrians killed), followed by Clayton County, Georgia (7.7) and Orleans Parish, Louisiana (7.7; pedestrian deaths 10 to 28). US average pedestrian death rate: 2.1 per 100,000.
4. **The state extremes (Censal, same study).** Mississippi had the highest road death rate of any state in 2024: 25.5 per 100,000 residents, more than twice the US rate of 11.5, up 18% since 2019 (642 to 753 deaths). Rhode Island had the lowest (4.7), followed by Massachusetts (5.1). The rate rose in 35 of 51 states (counting the District of Columbia). The District of Columbia's road deaths doubled, from 23 to 47 (rate 3.4 to 6.8). Among large counties, Marion County, Florida (Ocala) had the deadliest roads in 2024 (26.4 per 100,000); among counties over a million people, Pima County, Arizona (Tucson) did (16.9). Manhattan and Brooklyn had the lowest (2.4 each).
5. **The national context that makes the map a correction, not a contradiction (NHTSA DOT HS 813 791, "Overview of Motor Vehicle Traffic Crashes in 2024," April 2026; NHTSA DOT HS 813 800, "Early Estimate of Motor Vehicle Traffic Fatalities and Fatality Rate in 2025," April 2026).** 2024 final: 39,254 fatalities, down 4.3% from 41,025 in 2023; 36,297 fatal crashes; fatality rate 1.19 per 100 million VMT, down from 1.26; VMT 3,294 billion (+1.5%). 2025 early estimate: 36,640 deaths (-6.7% vs 2024), fatality rate 1.10 per 100 million VMT — the second-lowest rate in recorded history, behind only 1.08 in 2014. Q4 2025 was the 15th consecutive quarterly decline beginning Q2 2022. Censal notes county-level data for 2025 is not yet published, so the 2019-vs-2024 comparison is the latest the map can make — and the 2025 national improvement may or may not have reached the counties that got worse.

## Original analysis (for the article)
- **The share math:** 183 of 286 large counties is 64.0%. Nearly two-thirds of the counties where most Americans live got deadlier while the national total fell. The national decline was driven by the other third plus rural America holding level — a composition effect, not a uniform improvement.
- **The two-county check:** Harris (+162 deaths) and Maricopa (+154) together added 316 deaths — more than a tenth of the entire national increase of 2,899 since 2019, from two counties. The national number moved because a handful of huge counties moved a lot, in both directions.
- **The mode-split math:** Cyclists +25.6% on a base of 856 deaths is +219 people; motorcyclists +23.4% is +1,181. The vulnerable-user increases are not rounding noise on small bases — motorcyclist deaths alone account for 41% of the total national increase (1,181 of 2,899). The recovery, such as it is, is a car-occupant recovery.
- **The rate-vs-count distinction:** The Bronx "doubled" from 27 to 52 deaths and its rate went from 1.8 to 3.7 per 100,000 — still a third of the national rate of 11.5. New York City remains the safest big-city road system in America even after its worst borough doubled. Absolute risk and trend are different stories; the article must carry both.
- **The 2025 caveat as the honest ending:** NHTSA's 2025 estimate (36,640) is essentially back to the 2019 count (36,355). If the county pattern holds when 2025 county data arrives in early 2027, the story is "the pandemic surge fully unwound." If it doesn't — if the big counties stayed elevated while the national total fell — the story is "the surge moved, it didn't end." The article should end on that open question, not on a victory lap.

## Limitations
- Censal's study compares two years, 2019 and 2024. Deaths peaked in 2021 and have fallen since; a two-year comparison cannot show whether a county's 2024 elevation is a persistent new level or the tail of the 2021 peak. Say so.
- Rates are per resident, not per mile driven. Counties with heavy through traffic or many visitors (Las Vegas, Orlando) can have high rates per resident because the denominator misses the people actually on the roads. Censal flags this explicitly.
- Deaths are counted where the crash happened, not where the victim lived. Same through-traffic caveat.
- The study is associational: it shows where rates changed, not why. Road design, speed, vehicle size, traffic, enforcement, impairment, and emergency care can all play a part. Do not attribute the Bronx doubling or the Philadelphia rise to a single cause.
- Small-number noise: Censal ranks only counties with 250,000+ residents, tests every change, and corrects for testing 286 counties at once. The seven "statistically clear" rises passed that correction; the 183-of-286 headline count includes counties whose individual moves may be chance. Distinguish the two.
- NHTSA's 2025 figures (36,640; rate 1.10) are early estimates, revisable when the FARS Final File for 2024 and the ARF for 2025 arrive in early 2027. County-level 2025 data is not yet published.

## Strongest counterargument
The national numbers are the real story, and the county map is a cherry-picked frame. Deaths fell three straight years from the 2021 peak, every quarter since Q2 2022 is down year-over-year, and the 2025 fatality rate (1.10) is the second-lowest ever recorded. Picking 2019 as the baseline guarantees a bad-looking comparison, because 2019 was the low point before a pandemic spike; compare 2024 to 2021 and nearly every county looks better. The map also leans on per-resident rates in places where the denominator is wrong (tourist counties, commuter counties), and seven "statistically clear" rises out of 286 counties is a thin basis for a national indictment. The article must carry this at full strength: the improvement is real, the baseline choice does work, and the county finding is about distribution, not about whether the country got safer overall. It did. Unevenly.

## Actionable takeaways
- Look up your county: Censal publishes a lookup for all 3,133 counties (road deaths per resident, 2019 and 2024, rank, and comparison with its state) at censalresearch.com/studies/road-deaths-map. If your county is one of the 183, the national improvement did not reach you — and local road-safety decisions (speed management, pedestrian infrastructure, enforcement) are made at the county and city level, not in Washington.
- If you walk, bike, or ride: the mode split is the actionable finding. Pedestrian deaths +12.9%, cyclist +25.6%, motorcyclist +23.4% since 2019, while overall deaths rose 8.0%. The roads got more dangerous specifically for people outside a car. Visibility gear, separated routes, and avoiding high-speed arterials on foot or on two wheels are the individual-level responses; the systemic one is showing up to the local meeting where the arterial gets redesigned.
- If you live in one of the seven clear-rise counties (Cameron TX, the Bronx, Philadelphia, Clark NV, Shelby TN, Harris TX, Maricopa AZ), your county's road death rate rose 26% to 134% since 2019. That is a local-government accountability question: ask your county what changed, and what it is doing about speed — speeding-related deaths rose 17.7% nationally.
