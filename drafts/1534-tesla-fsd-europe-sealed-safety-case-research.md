## Story in one line
Ten European countries have approved Tesla's Full Self-Driving for their roads on the strength of one Dutch safety review whose evidence is sealed as a trade secret, while the same software sits under open US federal investigations for running red lights and going blind in glare — and a December vote could extend the sealed approval to all 27 EU member states at once.

## Verified facts

1. **The Dutch approval (April 10, 2026).** The Netherlands' vehicle authority RDW granted a provisional type approval for FSD (Supervised) under UN Regulation 171, via Article 39 of EU Regulation 2018/858 — a provision for technologies so novel they cannot be evaluated under existing standards. It covers Hardware 4 (AI4) cars only. Tesla markets the system in Europe as "Tesla Assisted Driving." (evcompared tracker; teslablog.eu; notanfsdtracker.)
2. **The cascade: ten countries.** As of October 26, 2026, ten EU member states have recognised the Dutch approval for their own roads: Netherlands, Lithuania, Estonia, Denmark, Belgium, Slovenia, Czechia, Croatia, Slovakia (October 16), Latvia (October 26). None conducted independent testing; each accepted the Dutch decision. The ten hold 64.4 million people, 14.3% of the EU population. (evcompared tracker; teslablog.eu status Nov 4, 2026.)
3. **The vote that would make it EU-wide.** The Technical Committee on Motor Vehicles (TCMV) discussed the Dutch application on October 6, 2026, for 25 minutes without voting. The earliest possible vote is at its December 2026 meeting, not confirmed. Passing needs a qualified majority: at least 15 of 27 member states representing at least 65% of the EU population. Germany, France, Italy and Spain have not approved the system. France and Sweden object to the Speed Offset feature, which lets the system drive above the posted limit by a driver-configurable margin. If the EU rejects it, the Dutch approval becomes invalid after six months and the national permits built on it lapse. (evcompared; teslablog.eu; notanfsdtracker.)
4. **The sealed safety case (Reuters, via Tech Times, Aug 6, 2026).** Emails obtained by Reuters through public records requests show Tesla pressed RDW from late 2024 to keep FSD review documentation from public view. In an April 2025 exchange, a Tesla representative sought written confirmation that a specific document would "never" be released and asked how RDW would ensure it was "withheld from public disclosure," saying the company could not provide further information until that was settled. RDW told Tesla manufacturers can seek a trade-secret exemption, and Tesla claimed it broadly. RDW told Reuters it makes its own trade-secret determinations, but its stated justification reportedly echoed Tesla's language almost verbatim. Regulators in six European countries, including Germany, Norway and Denmark, told Reuters they cannot release FSD testing data for the same reason. The RDW certificate and its exemption annex (which models, hardware and software it covers, which exemptions were granted) were not publicly available as of October 2026. (Tech Times; tesorb.com notes the annex gap.)
5. **The experts on the secrecy.** Oliver Carsten, transportation-safety professor at the University of Leeds involved in crafting European automated-driving regulations, told Reuters: "I don't see any reason why that couldn't be public." Frank Mutze, policy manager at the European Transport Safety Council, said the arrangement leaves the public to trust authorities "have done their homework, which of course isn't good enough for us." ETSC formally wrote to EU transport ministers urging a pause before the vote, calling the TCMV "an unelected body that meets behind closed doors in Brussels." (Tech Times, Aug 6, 2026.)
6. **RDW's own confidence retreated.** In April 2026 the agency announced FSD was "safer than other driver assistance systems"; by June 2026 that had softened to "at least as safe as other driver assistance systems." No data was provided in support of either claim. (Tech Times.)
7. **Norway read the data and disagreed with the headline.** When Norway's Public Roads Administration reviewed the data RDW shared on its FSD assessment, officials noted it shows Tesla's vehicles are less safe than the company's publicly published statistics claim. Tesla's claim that FSD cars travel more than seven times farther between crashes than the average American driver rests on counting only crashes where airbags deployed, compared against a US database capturing all crashes serious enough to require a tow. Marco Benedetti of the University of Michigan Transportation Research Institute corrected the comparison to a consistent severity threshold; the claimed advantage collapsed from roughly 7x to approximately 1.1x. Neither Tesla nor RDW has addressed Norway's finding publicly. (Tech Times.)
8. **The American ledger: two open defect probes.** NHTSA ODI Preliminary Evaluation PE25012 (opened Oct 7, 2025): 2,882,566 Teslas with FSD, 58 incidents at opening (14 crashes, 23 injuries), alleging red-light running and wrong-way driving, including cars that stopped at red lights and then proceeded. NHTSA Engineering Analysis EA26002 (upgraded Mar 18, 2026): 3,203,754 Teslas, 9 incidents including 1 fatal crash, alleging FSD's degradation detection fails to warn drivers when cameras are blinded by glare, fog or dust. Both verified in this site's own research (#1117, #1524). A third federal track covers Tesla's crash-reporting practices. (NHTSA ODI resumes; Tech Times.)
9. **The American ledger: the worst crash-reporting month on record.** In May 2026 Tesla reported a record 207 crashes involving Autopilot and FSD to NHTSA in a single month — the highest monthly total in the history of the ADAS reporting program, more than Tesla reported for the entire year of 2021. Tesla accounts for approximately 85% of all ADAS crash reports across the US industry, and redacts the crash narrative in 99.9% of its reports, citing confidential business information. Every other major automaker redacts essentially none. (Tech Times, citing NHTSA SGO data and Electrek.)
10. **The money.** FSD costs €99 per month in the Netherlands; Tesla ended the one-time purchase option in May 2026 (previously €7,500). European registrations fell sharply through 2025, down 40–80% year-on-year in France, Sweden, Denmark and the Netherlands, while BYD matched Tesla's European market share at 2.4% in H1 2026. FSD subscription revenue is one of the clearest paths to improving Tesla's European margins without selling more cars. (Tech Times.)
11. **The disclaimer that will not travel.** RDW's approval statement says FSD-equipped vehicles "are NOT autonomous or self-driving." That disclaimer appears on the Dutch approval but will not appear on an EU-wide authorization. (Tech Times.)

## Original contribution: the asymmetry arithmetic + the two ledgers

**The 14.3% cascade.** Ten countries sounds like momentum. It is 64.4 million people out of ~450 million, 14.3% of the EU population, against a 65% threshold for EU-wide approval. Every one of those ten approvals traces to a single Dutch file that no member of the public has seen. The cascade is wide and shallow: breadth without depth, because no second regulator has independently tested the system.

**The two ledgers.** Put the European and American records side by side and they describe different products. Europe: approved in ten countries, "at least as safe as other driver assistance systems." America: two open defect investigations, a record crash-reporting month, 99.9% of crash narratives redacted, and a DOJ criminal probe into marketing the reporting characterizes as potentially fraudulent. The difference is not the software. It is the evidence standard: one regulator accepted a sealed safety case; the other is still demanding data.

**The retreat as a tell.** RDW downgraded its own claim within two months ("safer than" to "at least as safe as") without publishing data for either version. A regulator that had the data would not need to soften the sentence. A regulator that had Tesla's data under seal could not show its work either way.

## FARS cross-check
The repo FARS dataset (2014–2023) shows Tesla Model Y at 0.03 and Model 3 at 0.05 deaths per 100M VMT — the lowest rates in the dataset — but on tiny death counts (57 and 92) from a fleet that was young and small through most of the window. This is reported only as context with the caveat: the aggregate rate looks excellent while the probe record documents specific failure modes (red-light running, visibility degradation) that a per-mile average cannot capture. The article does not claim Teslas are unsafe per mile; it claims the European approval rests on evidence nobody can check.

## Kill-test notes / what NOT to claim
- Do NOT say the EU has approved FSD. Ten countries recognised the Dutch approval; EU-wide approval needs a December TCMV vote that has not happened and is not confirmed. Germany, France, Italy, Spain have not approved it.
- Do NOT say FSD is proven unsafe. The US probes are preliminary evaluation / engineering analysis, not defect findings. The article's claim is about evidence and process, not a safety verdict.
- Do NOT present the "7x safer" collapse as this site's own calculation. It is Benedetti/UMTRI's correction as reported by Tech Times; attribute it.
- Do NOT present the May 2026 "207 crashes" or "85% of ADAS reports" as NHTSA-published figures. They are reported from NHTSA SGO data via Tech Times/Electrek; attribute accordingly.
- Do NOT claim the DOJ probe found fraud. The reporting characterizes the marketing as potentially fraudulent under a criminal probe; say exactly that and no more.
- The techtimes piece predates the October 6 TCMV meeting and speaks of an "October vote." The current status (no vote Oct 6; December earliest) comes from the November trackers and supersedes the August framing. Do not repeat "October vote" as pending.
- Tesla counts nine EU countries in its Q3 2026 update; the trackers count ten (Latvia recognised Oct 26, rollout pending). Use ten with the tracker citation; note Tesla's own count differs.
- The "three concurrent probes" framing comes from Tech Times: EA26002 + PE25012 + crash-reporting practices. Name all three; do not invent a third defect probe.
- RDW's position, per its statement to Reuters, is that it makes its own trade-secret determinations and did not withhold information at Tesla's request. Include that rebuttal at full strength; the article's case is about the outcome (sealed evidence), not about RDW's intent.
