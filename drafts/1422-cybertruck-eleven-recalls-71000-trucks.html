Tesla Is Killing the Cybertruck. 11 Recalls for 71,000 Trucks Wrote the Obituary.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

Tesla announced Friday it will wind down Cybertruck production at Gigafactory Texas around mid-2027, clearing floor space for the Cybercab robotaxi.[1] First deliveries landed in November 2023. U.S. sales fell 48 percent last year, to 20,237 from 38,965.[4] Somewhere between the launch party and the obituary, the truck collected 11 federal recall campaigns against roughly 71,000 vehicles ever built.[2] That works out to one recall for every 6,455 trucks.

Recall campaigns per Cybertruck built: 11 NHTSA campaigns against roughly 71,000 trucks (NHTSA recalls API; Reuters production estimate)

Run the campaigns in order and the story tells itself. January 2024 opened with warning-light lettering too small to read, fixed overnight by software. April brought the accelerator pedal pad that could dislodge and jam against the trim, 3,878 trucks, physical repair, unintended acceleration on the table. June doubled down with two filings on the same day: a wiper motor that could cook itself on excessive current, 11,688 trucks, and trunk-bed trim that could peel off and become a road hazard, 11,383. September's rearview camera arrived late to its own party, showing a delayed image after the driver shifted into reverse. November's drive inverter could cut power to the wheels entirely. December's tire-pressure warning light could not stay lit between drive cycles.[3] March 2025 brought the big one, a light bar that could delaminate and detach, 46,096 trucks. Three of the eleven are parts coming off the truck. Four were fixed by software pushed overnight. Seven needed a service bay.

Now the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is where this stops being a vibe and becomes an obituary. Seven of the eleven campaigns landed in the first twelve months, 63.6 percent of the truck's entire recall history before its first birthday. Filings run January 2024 to April 2026, one roughly every two and a half months. That single light-bar campaign touched 46,096 trucks, 64.9 percent of every Cybertruck ever built. Total lifetime production, about 71,000, equals roughly seven months of one Texas line's rated annual output of 125,000. Tesla built a factory that could make 125,000 trucks a year and then made 71,000 in three and a half years. Gigafactory Texas is the only plant equipped to build the truck, and no replacement site has been announced.

Here is the part that ruins any clean narrative. This same truck earned IIHS Top Safety Pick+ in March 2026, Good across every crashworthiness category, while the F-150, Silverado, and Ram earned no IIHS award at all.[5] A truck can be the safest thing in the crash lab and the most recalled thing on the road at the same time, because crashworthiness and build quality are different tests answered by different departments. Tesla aced one and flunked the other in public, eleven times, in front of a regulator that keeps receipts.

Fairness demands the other side get its paragraph. Recall volume is a lousy proxy for quality. A company that finds problems and files fast will always look worse than one that slow-walks defects into the next model year, and Ford has made exactly that argument while leading the industry in filings. Four of the eleven were fixed overnight by software, which no legacy truck can do. All eleven have remedies available, which is not true of every recall on the books. Three open investigations and roughly 200 owner complaints sit alongside the closed campaigns, and complaints are unverified reports, not findings of defect. The record proves the truck arrived unfinished. It does not prove it is unsafe in a crash. Both statements are true, and any honest obituary carries both.

What this piece did not prove, stated plainly. The 71,000 figure is Reuters' assessment, not a Tesla disclosure, and every per-truck ratio inherits its uncertainty. Campaigns are not equal: a font-size compliance fix and a pedal that sticks open each count as one, so the density ratio measures volume, not severity. Campaign populations overlap heavily, so adding affected-vehicle counts across campaigns double-counts trucks. The wind-down is announced for one factory on an approximate date; Tesla has not confirmed a global discontinuation or a last build date. Treat the ratios as the shape of the record, not a precision instrument.

What you can actually do with this. If you own one, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls instead of assuming the over-the-air updates covered everything, because seven of the eleven campaigns need physical work: pedal, wiper motor, trim, inverter, light bar, wheel studs. The wind-down changes nothing about Tesla's obligation to fix them free. Keep every recall notice and service record, because the question nobody at Tesla has answered is how long parts and service last for stainless panels, steer-by-wire, and rear-wheel steering that no other production pickup uses. Seventy-one thousand owners are about to find out together.
