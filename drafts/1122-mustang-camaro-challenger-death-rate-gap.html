The Gap

The Same Drivers, the Same Roads, Six Times the Dying

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

Three American muscle cars rolled off the same idea: big noise, rear drive, affordable horsepower. Over a decade of fatal crash data, the Ford Mustang killed at 6.02 deaths per 100 million miles traveled. The Dodge Challenger killed at 1.00. Same segment, same price bracket, and here is the part that should bother you: the drivers test out nearly identical.

6.02×
The Mustang's death rate per 100M miles versus the Challenger's, on statistically indistinguishable driver impairment rates

In fatal crashes from 2014 through 2023, toxicology screens flagged impairment in 21.9 percent of Mustang drivers, 23.0 percent of Camaro drivers, and 22.5 percent of Challenger drivers. That is a 1.1-point spread. Statistically, these are the same drivers making the same mistakes on the same roads, because across 9,500 tested drivers, the numbers refuse to move. Yet the Mustang piled up 2,739 deaths on a fleet barely 18 percent larger than the Challenger's, which lost 385. Run the arithmetic however you like, and you land on six.

So much for the theory that Challenger drivers are just calmer. Dodge's own brand manager told reporters in 2019 that the average new Challenger buyer was 51, the youngest average buyer in the segment, younger than Mustang and Camaro customers. The safest of the three cars is bought by the youngest of the three crowds. Driver virtue cannot explain this; mass can.

The Challenger is the bluntest instrument in the trio. It rides on a 116.2-inch wheelbase and tips the scales at roughly 4,200 to 4,400 pounds, hundreds of pounds heavier than either rival, with an actual back seat and a trunk you can use. In a two-car crash, physics does not care about your brand loyalty. IIHS research on vehicle size and weight has shown for decades that the heavier vehicle shields its occupants while endangering the occupants of the smaller one, and every pound of that Challenger is working for its driver and against everyone else. It is also the least twitchy of the three, a touring car wearing a muscle suit, which shows up as fewer of the single-vehicle loss-of-control crashes that fill FARS rows for Mustangs.

Now the honest accounting, because the numbers deserve it. FARS only records fatal crashes, so injury rates are invisible here, and the per-mile rates are built from estimated vehicle miles, not odometer readings, which means roughly 15 percent uncertainty on models like these. The Challenger offered all-wheel drive, a segment first that could tilt its exposure toward bad weather and away from summer showboating, and Mustang culture probably includes more track-day energy and horsepower-per-dollar recklessness than any toxicology screen can measure. Weight also has a victim: in every two-car crash the Challenger survives, somebody smaller absorbs it, and that is the full ledger.

Before you sign that title transfer on a used V8, understand what you are buying. A Challenger is not six times safer because its drivers are six times wiser; it is six times safer per mile because it is roughly 500 pounds heavier and built like a battering ram with cupholders. If your budget says Mustang and your family says Challenger, the data has a vote. Check the exact model year's IIHS ratings, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, and remember that the single most predictive safety feature in any two-car crash is not under the hood. It is on the spec sheet, measured in pounds.
