## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0/10
No individual victims are named or dramatized, no crash gore, no moral judgment of truck drivers or car drivers. The piece frames the toll as physics and exposure, not as a verdict on the people involved, and it refuses the easy villain: the truck driver is explicitly the most law-abiding driver in the data. The counterargument gives the other side its full weight instead of strawmanning it. Nothing here exploits grief for clicks.

## 4. Social / Shareability — 8.8/10
Headline is built to travel: "the safest driver in the crash drives the deadliest vehicle" is a paradox that argues with itself, and 26 to 1 is a quotable number that fits in a post. The docket-number policy ask gives readers something specific to demand, which is rarer than it should be. Slight deduction: there is no single human story or villain to argue about, and the AEB rulemaking detail is dry for a feed, so the piece travels on its numbers rather than its heat. The numbers are strong enough to carry it.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.9/10
Every factual claim traces to a cited source: NHTSA Large Trucks 2024 (DOT HS 813 816), IIHS Fatality Facts 2024, the NHTSA/FMCSA heavy-vehicle AEB NPRM (88 Fed. Reg. 43174, docket NHTSA-2023-0023), and NSC Injury Facts. The IIJA order date (November 2021) supports "five years after it was ordered." The AEB figures are correctly framed as the agencies' modeled estimates, not observed results. Deduction: reference 3 links to a law-firm summary of the NPRM rather than the Federal Register text itself; the docket number is given, which mitigates it, but the primary document should have been the link. Pre-panel self-review caught and fixed one error: the national fatality rate of 1.19 was first called "the second-best year on record," which is wrong (2014 was 1.08 and 2019 was 1.11); corrected to a plain year-over-year comparison before the panel ran.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.9/10
Original contribution is real and checkable: the per-mile rates computed from the IIHS table (passenger-vehicle occupant deaths per 100M truck miles 0.82 in 2009 to 1.00 in 2024, +22%; truck-occupant rate 0.17 to 0.28, +65%, against only +14% truck miles), the 26-to-1 odds restatement of the 2,511-vs-95 split, the deaths-down/injuries-up divergence in 2024, and the behavior-versus-outcome paradox cross-tab. Limitations are explicit (FARS counts involvement, not fault; 2024 is Annual Report File, not Final File; the "large truck" category blends delivery vans with 80,000-pound rigs; FHWA mileage method changed in 2007). The counterargument is stated at full strength, including the phantom-braking engineering analysis. Deduction: the 2009-2024 per-mile comparison rests on FHWA mileage estimates, and the 60,000 rear-end-crashes figure reaches the article through secondary reporting of the NPRM rather than the NPRM text itself.

## 7. Data Presentation — 8.8/10
Counts sit next to percentages throughout (2,511 vs 95; 3,753 / 920 / 667; 0.82 to 1.00), so no ratio floats without its raw numbers, and the 20-40% stopping-distance range is translated into "a fifth to two-fifths more road" for readers who do not think in percentages. References are linked and specific. Minor: the article uses NHTSA's 70/17/12 person split and never notes that IIHS's own page reports a different split (62% passenger-vehicle occupants, 19% pedestrians/cyclists/motorcyclists) because the two classify motorcyclists differently; a sharp reader comparing sources will notice the gap and the article does not pre-empt it.
