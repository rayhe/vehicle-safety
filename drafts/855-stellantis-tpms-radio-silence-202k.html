Stellantis Recalled 202,000 Jeeps Because the Radio Forgot to Watch the Tires

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

Your tire-pressure warning is a software feature now. It lives in the radio frequency hub, sharing silicon with the Bluetooth stack and the satellite radio tuner. Today Stellantis recalled 201,976 Jeep vehicles because a bug in that hub can prevent the tire pressure monitoring system from detecting low pressure or lighting the warning at all.[1] No light. No chime. Nothing to tell you the thing that tells you things is broken.

1,045,976
Stellantis vehicles recalled in about four weeks because radio or RF-hub software disabled a federally mandated safety warning

Start with the failure mode, because it is the whole story. FMVSS 138 requires the low-pressure telltale to illuminate within 20 minutes of a tire dropping to 25 percent below placard pressure.[5] That 20-minute clock only starts if the hub software processes the sensor data and decides a warning is warranted. Kill the processing and the clock never starts, the telltale never lights, and the driver has no self-test, no diagnostic, no dashboard confession that the watchdog is asleep. A warning system with no warning for its own failure. Elegant.

Now the pattern, because this is the second time in a month. In mid-August Stellantis recalled roughly 844,000 vehicles (campaign 26V531) after radio software caused the rearview camera image to fail to display, sometimes showing a blue screen instead, a violation of FMVSS 111's rear visibility standard.[2][3] Add today's 201,976 and you get over a million vehicles in about four weeks recalled because software in the radio stack disabled a federally mandated safety warning: two standards, one compute stack, zero warnings. Consolidation is efficient right up until one bug class takes out two safety systems at once.

And the stakes are not theoretical. NHTSA attributes nearly 11,000 crashes and more than 600 deaths a year to tire problems.[6] Back in July I wrote that TPMS is already legally permitted to stay dark until you are 25 percent underinflated, which the tire industry itself calls too late to prevent damage for some vehicles. A software bug that silences even that permissive warning is not a marginal degradation. It is the difference between a late warning and no warning on a system where 85 percent of drivers never check pressure manually.

What to do: if you drive a Wagoneer, Grand Cherokee, Grand Cherokee L, Grand Cherokee 4xe, Grand Wagoneer, Wagoneer L, or Cherokee from the affected years, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today and get the free dealer software update when the letters go out.[1][8] Everyone else: buy a ten-dollar gauge and check your pressures monthly, because the dashboard light, when it works exactly as designed, is allowed to wait until you are a quarter low. Trust, but verify, preferably with a gauge in your own hand.

Fairness requires the other side. Software consolidation is the industry's direction for real reasons: a software defect gets fixed in minutes at a dealer, or overnight over the air, instead of the months a hardware recall takes. No crashes or injuries have been tied to this TPMS bug, which suggests the internal testing and NHTSA monitoring caught it before the failure mode met the real world. And the correlated-failure critique cuts both ways: one software stack means one fix deploys everywhere simultaneously. The honest limitations: NHTSA had not published the campaign number or the Part 573 chronology at press time, the fraction of the 201,976 vehicles actually exhibiting the bug is unknown, and the 1,045,976 combined figure spans two distinct defects, measuring exposure to the pattern rather than a single root cause.
