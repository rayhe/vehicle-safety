NHTSA Wants to Delete the Rollover Test. The Ford E-350 Is Why Congress Ordered It.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026

Park a Ford E-150 next to a Ford E-350 and you are looking at the same van wearing two different tonnage ratings. Feed a decade of federal fatal-crash data into the comparison and the resemblance ends: the E-350 kills at 7.2 times the death rate of the E-150, 2.51 deaths per hundred million miles against 0.35.[1] Middle child E-250 lands at 0.31, which completes the family portrait: same Econoline bloodline, same blue oval, same era of American work van, and the one-ton sibling buried 776 people in ten years while the other two combined managed 152.

First suspect in any family massacre is usually the drivers, so cross that one off. Of the three, the E-350 is the soberest: 15.7 percent of its drivers in fatal crashes showed any impairment, against 22.1 percent for the E-150 and 19.0 for the E-250.[1] Drunk driving does not explain a sevenfold gap. What explains it sits behind the rear axle. Ford rated the E-150 wagon for eight passengers, max. The E-350 wagon carried twelve in regular length and fifteen in the extended body, which is the configuration that made the evening news for twenty years.[2] Every occupant is a sandbag stacked above the floor, and the center of gravity climbs with each one.

NHTSA measured exactly how that ends, in a research sample of single-vehicle crashes. In the agency's own 15-passenger van research, vans carrying fewer than five occupants rolled over in 12.3 percent of single-vehicle crashes. Load ten or more aboard and the rollover ratio jumps to 35.4 percent, nearly triple. Stuff more than fifteen people into a fifteen-passenger van and seven out of ten single-vehicle crashes end on the roof, seventy percent.[3] At that point you are not driving a van so much as piloting a tall, narrow room full of unrestrained physics, and the E-350 was the only E-Series with enough seats to reach the worst rows of that table.

Which brings us to the Federal Register: on August 17, NHTSA published a request for comments, docket NHTSA-2026-1651, proposing to pull the dynamic rollover test, the infamous Fishhook maneuver, out of the New Car Assessment Program, along with side airbag out-of-position testing. Comments close October 16, thirteen days from now.[4] The agency's case is not frivolous, and I will state it the way its engineers would. No vehicle has tipped up in the Fishhook in fifteen years. Electronic stability control, mandatory since the FMVSS 126 phase-in finished in 2011, runs about 70 percent effective against first-event rollovers. Even back when vans did tip, the dynamic portion moved the calculated rollover risk by 1.6 to 4.8 percentage points, meaning the tape measure was always doing the heavy lifting. A test that passes everyone tells buyers nothing, which is fair.

Now the part I would argue with, over drinks, at length. Congress did not write 15-passenger vans into the rollover program by accident. The TREAD Act of 2000 ordered a dynamic rollover consumer test in the wreckage of the Explorer and Firestone era, and the 2005 highway bill deliberately named 12- and 15-passenger vans for star ratings because loaded vans were rolling and killing people.[5] The reason the Fishhook stopped failing is that the vans it was written for aged out of showrooms. In my data, 84.4 percent of E-350 deaths are in pre-2012 model years, built before stability control was mandatory. The test looks obsolete only if you shop new. Nobody shops new for a fifteen-passenger van; churches, schools, and shuttle operators buy fifteen-year-old E-350s with 140,000 miles and a cassette player, which is where 776 bodies came from, and the geometry problem did not retire with the nameplate. The van Ford built to replace it, the Transit, carries a one-star rollover rating.[6] The Static Stability Factor that would replace the Fishhook is a ratio measured once, with an empty van. A loaded van's center of gravity is not a fixed property of the vehicle, which was the entire point.

The sharp edges on this dataset, stated plainly, are as follows. FARS does not record body configuration, so the E-350 nameplate mixes cargo vans, fifteen-passenger wagons, and cutaway chassis running as ambulances and airport shuttles. I cannot isolate the wagon's rate from the pile. The dataset also imputes roughly 13,500 miles a year to every vehicle, and an E-350 in shuttle or ambulance service drives far more than that, so part of the sevenfold gap is exposure rather than per-mile risk. If the big vans average double the miles, the per-mile gap is closer to three and a half, still large, and the occupancy mechanism covers the rest. The FARS window is 2014 to 2023 while the NCAP change lands on model year 2027, and NCAP only rates new vehicles, a category the E-350 wagon exited in 2014. My argument is about the van class Congress legislated into the program, not about E-350s on dealer lots. Finally, NHTSA's occupancy rollover ratios come from the agency's single-vehicle crash research, not from my cross-tab.

And the strongest case for the other side, at full strength, because it deserves it. Test capacity is finite, and crash-test slots and staff hours are the actual currency of the program, which NHTSA wants to spend on THOR dummies, the frontal oblique test, and the crash-avoidance rating Congress has now ordered twice and never received. Every one of those would tell buyers more than a Fishhook that passes everyone. Deleting a dead test to fund live ones is honest triage, and regulators almost never publish a document conceding one of their own procedures stopped working. But a consumer rating is worth exactly the distance between it and the legal minimum, and the Fishhook was the only dynamic check ever applied to the one vehicle class Congress singled out by name. When the test stops separating the good from the merely adequate, the usual answer is a meaner test, not no test. The agency chose no test, and banked the savings against upgrades that just slid to 2029 and beyond.

What to do: If your church, school, or shuttle service runs a fifteen-passenger van, the rollover star is the one that matters, and it still reflects a dynamic test through the current ratings. Read it before the tape measure takes over. Then practice the cheapest safety device ever invented: loading discipline. Ten or more occupants nearly triples the rollover ratio, so keep heavy weight low and forward, never on the roof, and belt every passenger, every trip. Confirm the van actually has stability control and that it works; anything from the pre-2012 era may not. Shopping the used market, understand that the 1-ton van is not the half-ton van with a bigger engine. It is a different risk class, and the price should reflect that. The proposal is not final: comments on docket NHTSA-2026-1651 close October 16 at regulations.gov. And before any long trip in any van, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. It is free, it takes ninety seconds, and it is the only test nobody is proposing to delete.
