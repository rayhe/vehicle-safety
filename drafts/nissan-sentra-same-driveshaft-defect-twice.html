Nissan Recalled the Sentra for the Same Driveshaft Defect Twice. The Repair Got 50% Longer.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
August 15, 2026

In June 2024, Nissan recalled an unspecified number of 2024 Sentras under NHTSA campaign 24V-304 because the front left driveshaft wasn't fully seated in the CVT assembly.[1] The prescribed fix took about seven hours at a dealership. On July 3, 2026, Nissan filed campaign 26V-410 for 946 units of the 2025 Sentra built over a three-day window in September 2025 at the Aguascalientes II plant in Mexico.[2] Identical defect, from the same assembly line, affecting the same left driveshaft with the same seating failure. Only now the repair takes up to 10.5 hours, because Nissan added a full CVT replacement on top of the driveshaft swap.

50%
Increase in repair time between the first and second recall for the same defect

That escalation is the quiet confession buried in the paperwork. When a manufacturer voluntarily expands the scope of a fix from "replace the driveshaft" to "replace the driveshaft AND the entire continuously variable transmission," they're admitting the original repair wasn't enough. An improperly seated driveshaft doesn't just sit there politely; it leaks transmission fluid, starves the CVT of pressure, and can destroy the internals before anyone notices. A customer who walks out of the dealer after the first recall's seven-hour driveshaft swap might be driving on a CVT that was already chewing itself apart during the weeks or months the shaft was loose.

Nissan's own filing names the root cause: a supplier production-tooling error that made the bearing end slightly oversized, preventing the driveshaft spline from fully engaging the differential gear.[3] That tooling existed when the 2024 models rolled off the line. It still existed when the 2025 models rolled off the same line in September 2025, more than a year after the first recall was filed. Nissan caught 13 warranty claims, three technical reports, and two consumer affairs cases before pulling the trigger on the second recall. The earliest report came in fall 2025 when a customer got a "CVT reduced power" warning mid-drive and a dealer found a fluid leak pooling under the driver's side front axle.

Nissan estimates 3.8% of the 946 recalled vehicles actually have the defect, which works out to roughly 36 cars. That's a small number, tiny even, but the pattern matters more than the count. A recall is supposed to fix production, not just production output. If the tooling that caused campaign 24V-304 survived into the next model year's build window, the recall fixed the cars already built while leaving the factory free to build more of the same problem. Sentra owners already sit on a model with 2,571 deaths in FARS and a fatality rate of 2.13 per 100 million VMT, an entry-level sedan disproportionately driven by young and budget-constrained buyers who can least afford to be blindsided by a loss of drive power at highway speed.[4]

The strongest argument in Nissan's favor: no injuries, no crashes, and a three-day production window is more quality escape than systemic meltdown. Fair. But the counterargument writes itself. A quality escape implies the system caught the error. Their quality system did not catch the error. Supplier tooling persisted across model year boundaries, and two customers driving their new cars discovered it the hard way before Nissan's internal process flagged it. That is not a quality system catching an escape. That is a quality system sleeping through the alarm twice.

If you own a 2024 or 2025 Nissan Sentra, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for both campaigns. The symptoms are distinctive: sudden "CVT reduced power" message, grinding or abnormal noise from the transmission, and visible fluid leak under the driver's side front axle. A 30-second VIN lookup beats a 10.5-hour dealership visit you didn't plan for, and it definitely beats losing drive power on a freeway merge.
