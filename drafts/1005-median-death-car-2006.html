Half of America's Traffic Deaths Happen in Cars From 2006 or Older

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 28, 2026

I took all 187,058 deaths in the FARS database from 2014 through 2023, lined them up by the model year of the vehicle involved, and found the middle one. The median death car is a 2006. Twenty years old. Half of everyone who dies on American roads dies in a twenty-year-old vehicle, and the industry keeps holding press conferences about next year's driver-assist software, technology that will protect the buyers of 2027 cars while the vehicles doing the actual killing rolled off the line when flip phones were still cool.

2006
Median model year of the vehicle in a fatal crash, 2014–2023 (mean: 2006.7)

Here is the distribution, because the median undersells it. Cars from model year 1999 or earlier account for 15.0% of deaths (28,036 people), and model year 2008 or earlier accounts for 62.3%. 71.9% of all deaths involve vehicles built before the federal electronic stability control mandate, which took full effect for model year 2012. Only 7.6% of deaths happened in cars from 2018 or newer. The single deadliest model year on record is 2005, with 11,363 deaths. The single deadliest model-and-year cell in the entire database is the 2001 Ford F-150, with 672.

Cut it by class and the picture gets older. Pickups have the oldest median death vehicle of any class: 2004. By make, Buick's median death car is a 2003, GMC's a 2004, and Chevrolet, Ford, Dodge, and Jeep all sit at 2005, while Toyota and Honda land on 2006. Then the curve bends upward for a reason that flatters nobody's engineering: Nissan (2010), Hyundai (2012), Kia (2015). Those brands aren't safer; their fleets are just younger, because they sold most of their cars recently. This metric is a mirror of fleet age, not a safety ranking, and I am saying that now so nobody turns it into one later.

The question is why any of this matters, and the answer is that 2006 sits on the wrong side of the most important safety line in modern automotive history. IIHS research found electronic stability control cuts the risk of a fatal single-vehicle crash by about 56%, and NHTSA's final rule made ESC standard on every light vehicle starting with model year 2012, estimating it would save 5,300 to 9,600 lives a year. A 2006 probably doesn't have it, and meanwhile the fleet keeps aging: S&P Global Mobility puts the average American light vehicle at a record 12.8 years old, with passenger cars at 14.5. We are mandating safety technology for cars people aren't buying while the cars people are dying in predate the mandates.

Now the objection, stated at full strength, because it deserves it. Old cars are cheap cars, and cheap cars are driven by young drivers, poor drivers, rural drivers, and unbelted drivers, which means vehicle age and driver risk are hopelessly tangled in this cut, which is exactly why the honest version of this article is a description of where the bodies are rather than a proof of what killed them. There is also survivorship bias working against the old fleet: the 2006s still on the road in 2023 are disproportionately high-mileage workhorses, and pickups (median 2004) are Exhibit A for the exposure story. I cannot separate the car's age from the driver's risk with this data. What I can say is descriptive and still damning: wherever the cause lives, the killing happens in old metal, and barely a quarter of deaths involve ESC-mandate-era vehicles.

So here is the actionable part, and it is the cheapest safety advice on this site. If you are buying used, buy 2012 or newer. That is the ESC line. 2018 or newer gets you widely available automatic emergency braking on top of it. A pre-2009 car in your driveway is the single biggest changeable risk factor in this entire dataset, bigger than brand, bigger than body style, and it costs nothing to check: run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because old cars also carry old unrepaired recalls. Nobody is coming to retrofit your 2005, and if yours is older than your teenager, replacing it is the biggest safety upgrade money can buy.
