The Government Wants Your Car to Catch You Drunk. The Math Says It Will Mostly Catch Sober People.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, drunk drivers kill roughly 12,000 Americans a year. Congress responded the way Congress responds: it ordered a machine to fix it. Section 24220 of the 2021 infrastructure law told NHTSA to require a passive drunk-driver detector in every new car by November 2024.[1] That deadline is nineteen months in the rearview mirror, and zero rules have been issued. And the agency's own arithmetic says that even a detector that worked 99.9 percent of the time would wrongly stop or slow millions to tens of millions of sober drivers a year.[2]

9,409
Lives per year IIHS says perfect drunk-driver detection would save. Perfect detection does not exist yet, and imperfect detection is not close.

NHTSA opened the rulemaking in January 2024 with an advance notice that drew 18,367 public comments. The docket is still marked pending; its last update was June. The agency's own studies, summarized in its report to Congress, could not find commercially available technology that "detects driver alcohol impairment accurately and passively."[3] Read that twice. The regulator Congress ordered to mandate the technology has certified, in writing, that the technology is not good enough to mandate.

This is where the math gets uncomfortable, because the advocates' math is excellent. IIHS researcher Chuck Farmer ran the FARS data from 2015 through 2018 and found that preventing anyone with a BAC of 0.08 or higher from driving would have averted an average of 9,409 deaths a year, net of crashes that would have happened anyway. Lower the limit to 0.05 and the number rises to 10,500; if nobody drank at all, 12,000.[4] That is roughly a quarter of all traffic fatalities. Almost nothing else in road safety touches that number.

Now run NHTSA's arithmetic against it: take the low end of the agency's own range, two million wrong interventions a year, and divide by 9,409 lives saved. That is about 213 wrongly warned, speed-limited, or ignition-locked sober drivers per life saved, and it assumes the fleet turns over overnight, which it does not, because a mandate would cover only new cars and the American fleet takes fifteen years to replace itself. Take the high end of NHTSA's range and the ratio gets ten times worse. Perfect detection saves a quarter of all traffic deaths. Merely excellent detection harasses a city the size of Houston every year.

Even the institution supplying the 9,409 figure cannot quite decide what it believes. IIHS told NHTSA the detection technology "is not readily available in the marketplace," then urged the agency to mandate it anyway, arguing that "a regulatory requirement to equip passenger motor vehicles will inspire the additional effort needed to make it a reality."[5] Seatbelts, airbags, stability control: the pitch is that you mandate first and invent second. Meanwhile, seatbelts were never asked to distinguish your breath chemistry from your breakfast.

While America waits for a rule that may never come, Europe already has its cameras and wrote the legal envelope around them. Since July 7, every new car registered in the EU carries an infrared driver-monitoring camera, and the same regulation that mandates the sensor mandates what it may do with the data: no continuous recording, no third-party access, immediate deletion after processing, and a flat ban on identifying the person.[6] In America, the identical hardware ships in the identical dashboard, and the law governing it is whatever the manufacturer's privacy policy says, plus a patchwork of state biometric statutes; hardware crosses borders, statutes don't. One supplier of this software, Seeing Machines, reported 4.8 million cars on the road in March, up 67 percent year over year.[6] The camera base is installed; the legal framework is not.

Be fair to the other side, because the other side has corpses on its side of the ledger. MADD's coalition calls it 37 lives a day, a 33 percent increase since 2019, and says NHTSA's delay is costing a body count it publishes in press releases.[7] A stranded sober driver is an inconvenience; a drunk driver is a fatality. Ignition interlocks already prove the concept for convicted offenders, and a Johns Hopkins survey found 65 percent of Americans want impairment prevention as standard equipment, more than want speed limiters.[4] If you believe regulation invents technology, mandating is the moral move. If you believe two million false accusations a year is a civil-liberties fire, it is not. Both readings fit the same numbers, so pick yours; the docket is still open.

What the data does not prove: FARS alcohol-impaired deaths are modeled estimates, since many fatally injured drivers are never tested. The 9,409 figure is a counterfactual model, not an observed outcome, and it assumes no behavioral adaptation, like drunk drivers keeping their old cars longer. NHTSA's millions-to-tens-of-millions is a scenario bound, not a measurement, and a warning chime costs far less than an ignition lock. The breath-and-touch sensors from the DADSS research program are a different technology than distraction cameras; conflating them is a category error the debate keeps making.[8] And Europe's ADDW rule targets distraction and drowsiness, not alcohol, so the parallel is about regulatory philosophy, not identical function.

What you can actually do starts with reading the driver-monitoring section of your car's privacy policy, not the manual, because in the United States that document is your regulator. Check whether your car's disable option stays off or resets at every ignition cycle; Europe's rule forces a reset, and several American implementations copied the behavior. Do not hold your breath for showroom alcohol detection; the DADSS breath and touch sensors are the furthest along, and "ready by the end of 2025" was an acknowledgement, not a shipment.[7] And support ignition interlocks for convicted offenders: they avert at most 837 deaths a year, under nine percent of the preventable total, but it is deployed technology, not vaporware, which in this particular debate counts as a distinguishing feature.
