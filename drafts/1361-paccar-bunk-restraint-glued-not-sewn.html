Paccar Glued the Seatbelt Onto Your Bed. The Sewing Was Optional.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 2, 2026

Somewhere in a factory, a human being held a roll of seatbelt webbing up to a sleeper bunk, reached for the glue gun, and thought: good enough. That decision is now NHTSA campaign 26V674000, covering 310 brand-new Kenworth and Peterbilt trucks, and Paccar estimates 0.6 percent of them, call it two cabs, actually got the glued-not-sewn treatment.[1]

0.6%
of the 310 recalled trucks Paccar estimates actually have the defect: roughly two cabs

Here is the part that rearranges your furniture. In team trucking, one driver drives while the other sleeps in the bunk, behind the seats, in a vehicle doing sixty-five. Federal regulation 49 CFR 393.76 exists precisely because someone sleeping in a moving truck is an occupant, and occupants get restraints. The bunk has a seatbelt. A seatbelt is webbing plus stitching plus an anchor, held together by the full attention of industrial civilization. One of those steps did not happen.[2]

Glue, to be clear, is not nothing. Glue holds shoes together, and it holds the binding on paperback novels you read in bunks. But a bunk restraint has exactly one job, and that job begins at the moment a multi-ton vehicle stops obeying physics in an orderly fashion. Under those conditions, glue is a suggestion; stitching is a commitment.

The trucks were built in July and August 2026, and the recall was filed October 14. Two months from assembly line to federal filing, which tells you this was not discovered through the usual archaeology of warranty claims and field reports. Someone looked at a bunk restraint, looked at the webbing, and noticed the stitching was missing. The trucks had not even accumulated dust.[2] The Peterbilt 589 accounts for 101 of the 310, and the Kenworth T880 another 81: two models hold nearly three-fifths of the recall population, as reported.[2]

Now the part where I argue against myself, because the counterargument deserves its full weight. Two trucks. That is the honest exposure: Paccar's 0.6 percent of 310, no injuries reported, no crashes, and the company's interim guidance is simply to not use the bunk restraints until dealers inspect and replace them.[3] The Crash Report has covered recalls of six cars; this one still clears that bar, but let us not inflate it: two cabs, a glue gun, and an unfinished step.

And yet. A seatbelt exists downstream of a promise: that the end of the line checked. The verification step looked at webbing that was glued but not sewn and passed it. That is the finding that matters, because the federal standard assumes stitching and the factory delivered adhesive, and if the quality gate cannot distinguish between those two states on a restraint, it is worth asking what else it cannot distinguish, on trucks built in the same two months.[4]

What the data does not show: whether the glued-but-unsewn restraints appeared across shifts or from one supplier batch, how the missing stitch escaped in-process inspection, or whether the concentration in the Peterbilt 589 (101 of the 310) reflects anything beyond production mix. Those answers are inside Paccar's Part 573 chronology, and the filing does not publish them.

If you drive or fleet a 2027 Peterbilt 567, 579, or 589, or a 2027 Kenworth T680, T880, W900, or W990, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls under campaign 26V674 (Paccar 26PACH) and call Kenworth at 425-828-5888 or Peterbilt at 940-591-4220. Do not use the bunk restraint until the dealer inspects it. Sleep tight is not a strategy.[1]
