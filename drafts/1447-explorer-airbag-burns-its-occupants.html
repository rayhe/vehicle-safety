The Airbag Worked. Then It Burned the Officer It Just Saved.

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Editor
November 9, 2026

On February 20, 2025, a UPS truck hit the right front of a 2022 Ford Explorer Police Interceptor as it cleared an intersection. Side airbag deployed exactly as designed. It also burned through the officer's vest, sweater, undershirt, and T-shirt, then kept going. Third-degree burns underneath. Surgery. A skin graft. That complaint is one of eight behind Preliminary Evaluation PE26008, which NHTSA opened on November 6, 2026, into an estimated 1,236,155 Ford Explorers from model years 2021 through 2026.[1][2]

90.9%
Share of the 11 incidents NHTSA counted (10 of 11) that produced second- or third-degree burns requiring medical treatment. Zero deaths. (NHTSA ODI, PE26008)

Start with what the bag is. A seat-mounted side airbag lives in the outer bolster of the front seat, inches from your ribs. It inflates faster than a frontal bag because there is almost no crush space between you and whatever just hit the door, so it fires with a small pyrotechnic charge and vents hot gas somewhere. Where that gas goes is the whole story. Three of the eight complaints describe the same arrangement: the exhaust port facing inward, toward the person in the seat, instead of outward toward the door. A fourth names the exhaust port as the burn source without saying which way it faced. Half the complaints point at a vent.[2]

Read the complaints in the officers' own words and the pattern stops looking like coincidence. A 2023 Explorer ST owner wrote that the exhaust port faced inward and "caused the hot chemical from the airbag to push out all the gas at the left side of my body, melting my clothes and causing chemical burns to my side." A 2026 Police Interceptor officer took second- and third-degree burns to the hip and side abdomen because, in his words, "the airbag vent faces towards the driver when it deploys." A second officer, in a 2024 Interceptor, watched the vented gas melt components on his police utility belt and wrote that without the belt "there is a high probability that the driver would have suffered burns similar to one of our other officers." Three complaints. Same direction. Same injury. NHTSA has made no finding on vent direction, and that sentence matters, but three independent people describing the same geometry is not nothing.[2]

Police Interceptors account for three of the eight complaints, which is a lot for one trim. Run the partial VINs through NHTSA's own decoder and three of eight come back as Explorer Police models with the 3.3-liter engine. Two of the three police complaints describe duty gear sitting in the path of the gas, a vest in one case and a utility belt in the other. Civilians made up the other five complaints: three XLTs, one ST, one Timberline, all from model years 2021 to 2023. Nobody in a back seat reported a burn. Nobody reported a frontal bag doing this. Only the seat-mounted side bag, only the front row.[2]

Not every complaint agrees on the mechanism, and honesty requires saying so. Two complainants blamed the curtain airbag, not the seat bag. One described a side bag that burst open and left unreacted propellant on the driver's skin, a burn that grew to the size of a fist and ate through skin over the following days. NHTSA grouped all eight under the seat-mounted side airbag anyway, which means sorting out which bag burned whom is part of what the investigation has to settle. A preliminary evaluation is not a recall, not a defect finding, and not a repair you can schedule. NHTSA gave itself eight months to decide whether these burns point to a defect, and Ford had not commented publicly as of the morning of November 9.[1][3]

Earlier Explorer airbag recalls fixed fasteners, not burns. Recall 20V497 in August 2020 covered side airbag attachments that may not have been properly tightened. Recall 21V652 in August 2021 covered side airbag weld studs that could detach during deployment and fail the federal side-impact standard. In September 2026, Ford recalled seven 2026 Explorers because the active vent on the passenger frontal airbag may not open as intended, a dashboard bag, not the seat bag. None of the Explorer recall summaries NHTSA lists for model years 2020 through 2026 mentions burns. This probe is the first time the word appears.[2]

Context makes the nameplate sting. Ford issued 153 recalls covering almost 13 million vehicles in 2025, breaking a record GM had held since 2014, and has published 85 more in 2026 covering over 14 million vehicles. This site's own NHTSA API pull on November 7 found the Explorer is the most-recalled 2026-model-year passenger vehicle in America, with 14 separate campaigns. Now add a federal probe into whether its side airbag vents hot gas at the people it protects. Here is the pairing that should worry you more than the probe itself: in NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System for 2014 through 2023, the Explorer killed 3,797 people in 6,626 fatal crashes, a rate of 1.54 deaths per 100 million miles. That is 4.4 times the median SUV rate of 0.35, and 8.1 times the Toyota RAV4's 0.19. Ranked ninth of 337 models by total deaths. An SUV that ends up in fatal crashes this often is an SUV whose side airbag fires a lot, which is exactly the bag now under investigation for burning the person sitting next to it.[4][5][6]

Now the other side, stated at full strength, because eight complaints in 1.2 million vehicles is 0.00065 percent of the population. Every one of these bags deployed in a real crash, doing the job it was built for, and nobody died. Burns arrived in crashes that could have killed the occupants without the bag, and airbags vent hot gas by design, so some burn risk is intrinsic to a device that inflates with a small explosion inches from your ribs. If this PE closes with no defect finding, the story is eight unlucky people, not a scandal. Fair. But that reading has to survive the vent pattern, because "airbags sometimes burn people" and "this airbag vents at people" are different claims, and three complaints describing the same inward-facing exhaust is the difference between them. Ford has eight months of NHTSA's clock to explain which one it is.[3]

There is no recall and no repair to schedule, so here is what you can actually do. If you own a 2021 through 2026 Explorer, or you drive a Police Interceptor, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Investigation information appears with the vehicle search, and the Explorer already carries 14 other campaigns from this model year alone. If a side airbag deployed and burned you, file a complaint at NHTSA.gov or call the Vehicle Safety Hotline at 888-327-4236. There is no minimum complaint count to open an investigation, and staff may contact you for receipts or photos. Photograph the deployed bag, the seat, and the damaged clothing, because that is the evidence a complaint can carry. Fleet managers running Interceptors should ask their officers whether any deployment left burns and log the answer. An airbag that saves your life and then burns you is still better than no airbag. It is also a design choice somebody made, and NHTSA is now asking who made it and why the vent points the wrong way.
