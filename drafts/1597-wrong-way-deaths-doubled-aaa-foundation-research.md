## Angle (1-2 sentences)
Wrong-way crashes on divided highways nearly doubled in a decade — 278 fatal crashes in 2014 to 520 in 2023, deaths 373 to 699 — while total US traffic deaths rose far more slowly. The AAA Foundation's March 2026 research brief (the first national update since 2021) profiles the wrong-way driver with unusual precision: six in ten are over the .12 BAC line, one in five is over 80 years old in odds terms, 86% are alone, 81% of the crashes happen in the dark. The composite driver is drunk, old, alone, lost, and driving a car that isn't theirs.

## Verified facts (all from the AAA Foundation brief, PDF read directly this run)

### Primary: AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety, "Fatal Wrong-Way Crashes on Divided Highways, United States, 2014–2023," Research Brief, March 2026
https://aaafoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/202603-AAAFTS-Wrong-Way-Crashes.pdf
(Based on NHTSA FARS; update of the Foundation's 2021 brief covering 2010–2018.)

1. **The doubling:** 4,164 fatal wrong-way crashes on divided highways, 2014–2023, killing 5,730 people. Annual fatal crashes rose from 278 in 2014 to 520 in 2023 (about double); their share of all fatal crashes on divided highways rose from 3.4% to 4.6%. Deaths rose from 373 in 2014 to 699 in 2023. Yearly deaths: 2014: 373; 2015: 444; 2016: 511; 2017: 508; 2018: 505; 2019: 559; 2020: 572; 2021: 763; 2022: 796 (peak); 2023: 699. Wrong-way fatalities rose faster than total traffic fatalities over the same period.
2. **Alcohol, the dominant factor:** nearly 70% of wrong-way drivers had BAC above 0.08. BAC 0.08–0.12: odds ratio 18.6 (matched) / 16.3 (non-matched) vs BAC 0. BAC above 0.12: 59.9% of all wrong-way drivers, odds ratio 83.1 (matched) / 72.2 (non-matched). BAC 0.01–0.079: OR 6.4 / 7.4. Only 20.2% of wrong-way drivers had BAC 0.
3. **Age, the second factor:** drivers 80+ had odds ratios of 20.3 (matched) / 21.3 (non-matched) vs the 50–59 reference group. Ages 70–79: OR 6.8 / 5.5. Ages 60–69: OR 2.0 / 2.1. Drivers 20–39 were more than half of all wrong-way drivers but were NOT at significantly elevated odds — the young are numerous, the old are disproportionate.
4. **License:** 14.0% of wrong-way drivers had expired/revoked/suspended licenses (OR 2.4); 8.3% were not licensed at all (OR 2.3). Valid license: 76.3%.
5. **Alone:** 86.4% of wrong-way drivers had no passengers. One passenger: OR 0.3. Two passengers: OR 0.2. Three or more: OR 0.2. A passenger is the cheapest wrong-way countermeasure in the dataset.
6. **Vehicle:** passenger cars 57.9% of wrong-way drivers. Large trucks/buses: only 1.5% of wrong-way drivers vs 15.9% (matched) / 19.9% (non-matched) of right-way drivers — OR 0.1. The truck is almost always the victim, not the wrong-way driver. Motorcycles 2.2%, OR 0.6 / 0.1.
7. **Vehicle age:** nearly half of wrong-way vehicles were more than 10 years old. Vehicles over 20 years old: OR 2.0 / 1.5 vs vehicles 5 years or newer.
8. **Distance from home (new finding):** drivers more than 250 miles from their home zip code had OR 1.8 (non-matched; not significant in matched). The far-from-home effect is real in one model, weak in the other — report both.
9. **Lighting (new finding):** 80.7% of wrong-way fatal crashes happened in darkness (OR 2.4); dawn/dusk OR 2.0. Only 16.4% in daylight.
10. **Rural (new finding):** 34.6% of wrong-way crashes were rural; odds 1.8x higher in rural areas than urban on divided highways.
11. **Ownership (new finding):** stolen vehicles were 0.6% of wrong-way drivers but carried OR 35.8 (matched) / 70.0 (non-matched). Vehicles registered to another private owner: 29.9% of wrong-way drivers, OR 1.2. Driver = owner: 62.5%.
12. **Sex:** male 70.6% of wrong-way drivers, but no significant odds difference by sex.
13. **States (Appendix A):** Texas 894 wrong-way deaths (6.3% of the state's divided-highway deaths), California 532 (3.8%), Florida 406 (3.0%). Highest shares: Washington 9.2%, South Dakota 8.9%, Connecticut 8.3%. Lowest counts: Vermont 3, Alaska 3, Hawaii 3, DC 2.
14. **Countermeasures (brief, pp. 8–10):** driver-based (DUI enforcement/education, older-driver interchange navigation education); vehicle-based (alcohol interlocks, in-vehicle detection, app-based warnings via navigation apps — Bosch cloud-based warning, TxDOT); roadway-based (larger/more/low-mounted signage, retroreflective materials, blank-out signs, LED borders, rapid-flashing beacons, in-lane arrows, directional rumble strips); system-based (integrated detection + alert networks in 14 states: AZ, CA, CO, CT, FL, IA, MI, MO, NV, OH, TX, RI, WI, WA — several report more drivers self-correcting).

## Original contribution (novel analysis for the article)
1. **The growth-rate comparison nobody ran:** wrong-way deaths +87.4% (2014→2023) vs all US traffic deaths +24.9% over the same span. The wrong-way problem grew roughly 3.5x faster than the overall death toll — while the overall toll itself was rising. This is the brief's own "increased even more" claim, quantified.
2. **The composite driver:** no single factor explains the crash; the brief's odds ratios stack. A driver who is over .12 (OR ~80), over 80 (OR ~20), alone (passenger OR 0.3 inverted = ~3.3x risk vs accompanied), at night (OR 2.4), rural (OR 1.8), 250+ miles from home (OR 1.8), in a 20-year-old borrowed car — the profile is a checklist, and Thanksgiving night checks nearly every box at once.
3. **The victim asymmetry:** large trucks and buses are 1.5% of wrong-way drivers but roughly a fifth of the right-way drivers in these crashes. In a wrong-way crash, the oncoming truck driver is usually the one who did nothing wrong and still dies. The brief's table proves it; no prior site article has said it.
4. **The Texas concentration:** one state, 15.6% of the national wrong-way death toll; three states, 32%. Wrong-way death is not evenly distributed — it follows big-state highway mileage and, in share terms, Washington (9.2%) and South Dakota (8.9%) punch far above their size.

## Actionable insights (for article)
1. If you see headlights coming at you on a divided highway: slow down, move right, flash your lights, call 911. Wrong-way drivers hug what they think is their right — your left — so the right lane is the safer one. (Standard guidance; the brief's countermeasure section and MassDOT guidance agree.)
2. If you realize YOU are the wrong-way driver: pull to the shoulder, stop, hazards on, call 911. Do not attempt a U-turn across highway lanes at speed.
3. If you are driving tonight (Thanksgiving) or any holiday night: the profile is the risk — drunk, dark, alone, far from home. A passenger is a measurable safety device (OR 0.2–0.3); bring one, and make them the navigator.
4. Families of drivers 80+: the odds ratio at 80+ is ~20x. Night driving on unfamiliar divided highways is the specific exposure to remove first — not all driving, that trip.
5. If you drive a car over 20 years old: the odds are ~2x, and the brief ties it to older vehicles lacking modern driver-assist and lighting. Not a reason to scrap the car; a reason to be extra careful at unfamiliar interchanges after dark.
6. Check whether your state runs wrong-way detection (14 states do, per the brief). If it doesn't, the ask is concrete: detection systems plus blank-out signs at high-risk ramps.

## Limitations (must state in article)
- The brief covers divided highways only (Interstates, freeways, expressways, divided arterials, and their ramps). Wrong-way crashes on undivided roads are not counted, so 5,730 is a floor for the true national wrong-way toll, not the total.
- Cases were identified from FARS violation/driver-factor fields; FARS has no universal wrong-way flag, so some wrong-way crashes were certainly missed and some misclassified.
- Drug impairment was NOT examined (data-quality concerns, per the brief). Given the alcohol association, other impairment likely contributes — unmeasured.
- BAC values were partly imputed with a custom model; the odds ratios are model-based estimates with wide confidence intervals at the extremes (e.g., stolen-vehicle OR 35.8, CI 4.7–272.2 — the point estimate is unstable; say so).
- The far-from-home effect (OR 1.8) was significant in the non-matched model only, not the matched model. Report both; do not overclaim it.
- The brief is March 2026; the data end in 2023. It is the latest national accounting available, but it cannot say whether the doubling continued into 2024–2025.
- Odds ratios compare wrong-way drivers to right-way drivers in fatal crashes — they measure relative risk of being the wrong-way driver, not the absolute probability of a wrong-way crash on any given trip.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)
Wrong-way deaths are ~1.8% of the annual US traffic toll — 699 deaths in 2023 against 39,254 total in 2024. Speeding kills roughly 11,000 a year, alcohol impairment roughly 12,000. Every dollar spent on wrong-way detection trailers, blank-out signs, and LED borders is a dollar not spent on interlocks, speed management, or pedestrian infrastructure that would save more lives per dollar. The doubling is real but starts from a small base (373 deaths), and the profile is a composite — almost no real driver carries all the risk factors at once, so the "80x" and "20x" odds ratios describe extreme slices, not the typical case. The rational critic says this is a vivid, terrifying, statistically minor problem, and the detection network is security infrastructure for a fear that kills fewer people than a bad week of speeding. The article must carry this, not wave it away: the wrong-way crash is the most cinematic way to die on an American highway, and the least common.
