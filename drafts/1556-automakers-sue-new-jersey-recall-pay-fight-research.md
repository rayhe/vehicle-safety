## Story in one line
On November 20, 2026, Ford, Honda, Stellantis, and Volkswagen sued New Jersey in federal court to block a law that forces them to pay dealers retail rates for warranty and recall repairs. That same day, Honda and Toyota recalled about 1.6 million vehicles over Takata airbags and Stellantis recalled 82,843 Rams. Every one of those free repairs has to be done by a franchised dealer. The four automakers sued the people who do the work, on the day they assigned them 1.2 million more jobs.

## Key verified facts
- **The plaintiffs:** Ford Motor Company, Honda (American Honda Motor Co.), FCA US LLC (Stellantis), Volkswagen Group of America. Filed November 20, 2026, in federal court in Trenton, New Jersey.
- **The law:** New Jersey Motor Vehicle Open Recall Notice and Fair Compensation Act (S3309/A4380). Signed September 11, 2025, by former Governor Phil Murphy. Effective April 1, 2026.
- **What the law does:** (a) dealers must be reimbursed at no less than the average retail labor rate, retail time allowance, and retail parts markup for warranty and recall work; (b) dealers holding a vehicle under a stop-sale or do-not-drive order get at least 1.5% of trade-in value per month until parts arrive, capped at trade-in value; (c) dealers may not be punished with chargebacks or incentive exclusions for asserting claims; (d) the Motor Vehicle Commission must send open-recall notices with registration renewals, pointing owners to NHTSA's database and stating repairs are free at authorized dealers; (e) dealers must check NHTSA for open recalls before selling a used car at retail.
- **What the automakers claim:** the law hands dealers a "blank check" to set prices, bars manufacturers from avoiding "extortionate" results, shifts more than $375 million a year to dealers as a "windfall" with no obligation to spend it on service or wages, and has already produced bills "for hours that no technician works" that exceed both comparable dealer charges and retail customer prices. They also object to paying marked-up prices on parts dealers got cheap or free, and to being barred from supplying cheaper parts.
- **The same-day ledger:** Honda 810,592 (26V765) + Toyota 314,029 (26V748) + Stellantis 82,843 (26V756) = 1,207,464 US vehicles recalled in the November 19-20 window. The four plaintiffs account for 893,435 of those (Honda + Stellantis); Toyota, which did not join the suit, accounts for the other 314,029.
- **The toll behind the recalls:** at least 28 deaths in the US from defective Takata inflators since 2009; about 4.7 million US vehicles still carried unrepaired Takata airbags as of September 2026 (Carfax, via #1123).
- **The completion gap:** half of all recalled vehicles never get fixed within the reporting window (50.3% completion, February 2026). For cars 5 to 10 years old, completion is 44%. For cars past 10 years, about 29%.

## Original contribution (Clara's math and synthesis)
1. **The same-day ledger.** 810,592 + 314,029 + 82,843 = 1,207,464 US vehicles were recalled in the November 19-20 window, and every one of those free repairs must be performed by a franchised dealer. The four automakers suing New Jersey over what dealer recall work pays account for 893,435 of those vehicles (74%). The lawsuit and the recalls are not two stories that happened to land on the same day. They are one story: the price of the repair and the volume of the repair arrived together.
2. **The scale check.** The automakers say the law shifts "more than $375 million a year" to dealers. New Jersey's auto retail sector is $45.4 billion (NJ CAR). $375 million is 0.83% of that sector's revenue. That does not make the automakers wrong - $375 million is real money, and it is concentrated on four companies - but it puts the "windfall" in proportion: the fight is over less than one cent in every dollar of New Jersey car retail.
3. **The stop-sale arithmetic.** At 1.5% of trade-in value per month, a dealer holding a $30,000 trade-in vehicle under a do-not-drive order collects $450 a month, $2,700 over six months, capped at $30,000. That is the provision the automakers call a blank check and the dealers call the cost of a car they cannot sell. Both descriptions fit the same formula.
4. **The completion-rate stakes.** The repair pipeline at the center of this fight currently fixes about half of recalled cars (50.3%). The law's other half - the part nobody is suing over - is the open-recall notice that now goes out with every New Jersey registration renewal, telling owners their recall repair is free. The lawsuit is about the pay side of a system whose notification side exists because half the cars never show up.

## Limitations
- The complaint's claims are allegations from one side. The dealers' association has not filed a response in the suit; NJ CAR's September 2025 statement is the dealers' public position, not a legal answer to the complaint.
- The $375 million annual figure is the automakers' estimate in their complaint, not an audited number. No independent accounting of the law's cost has been published.
- Completion rates are national figures, not New Jersey figures. There is no published evidence that the New Jersey law has changed recall completion rates in the state since it took effect April 1, 2026, seven and a half months ago.
- The 1,207,464 same-day total mixes three separate recalls with different defects, different remedies, and different timelines. The Takata inflators will not be available until early 2027; the Ram fix is a dealer software update. Do not imply they are one recall.
- Toyota is not a plaintiff. Do not imply it joined the suit.

## Strongest counterargument
The honest case for the automakers: dealers writing their own reimbursement rates, challengeable only by auditing a sample of their own submitted orders, is a real structural problem, and the complaint's specific charges are concrete - bills for hours no technician worked, charges above what comparable dealers charge for the same work, charges above what retail customers pay for the same job. A $375 million annual transfer with no obligation to spend a dollar of it on service or wages is a windfall by any plain reading, and if automakers pass the cost through, New Jersey car buyers pay it in higher prices. The dealers' answer is that retail rate is what the market already charges, that the audit right exists, and that automakers spent decades paying below cost for warranty work while pocketing the difference. Both sides are describing the same money. The safety question is narrower than either side's rhetoric: whoever is right about the price, the repair has to happen, and right now about half of recalled cars never get it.

## Actionable takeaways
1. If you live in New Jersey, your registration renewal now carries an open-recall notice with a link to NHTSA's database. Read it. The repair is free at an authorized dealer.
2. Anywhere in the US: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Half of recalled cars never get fixed, and the owner who never checks is the reason.
3. Buying a used car in New Jersey: the dealer is now required to check NHTSA for open recalls before selling it at retail. Ask for the check in writing anyway.
4. If you own a 2012-2013 Honda or Acura in a hot, humid state, or a 2003-2017 Toyota or Lexus on the Takata list, or a 2022-2024 Ram 1500 Classic, your car is in the November 19-20 recall wave. Do not wait for the letter. Honda's interim letters go out December 18, 2026; Toyota's December 28, 2026 to January 11, 2027; Ram VINs are searchable November 26, 2026.
