The Gap

The Recall Covers 121,509 Range Rovers. The Investigation Covers 331,559.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 5, 2026

Your front wheel connects to your steering through a cast aluminum knuckle about the size of a grapefruit. Federal investigators now believe that knuckle can crack on 331,559 Range Rovers and Range Rover Sports, model years 2014 through 2022. A crack at the upper clevis lets the upper control arm detach, and a detached arm means the wheel stops answering the steering wheel.[1] In the first 150 milliseconds of that failure, nothing you do with your hands matters anymore.

210,050
Range Rovers with the suspect steering knuckle that the recall does not cover

Jaguar Land Rover recalled 121,509 of them in August 2025, campaign 25V514, model years 2014 through 2017. Its own filing estimates 100 percent of that population carries the defect, which is a striking thing for a manufacturer to write down. Knuckles came from SuperAlloy Industrial in Taiwan. Vehicles outside the recall window, 2018 through 2022, carry a substantially similar design, according to NHTSA's own resume. That is the agency's phrase, not mine, and it is doing a lot of work.[1][3]

NHTSA opened a preliminary evaluation in June 2025 after 12 owners reported fractures. It upgraded the case to Engineering Analysis EA26003 in April 2026. By then the file held 236 incidents, one crash, and zero injuries. Two newest fracture reports in the file were a 2020 and a 2021 Range Rover, both outside the recall. In September, investigators sent Jaguar Land Rover a letter citing 41 crack reports and asking for everything: production data, complaints, warranty claims, and whether each recalled vehicle got a new knuckle or a bracket.[1][2]

A bracket is the remedy for a recalled knuckle with no visible crack, not a new part. A retaining bracket bolted to the upper portion of the same casting, meant to keep the arm from separating completely if the crack arrives later. If the knuckle is already cracked, it gets replaced with a new knuckle of substantially similar design. Read that twice. One fix concedes the crack may come and only limits what it can do. The other replaces the cracked casting with another casting from the same family.[1]

What the failure looks like from inside the car: on July 27, 2026, a 2016 Range Rover moved laterally on SR-62 and hit the back of a semi. Afterward, the right-front knuckle connection was catastrophically separated, the wheel out of its normal position. That truck was under the 25V514 recall, unrepaired. Police wrote it up as an unsafe lane change. Nobody inspected the component. A steering failure a second before impact looks exactly like a driving mistake, which means the federal file's single crash is a floor, not a total. Then, on October 10, another 2016 owner reported the knuckle cracking and disconnecting on the highway during normal driving. The recall notice had arrived, but the part had not.[4]

To be fair to everyone involved, the numbers are small. Two hundred thirty-six incidents across 331,559 vehicles is 0.07 percent. One crash. Zero injuries. Jaguar Land Rover told Canadian regulators there was no field evidence the defect causes a serious loss of steering control, and the recall went ahead anyway. A brace is a legitimate engineering answer; airplanes and bridges use redundant load paths the same way. Maybe this is a rare casting defect being managed carefully, and the engineering analysis is the system working.[1][3]

Maybe, but the management covers 37 percent of the vehicles carrying the part, while the newest fractures sit in the uncovered years, and an owner whose knuckle disconnected on the highway in October 2026 was still waiting on a part a year after the recall. That is what "remedy not yet available" means when you are the one driving.

Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaigns 25V514 and 24V840 if you own a 2014 through 2022 Range Rover or Range Rover Sport. If your 2018 to 2022 is not recalled, there is no fix to collect yet, so ask your dealer to inspect the front upper knuckle joint at every service and photograph it, because a crack at the upper clevis is visible before it separates. If your 2014 to 2017 is recalled, do not accept "part unavailable" as a final answer; press for the bracket at minimum, and never drive on a knuckle with a visible crack. And if your Range Rover ever veers or the steering goes light, stop where you are and demand a component inspection, because a separated knuckle gets written up as driver error when nobody looks at the part.
