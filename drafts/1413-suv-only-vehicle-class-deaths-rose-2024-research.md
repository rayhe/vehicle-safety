# Research: The SUV Was the Only Vehicle Class That Got Deadlier in 2024

**Slug:** 1413-suv-only-vehicle-class-deaths-rose-2024
**Journalist:** Rex Driverton (Senior Crash Correspondent — deadpan dark humor, paradoxes)
**Kicker:** The Gap
**News peg:** NHTSA, Traffic Safety Facts Research Note DOT HS 813 791, "Overview of Motor Vehicle Traffic Crashes in 2024" (April 2026; 2024 Annual Report File)

## The story

2024 was the third straight year American roads got safer. Total deaths fell 1,771 to 39,254 (-4.3%), the fatality rate dropped to 1.19 per 100M VMT, and nearly every category improved: passenger-car occupant deaths down 9.7%, pickup deaths down 4.9%, van deaths down 5.7%, motorcyclist deaths down 2.2%, pedestrian deaths down 3.9%, speeding deaths down 5.2%, alcohol-impaired deaths down 3.9%.

One category went the other way. SUV occupant fatalities rose from 6,901 to 7,013 (+112, +1.6%) — the only occupant class in the entire report with more deaths in 2024 than 2023. And the injuries went the same direction: SUV occupants injured rose 28,952 (+4.2%), to 723,240, while car occupants injured fell 4.6% and pickup occupants injured fell 9.2% (both statistically significant decreases).

SUVs now account for 30.9% of all passenger-vehicle occupant deaths, up from 28.7% a year earlier — a 2.2-point share gain in a single year. Nearly one in five of all 39,254 traffic deaths in 2024 (17.9%) was an SUV occupant.

Context numbers:
- Total deaths 41,025 → 39,254 (-4.3%); rate 1.26 → 1.19 per 100M VMT (-5.6%); VMT 3,247B → 3,294B (+1.5%)
- SUV share of passenger-vehicle occupant deaths: 28.7% (2023) → 30.9% (2024); car share 49.2% → 47.0%
- SUV occupant deaths per day in 2024: ~19.2
- SUV alcohol-impaired drivers in fatal crashes: 2,641 → 2,624 (-17, -0.6%) — barely moved while passenger-car drunk drivers fell 5.0% (4,462 → 4,239). SUVs are the stickiest category for impaired driving too.
- Large-truck alcohol-impaired drivers rose 26% (178 → 224) — a secondary nugget, not the story.

## The novel contribution

First cross-read of the 2024 overview's person-type table isolating the SUV as the sole divergence: deaths up AND injuries up while every other occupant class improved on both measures. Plus the share math nobody published: SUVs went from 28.7% to 30.9% of passenger-vehicle occupant deaths in one year, and the per-day body count (19.2/day) now exceeds the entire van category's annual toll (910) every 47 days. Cross-checked against the site's own FARS 2014–2023 data (fars_output.js class aggregates): SUVs as a class run 5.47 deaths per 100M VMT vs 13.92 for sedans — so this is NOT a story about SUVs being the most dangerous vehicles per mile. It is a story about exposure and direction: the safest-per-mile class is the only one whose body count is still growing.

## Why it might be happening (mechanisms, stated as hypotheses)

1. **Fleet mix / exposure.** SUVs are roughly half of new light-vehicle sales and have been for years. More SUV miles driven means more SUV occupant deaths at constant per-mile risk. NHTSA's overview does not publish VMT by vehicle type, so no 2024 SUV-specific fatality rate can be computed from this report. This is the leading explanation and the honest counterweight.
2. **Size creep within the class.** The "SUV" bucket now spans subcompact crossovers to 6,000-lb three-rows; the class average keeps getting heavier and taller, which changes crash dynamics (rollover, compatibility) in ways the aggregate hides.
3. **Behavioral stickiness.** SUV drunk drivers in fatal crashes fell only 0.6% while car drunk drivers fell 5.0% — the impairment problem is retreating slowest exactly where deaths are rising.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)

This is almost certainly an exposure story, not a safety story. If America drives more SUV miles every year — and it does, because SUVs are half of what dealers sell — then SUV occupant deaths will rise even if every individual SUV gets safer, the same way drowning deaths rise when more people swim. The per-mile evidence points that way: in the site's own 2014–2023 FARS data, SUVs kill at 5.47 per 100M VMT against 13.92 for sedans, and the 2024 report's own car-occupant death plunge (-9.7%) happened while the car fleet shrank. Singling out SUVs for a +112 swing in a census year, when the class is absorbing the entire market's growth, mistakes a denominator for a defect. The honest headline is "Americans keep switching to SUVs faster than SUVs keep getting safer," and even that needs the VMT-by-vehicle-type data NHTSA does not publish.

## Limitations

- 2024 figures come from the Annual Report File (ARF), which NHTSA says is "subject to change when it is finalized the following year." The 2023 figures are Final File.
- No VMT by vehicle type is published in the overview, so an SUV-specific 2024 fatality rate cannot be computed; the exposure explanation cannot be confirmed or ruled out from this report alone.
- Injury figures are CRSS estimates (sample-based), not a census. The SUV injury increase (+4.2%) was not flagged as statistically significant in the report; the car (-4.6%) and pickup (-9.2%) injury decreases were.
- "SUV" is a broad NHTSA light-truck subcategory spanning subcompact crossovers to full-size three-rows; the aggregate hides within-class differences.
- Fatality counts are a census, so the +112 is a real count, not a sampling artifact — but a single-year swing of 112 on a base of 6,901 (1.6%) is small relative to year-to-year noise in other categories.

## Actionable takeaways

- Shopping for a family hauler: do not read this as "SUVs are unsafe." Per-mile, SUVs remain far safer than sedans (5.47 vs 13.92 deaths per 100M VMT in 2014–2023 FARS). The takeaway is narrower: the class is absorbing the market, so its aggregate body count is the one still rising — buy the specific model with the best crash and crash-avoidance ratings (IIHS Top Safety Pick+, good moderate-overlap rear-seat score), not the class average.
- If you already drive an SUV: the two levers that actually move your risk are the same as everywhere — belt use (48% of passenger-vehicle occupants killed in 2024 were unrestrained) and sobriety (SUV drunk drivers barely declined while car drunk drivers fell 5%).
- Check any VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before buying; a 2024 model-year SUV is now a 2-year-old used car, and the 2025–2026 recall cycle is already running.
