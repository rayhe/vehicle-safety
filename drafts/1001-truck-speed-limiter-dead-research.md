# Research: #1001 — Truck Speed Limiter Mandate Killed

**Slug:** `1001-truck-speed-limiter-dead`
**Title:** "The Government Killed the Truck Speed Limiter in the Name of Safety. Its Own Math Said 214 Lives a Year."
**Journalist:** Rex Driverton (paradox beat; deadpan noir; investigation kicker)
**News peg:** Sept 23, 2026 industry roundup re-affirms the July 2025 withdrawal as live policy; July 2026 reporting shows the heavy-truck AEB rule the withdrawal cited as overlap won't be final until 2027-2028 (effective ~2030-2031). Sept 1, 2026 one-year retrospective.
**Queue check:** Zero prior coverage of speed limiters in 1000 articles (grepped queue titles/slugs: no "speed limiter", "governor", "65 mph mandate" hits).

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
After 19 years of study, FMCSA/NHTSA killed the heavy-truck speed limiter mandate in July 2025, arguing slower trucks create dangerous speed differentials and that automatic emergency braking would cover the same crashes. But the agencies' own 2016 analysis projected 63-214 lives saved per year at 65 mph, and the AEB rule they cited as overlap doesn't exist yet and won't take effect until roughly 2030.

## Self-critique gate
- Genuinely surprising after 1000 articles? Yes. Zero prior speed-limiter coverage; the paradox is real (safety agency kills safety rule citing safety); the AEB-gap finding (cited substitute technology is 5+ years from effectiveness) is a novel analytical catch.
- Not just a data dump: original calculations (KE physics, midpoint arithmetic, AEB-gap lives, voluntary-adoption discount).
- Verdict: PROCEED.

## Kill test
If FMCSA's 2012 finding (65-mph limiters reduce crash risk even on 80-mph roads) fails to replicate, and the speed-differential literature dominates, then the withdrawal was correct and the 63-214 range was always modeling fiction. The article's thesis dies if the pro-limiter evidence base collapses. This is stated openly in the piece.

## Timeline (verified)
- 2006: Road Safe America petitions NHTSA/FMCSA for a speed limiter rule. [galfandberger]
- Jan 26, 2007: 72 FR 3904 notice; 3,000+ comments to Docket NHTSA-2007-26851. [NHTSA Draft EA DOT HS 812 324]
- Jan 3, 2011: NHTSA grants petitions for rulemaking. [NHTSA Draft EA]
- March 2012: FMCSA publishes "Research on the Safety Impacts of Speed Limiter Device Installations on CMVs," Phase II (FMCSA-RRR-12-006, MaineWay Services). 150,000+ trucks, 28,000+ crashes (2007-2009). Overall crash rate: 16.4 vs 11 per 100 trucks/year (without vs with limiters). Speed-relevant crashes: 5 vs 1.4 per 100 trucks/year. [Overdrive, CCJ, ExpeditersOnline citing the study]
- Sept 7, 2016: Joint NPRM, 81 FR 61942. Require speed limiters on trucks GVWR >26,000 lbs; comments sought on 60/65/68 mph settings. [Overdrive]
- 2016 benefit analysis (NHTSA Draft EA, Aug 2016), annual: 60 mph: 162-498 lives saved; 65 mph: 63-214 lives + 70-236 serious injuries prevented; 68 mph: 27-96 lives. Net benefits at 65 mph: $1,039-$2,757M (7% discount rate). [NHTSA DOT HS 812 324, Table 1]
- 7,225 comments on the 2016 NPRM. [trucksafe]
- 2017: shelved under first Trump administration as "long-term action" in the regulatory agenda. [Overdrive, autohaulersamerica]
- May 4, 2022: FMCSA advance notice of supplemental rulemaking, 87 FR 26317, revives the mandate. [Overdrive]
- 2022 ANSPRM drew 15,000+ comments (reported as 15,600+, "over 15,000 opposed"). [trucknews, trucksafe, dmlawusa]
- June 27, 2025: Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announces intent to withdraw as part of "pro-trucker" initiatives. [Overdrive]
- July 24, 2025: Withdrawal published, Federal Register 2025-13928, withdrawing both the 2016 NPRM and 2022 ANSPRM. [Overdrive, CCJ, landline]
- Withdrawal rationale (verbatim, via secondary quotes of the notice): "In light of significant policy and safety concerns and continued data gaps that create considerable uncertainty about the estimated costs, benefits, and other impacts of the proposed rule, FMCSA and NHTSA have decided to withdraw the proposal." Also: the 2016 NPRM "lacks a sufficiently clear and compelling safety justification for its implementation and raises significant concerns regarding federalism." AEB/FCW overlap: "rear-end crashes involving heavy vehicles (where the truck is the striking vehicle) could be reduced by crash avoidance technologies designed to mitigate or prevent such crashes, such as automatic emergency braking (AEB) and forward collision warning (FCW)." [Overdrive, mcusercontent regulatory update, thetrucker]
- July 2026: Land Line reports the 2026 regulatory agenda expects a joint supplemental NPRM on heavy-truck AEB "as soon as this month"; "it could be well into 2027 or 2028 before a final rule gets out the door." The 2023 proposal: Class 7/8 comply 3 years after effective date; Class 3-6 in 4 years. NHTSA's AEB false-activation investigation (opened 2023) upgraded to engineering analysis Oct 2025, still open. [landline.media]
- Sept 1, 2026: trucknews one-year retrospective ("One year ago, the FMCSA and NHTSA withdrew their proposed rules..."). DOT retrospective framed the withdrawal as removing a safety hazard "created... by forcing drivers to travel slower than surrounding traffic."
- Sept 23, 2026: getclue regulatory roundup re-affirms "FMCSA formally withdrew its proposal to mandate speed limiters on heavy trucks" as current policy.

## Key data points
- Large-truck crash deaths, 2024: 5,340 (FARS via coxwelllaw analysis, Sept 2026); 4,095 in 2015 (+30% over the decade); IIHS: +58% from 2009 low of 3,380. [coxwelllaw]
- 2018: 9,378 fatalities in crashes where at least one driver was speeding = 26% of total traffic fatalities (NHTSA via NSC). [nsc.org priority statement]
- 2024: speeding a factor in 29% of traffic fatalities (NSC via landline, Aug 2026). [landline.media]
- FMCSA: 10,440 people killed 2004-2013 in large-truck crashes where CMV speed likely contributed to severity. [nsc.org]
- Speed limiters standard equipment on heavy trucks since early 1990s. ATRI 2015/2016 survey: 85% of fleets govern 100% of their trucks; most common setting 65 mph. [nsc.org, ccjdigital]
- 14 states had maximum speed limits of 75+ mph for cars and trucks (IIHS, Oct 2020, via NSC). [nsc.org]
- FMCSA's own conclusion: "even though limiting heavy vehicles to 65 mph may increase the speed differential between these vehicles and the median travel speed on some roads, 65 mph speed limiting devices may actually reduce the risk of heavy vehicles being involved in a crash on roads with posted speed limits of up to 80 mph." [nsc.org quoting FMCSA]
- OOIDA counter-data (fatal large-truck crashes by posted speed limit, 2012-2014): 70-75 mph zones ~17-18% of fatal crashes; 80-85 mph ~0.2-0.3%; average posted limit ~55 mph. "The frequency of interactions with other vehicles by a vehicle traveling 10 mph below the posted speed limit is 227% higher than when moving at traffic speed." [ooida.com Paper 19]
- OOIDA methodological critique: the 2012 study's first draft (2010) said "the research could not definitely attribute the effect to the presence of an active SL"; the second final draft (2012, no new data collected) changed to "strong positive benefits for SLs." Coauthor Steven L. Johnson (Univ. of Arkansas) wrote a white paper on the report's limitations. [ooida.com Paper 10]
- Julie Anna Cirillo (34-year DOT veteran, former FMCSA chief safety officer/acting administrator), 2003 Senate testimony: "Traffic operating at or about the same speed, regardless of speed limit, is the safest traffic environment. Jurisdictions should do whatever they can to encourage this operating scenario and should never require the opposite." [Overdrive]
- Lewie Pugh, OOIDA Executive Vice President, July 2025: "After nearly 20 years, big trucking's proposed speed limiter mandate is dead... it would have made our highways less safe for all motorists." [landline.media]
- NSC position: "Research shows that limiting the speed of a heavy truck improves safety, even if other vehicles are traveling at higher speeds." [nsc.org]

## Original calculations
1. **Kinetic energy (physics, exact):** KE scales with v^2. An 80,000-lb truck at 75 mph vs 65 mph: (75^2 - 65^2)/75^2 = 1400/5625 = **24.9% less kinetic energy** at 65. At 68 mph: (75^2 - 68^2)/75^2 = 1001/5625 = **17.8% less**.
2. **Agencies' midpoint:** (63 + 214)/2 = **138.5 ~= 139 lives/year** at 65 mph. Against 5,340 large-truck deaths in 2024: **2.6%** of large-truck deaths.
3. **AEB gap arithmetic (labeled upper bound):** Withdrawal July 2025. Heavy-truck AEB: supplemental NPRM expected ~2026, final rule 2027-2028 at earliest, Class 7/8 compliance 3 years after effective date => effective ~2030-2031. Gap ~= 5.5 years x 139 lives/year ~= **765 lives** at the agencies' own midpoint, before any discount. Assumptions stated.
4. **Voluntary-adoption discount (labeled rough):** 85% of fleets already govern at ~65 mph (ATRI). The 63-214 range was modeled against a no-limiter baseline, not today's fleet. If marginal benefit scales with the ungoverned share (~15%): 0.15 x 139 ~= **21 lives/year** incremental. Linear-scaling assumption flagged as rough.
5. **Addressable-share check:** Per OOIDA's 2012-2014 table, only ~18% of fatal large-truck crashes occurred in 70-75 mph zones where a 65-mph limiter binds hardest; ~56% occurred in zones posted <=65 mph.

## Strongest counterargument (stated fairly)
OOIDA's case: speed variance kills more reliably than speed itself. A truck governed at 65 in an 80-mph flow is a rolling chicane; interaction frequency jumps 227% for vehicles 10 mph under the posted limit; most fatal truck crashes happen on roads posted at 65 or below, where the limiter changes nothing; 85% of fleets already govern voluntarily, so the mandate's marginal benefit is a sliver of the advertised 63-214; and the foundational 2012 study's headline conclusion ("strong positive benefits") appeared in a second final draft that collected no new data, after the first draft admitted it couldn't attribute the effect to limiters. If the differential literature is right, the withdrawal didn't cost lives, it saved them from a rule that would have manufactured conflict points across the interstate system.

## Limitations (dedicated section in article)
- The 63-214 range is 2016 NPRM modeling against a no-limiter baseline, not observed outcomes; the counterfactual (mandate imposed) is unobservable.
- 2024's 5,340 large-truck deaths occurred under today's voluntary-adoption fleet, so the midpoint can't be read as "lives the withdrawal cost."
- AEB timeline from the 2026 regulatory agenda is a projection; rulemakings slip.
- OOIDA's speed-zone table covers 2012-2014; posted limits have risen since.
- Federal Register withdrawal text verified via verbatim secondary quotes (direct fetch bot-blocked); citation 2025-13928 given.
- The 21/year discount assumes linear scaling with ungoverned share; rough by design.
- Heavy-truck AEB false-activation investigation (engineering analysis since Oct 2025) remains open.

## Actionable guidance
- Fleet operators: the evidence-backed play is unchanged, govern at 65. 85% of fleets already do; the 2012 data says governed trucks crash less.
- Drivers sharing the road: the differential is the real hazard. Don't linger alongside a governed truck; pass decisively or hang back. A 15-20 mph closing differential is where the risk lives.
- Policy watchers: the live rulemaking is now heavy-truck AEB (supplemental NPRM expected; comment period will open). That docket is where this fight moved.
