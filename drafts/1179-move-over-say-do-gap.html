90% of Drivers Say They Move Over. Only 64% Actually Do.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 15, 2026

According to the toxicology reports – and there are a lot of them – the deadliest thing on an American roadside is not the flat tire or the dead battery, but the other drivers. Ohio has logged 730 crashes involving roadside vehicles so far this year, with 93 people dead or seriously injured. Two data drops released today point to the reason: an enormous share of drivers are fibbing about moving over.[1]

26
The point gap between drivers who say they move over (90%+) and drivers observed actually doing it (64%)

A new AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety survey, released this morning ahead of National Slow Down, Move Over Day on Saturday, finds that more than 90 percent of drivers claim they slow down or move over for a vehicle on the shoulder. Previous observational research cited by the same foundation found that only 64 percent of vehicles subject to move-over laws actually changed lanes or reduced speed, leaving thirty-six percent that did neither. One in three drivers sailing past a tire change at highway speed, telling a surveyor they would never do such a thing.[2]

Ohio is where the numbers get ugly. Of the 730 crashes through mid-October, 75 hit ODOT crews and 11 hit state troopers. The trend line snapped: after falling from 2022 through 2024, move-over crashes spiked to 903 in 2025, and this year annualizes near 920.[1] One in eight produced a death or serious injury. For tow operators the occupational math is grotesque: government data puts their on-the-job death rate at 43 per 100,000 workers, fourteen times the all-industry average of 3.[4]

Now the part my desk actually covers. In 2025, AAA responded to 30 million-plus roadside events, and 15 roadside service providers were killed in move-over incidents. Six deaths involved hit-and-run drivers, five involved impaired drivers.[2] One in three of the dead was killed by someone too drunk or high to process flashing lights on a shoulder: partly a compliance problem, partly my usual beat wearing a different hat. Nationally, more than 2,100 people were struck and killed while stranded or working at the roadside between 2019 and 2023, about 420 a year, in a category of crash outlawed in all 50 states the entire time.[2]

The strongest case against this framing: the 90 percent self-report and the 64 percent observation come from different studies at different times, and AAA’s releases do not establish that the samples are cleanly comparable. Compliance may have improved since the observational work, which would shrink the gap, and Ohio’s 2025 spike could partly reflect better crash classification. Framed the way the releases frame it, the gap is real; the exact width carries a methodology asterisk.

What to do with this information is blessedly simple. See flashing lights on the shoulder, move over a full lane. If traffic makes that impossible, slow down well below the limit; several states codify twenty under. Put the phone down for the ten seconds it takes to pass. And if you are the one stranded: pull as far off the shoulder as physics allows, hazards on, and limp to the next exit if you can. Stay belted in the car while you wait for the truck, a luxury the truck driver cannot afford. You can.
