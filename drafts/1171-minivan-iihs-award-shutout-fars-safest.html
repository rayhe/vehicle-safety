The Gap

No Minivan Earned an IIHS Safety Award This Year. The Death Data Says They're the Safest Cars on the Road.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 25, 2027

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds, and then about what happens in the ten years after. Every minivan on sale in America just flunked safety class: not one earned an IIHS Top Safety Pick or Top Safety Pick+ for 2026.[1] Now read the body count. The Chrysler Pacifica kills 0.19 people per 100 million miles, roughly one-sixth the national rate.[2][3] The lab won't give minivans a ribbon. Meanwhile the morgue says they're the safest boxes on wheels. Somebody is measuring the wrong thing.

0.19
Pacifica deaths per 100M miles vs. the 2024 national rate of 1.19, from NHTSA FARS 2014–2023

Start with the report card, because it is genuinely ugly. IIHS made its 2026 criteria the toughest ever written: good in the small overlap front, the updated moderate overlap front, and the side test, plus standard crash prevention that aces the pedestrian exam.[4] The Sienna, best of the breed, managed only marginal in the moderate overlap test. The Odyssey scored poor, with the rear dummy's head and neck taking the hit; Pacifica and Carnival both landed marginal on rear chest protection. Sixty-three vehicles made the 2026 award list. Zero minivans. If you shop by badge, the family hauler is officially unsafe.

Then open the federal death registry and watch the badge logic fall apart. Sienna: 0.49 deaths per 100 million miles. Odyssey: 0.93. Grand Caravan: 1.33, still barely above the 2024 national average of 1.19. And the drivers are the soberest on the road: 15.4 percent of Odyssey fatal-crash drivers showed impairment versus 20 percent across all models, with the Quest and Grand Caravan even lower.[2] These are not lucky numbers. Ten years of FARS data, hundreds of thousands of vehicle-years, and the pattern never wavers: the box built to carry your kids kills fewer people per mile than nearly anything with four wheels.

So what is the test actually grading? The updated moderate overlap exam added a dummy to the back seat, and that single change is what sank the segment. Minivan second rows are flat, wide benches engineered for two things: swallowing child seats with LATCH anchors a toddler could find, and folding into the floor. Belt geometry for a belted adult back there is an afterthought, which means submarining risk and chest loading when the belt does the catching. IIHS is right that this is a real failure mode. It is also grading a passenger who barely exists: the rear dummy is an adult Hybrid III, while actual minivan second rows are occupied overwhelmingly by children in car seats, a restraint system the crash test never evaluates. The lab built a perfect test of a rare event and flunked the vehicle for it.

Credit where due. An unbelted teenager or a small adult riding loose in row two genuinely is worse off in a Sienna than in a TSP+ three-row SUV, and IIHS moving the goalposts is how seatbelts got better in the first place. But an award is a shopping shortcut, and this shortcut now points away from the vehicles with the best survival record in the country. The final exam is ten years of death certificates, not one afternoon with a dummy. On the final exam, the minivan wins.

What to do: do not cross minivans off your list over the missing badge. Every one of these vans carries a 5-star NHTSA overall rating, and the FARS death rates above are the number that actually predicts whether your family comes home.[1] The marginal and poor rear-seat scores do matter for exactly one passenger profile: belted teens and small adults riding in row two without a booster, so keep kids in proper restraints longer than the law requires. Note the 2027 Pacifica's reworked headlights were never tested by IIHS, and check any used van's VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before you buy. Shop the death data, not the trophy case.
