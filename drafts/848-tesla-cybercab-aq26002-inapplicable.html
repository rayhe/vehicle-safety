The Car That's Legal Because the Law Doesn't Apply

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 8, 2026

On September 3, a gold two-seater with no steering wheel, no brake pedal, no accelerator pedal, and no mirrors started charging fares on Austin streets. NHTSA opened Audit Query AQ26002 that same day, into how Tesla certified the thing as road-legal. Five days into paid service, and the feds already want the homework.[1]

45
Cybercabs registered in Texas as of September 4, out of 420 autonomous vehicles on state records

In the United States, no government agency inspects your car before you sell it. Every Camry and F-150 on the road got there on a manufacturer's signed promise that it meets the federal standards. Tesla's promise was just more creative than most. Rather than demonstrate compliance with the standards covering mirrors, accelerator controls, brakes, and steering-column protection, it determined those standards were simply inapplicable to a car built without the parts they regulate.[2] Compliance by irrelevance. The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk.

Consider the queue Tesla skipped: Zoox spent roughly four years in the exemption process, and in July 2026 NHTSA granted the first-ever approval for a steering-wheel-free commercial robotaxi.[3] Tesla declined to wait in line and self-certified instead. Meanwhile, in June, NHTSA proposed deleting the manual brake-pedal requirement for automated vehicles and is rewriting the AV rulebook through roughly eight rulemakings. So the agency is auditing Tesla for driving on a rulebook it is already tearing up. Arrive early to a rule change the regulator has already endorsed, and the door prize is a federal audit.

To be precise about what this is: an audit query is an investigative step, not an enforcement action, and it grounds nothing. Meanwhile, the 45 Texas-registered Cybercabs kept picking up riders while the paperwork flew, and Tesla told NHTSA it plans to expand to roughly a dozen states.[4] If the agency finds non-compliance, it can order a recall after procedural steps, and defying that risks major fines. The process is slow; the cars are fast.

If you hail one, understand the deal you are striking: there is no manual control an occupant can reach, and the federal safety regime never laid a wrench on this vehicle before fares were charged. That is not a loophole Tesla found; it is how the system works for every car on the road. The Cybercab is simply the first vehicle shaped like the promise was missing parts, which it was. NHTSA's investigations are public, so if you want to watch a regulator read Tesla's homework in real time, the ODI docket is where that happens.[4]

In fairness, the defense writes itself. Self-certification is how every vehicle in America gets sold, and the Cybercab may be safer for it: no pedals means no pedal misapplication, a crash category with its own body count. Zoox's four-year exemption slog reads less like diligence and more like the process failing. And punishing Tesla for arriving early to a rule change NHTSA already proposed is enforcement theater. None of this is settled by the data yet. It can't.

What the data does not prove: there is no crash data for the Cybercab, because at writing there were about 45 of them with roughly five days of paid service. Every safety claim in either direction is speculation. The FMVSS-by-FMVSS mapping above is press analysis, not NHTSA's filing; the audit document does not name which standards Tesla deemed inapplicable. And Tesla's internal certification rationale remains sealed inside Tesla. This article knows what the audit is asking, not what the answer will be.
