IIHS Finally Graded the Trucks Most Likely to Flatten You. Almost Everyone Failed.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 9, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. IIHS spent decades grading how well cars avoid hitting each other, then never once applied those grades to the vehicles that do the most damage when they hit things. Yesterday that changed, when eleven cargo vans and Class 3 pickups went through the Institute's first commercial crash-prevention ratings, and the results read like a confession: out of eleven vehicles, exactly one earned a Good in vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention.[1]

The motorcycle was the thing nobody could see. Across the test fleet, the motorcycle target proved hardest: several systems issued no warning and touched no brakes in most of the motorcycle trials. Ford's F-350 avoided the passenger-car target at 31 mph, then at 43 mph failed to reduce its speed at all: no warning, no braking, several tons arriving on schedule.[2] Other systems slowed enough to avoid or soften the crash and still got docked for never telling the driver in time, the machine quietly doing the job while the human sat uninformed.

And then there is the bottom of the table, where engineering ambition goes to die. The Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500 earned Poor in both tests, for the simple reason that they do not have automatic emergency braking. Optional forward-collision warning is all you get.[1] You can buy these vans new today, with 2026 VINs, carrying the crash-prevention philosophy of the late twentieth century. Meanwhile the federal rule that would force the industry's hand, NHTSA's 2023 proposal to require AEB on vehicles over 10,000 pounds, the rule the agency says would prevent 19,118 crashes and save 155 lives a year, remains unfinished.[3] IIHS graded the trucks on a rubric that is still voluntary, so the grades are a suggestion and the mass is mandatory.

Physics gets the last word, and it is not a defense so much as a verdict. A loaded 14,000-pound pickup stopping from 43 mph in the distance of a 3,500-pound sedan is a hard problem, and IIHS applied the passenger-car standard unchanged. The F-350 did avoid the target at 31 mph; Poor is a grade against a yardstick built for lighter vehicles.[2] These ratings cover only the 2025-2027 configurations IIHS tested, and 6,215 people killed in crashes involving medium- and heavy-duty vehicles in 2024 counts vehicles involved in crashes, not vehicles that caused them.[1] FARS captures only fatal crashes, a sliver of the 6.7 million annual collisions, and the 19,118-crash figure is a modeled estimate for a rule that has not been finalized.

What you should do: If you spec fleet vehicles, the scorecard is public now and the Sprinter 2500 is the only Good in vehicle-to-vehicle while the BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 lead on pedestrian avoidance; buy accordingly.[1] If you ride a motorcycle, assume the delivery van behind you cannot reliably see you, because IIHS has video of exactly that. And if someone tries to sell you a new Express or Savana, know you are buying a van whose crash prevention would embarrass a 1999 auto show.
