A Supplier Skipped the Furnace on 617 Toyota and Lexus SUVs. Now a Pothole Could Snap the Steering.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 14, 2026

Aluminum forgings do not get strong on their own. After shaping, a steering knuckle has to sit in an aging furnace so its internal crystal structure hardens into something you would trust with a 5,000-pound SUV hitting a pothole at 40 mph. One batch from supplier Bharat Forge Aluminum USA never made the trip. On September 11, Toyota filed NHTSA campaign 26V598000 covering 617 brand-new 2026 Toyota and Lexus SUVs whose front steering knuckles skipped that step, and Toyota's own testing says the weakened castings can fracture at the strut or tie-rod attachment points on a hard impact, with loss of steering control as the worst case.[1][2]

617
Vehicles recalled over one supplier's eight-day production window, traced to a single batch that skipped the aging furnace

The containment is the story. Every affected vehicle was built between June 18 and June 26, 2026: 196 Lexus RX 350s, 127 Toyota Grand Highlander Hybrids, 126 Grand Highlanders, 83 Lexus RX 350hs, 61 Lexus TX 350s, 23 Lexus TX 500hs, and a single RX 500h.[3] That is an eight-day window with exact per-model build dates, which means Toyota's engineers walked the bad batch back through the shipping records and identified every VIN that could have received one of these knuckles. Eight days of production, 617 SUVs, and zero ambiguity about which ones. That is about as tight as a recall population ever gets.

And the defect was found by the people who caused it. Bharat Forge caught the anomaly in a routine quality audit in late June 2026, then called Toyota after the parts had already shipped.[4] Toyota investigated, confirmed the weakness with computer simulation and structural stress testing, and issued the voluntary recall roughly two and a half months later. As of September 9, there were no U.S. warranty claims and no Field Technical Reports, which is a polite way of saying nobody's steering has broken yet. Dealers will inspect and replace the knuckles free, with owner letters going out November 2 through 16.[3]

Credit where it is due: this is the system working. A supplier's audit flagged a process escape, the automaker verified it instead of burying it, and the recall landed before a single fracture occurred. Compare that with the usual cadence, where campaigns arrive after the injury reports pile up, and this one looks like a near-miss in the best possible sense. Nobody died. Nothing exploded. Paperwork moved faster than the failures.

But do not miss the other half of it. A steering knuckle is a forged-aluminum casting that connects your suspension to your steering. It is not a trim piece. An aging furnace is not optional equipment on the line, it is the step that turns shaped metal into a structural component, and one batch walked past it without anyone's interlock or checklist catching the skip until a routine audit weeks later. The audit caught this batch. But the recall records cannot tell you whether an unaudited batch ever walked past the same door. Toyota's 24th recall of 2026 is the success story, which should tell you something about the other 23.

What to do: if you own a 2026 Grand Highlander, Grand Highlander Hybrid, Lexus RX, or Lexus TX, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now rather than waiting for the November letters, because Toyota itself cannot estimate what share of the recalled vehicles actually carry a bad knuckle, which means the recall deliberately over-covers and your truck could be on it.[2] The fix is free. And until it is done, treat any hard pothole or curb strike as a stop-and-inspect event: the defect's trigger scenario is exactly the road hazard your city refuses to fix.

Limitations

The fracture behavior is established by computer simulation and structural stress testing, not by any real-world failure. No crash, injury, or actual broken knuckle is on record. Toyota states it cannot estimate the percentage of recalled vehicles that actually contain a defective knuckle, so the 617-vehicle population is a batch-window containment, not a confirmed-defect count, and some owners will get letters for vehicles with perfectly good parts. The furnace-skip mechanism as described comes from secondary reporting of the Part 573 filing, which was not independently verified live. FARS cannot speak to this story: there are no injuries or deaths. The broader claim about unaudited batches is an inference, not evidence of another escape.
