## 1. General Editor — 8.5/10

Lede lands in three sentences: the compliance, the secrecy, the one-sentence letter. Structure is clean: filing, docket inventory, timeline, the questions asked, the arithmetic, the counterargument, the limitations, the actions. The docket inventory is the article's spine and it is stated twice, once as "two of three filings are empty" in the inventory paragraph and once as "Two of three filings are empty. Zero pages of the safety case are public" at the paragraph's end, which is once too often. The 57-day figure also appears in both the lede and the arithmetic paragraph. Docked half a point for the repeated inventory and the repeated calendar figure, and half a point for length: the piece earns its words, but barely, and the $139,000-per-car division is presented as a price NHTSA "put on a complete answer" when it is really an illustrative division of a maximum penalty by a population estimate.

## 2. Voice Coach — 8.5/10

Unmistakably Rex Driverton: "Rewind the tape, because the deadline is the tell," "Count what the public file actually holds, because the inventory is the story," "Tesla skipped the queue entirely, certified itself, and when the auditor came calling, handed over the homework with a cover sheet that says nobody may read it." No other byline on this site would write that lede. Banned phrases zero. Rhythm gate passes clean. Two tells remain: "Here is the honest version of the claim" is the same scaffold #1426 used the same week, and "What you can actually do with this starts..." is the site's formulaic closer, used across recent pieces. Noted, not blocking.

## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0/10

The piece advocates for transparency without caricaturing the regulator or the manufacturer. NHTSA's Special Order is described as a real enforcement tool, not a stunt. The counterargument is given at full strength, including the routine nature of Part 512 claims and the explicit statement that NHTSA has not found the Cybercab noncompliant. No victim is named, no crash is described, no rider is used as a prop. The share-drop causation is handled honestly, as coincidence rather than cause. The "do not put down a deposit" advice is proportionate to the facts. Clean.

## 4. Social / Shareability — 8.5/10

Pull stat "0" is the shareable number, and it only works because the label does the explaining, which is exactly what a pull stat is for. The headline is a complete argument that survives being pasted without context. The one-sentence letter ("The entirety of this file is confidential business information") is the detail people will quote in replies. Weakness: no single pull-quote sentence under 15 words besides the pull stat itself; the lede's second sentence runs long. The Part 512 decision is an open hook, but there is no date on it, so the sharing window is indefinite rather than urgent. Good, not great.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10

FMVSS 135, Part 512, Part 555, AQ26002, the $139 million maximum, the 21 questions, and the extension timeline (Sept 18 request, Sept 29 memo, Oct 30 deadline) all check against the cited sources. The Zoox Part 555 contrast is accurate: public exemption petition, approved July 2026, 2,500-vehicle annual cap. One caution, handled properly in the text: the article says false sworn answers "can bring criminal charges," which matches the research file's "a fine or up to 15 years in prison" without overstating the specific penalty. The "about 1,000 vehicles" population is correctly attributed to NHTSA's estimate, and the 45-registered figure from Texas records is kept in the limitations, not the body. Docked half a point only because the core fact, the letter's wording, rests on Electrek's reporting rather than a directly read docket PDF, which the article discloses.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.5/10

Original contribution is real and checkable: the docket inventory (three documents, two empty, one page vs. 30 pages, zero pages of safety case public) plus the Zoox-process contrast, which no prior site piece drew. Every number traces to a cited source: Electrek, MLQ, news18a, the NHTSA resume, Reuters. Limitations are explicit and honest, including the Electrek reliance, the unruled confidentiality claim, and the share-move caveat. Docked half a point for the 58-day arithmetic error, caught pre-panel and fixed, and half a point for the $139,000-per-car figure, which is an illustrative division presented without an "illustrative" caveat. The pre-panel catch is the system working; the score reflects that the error reached the draft at all.

## 7. Data Presentation — 8.5/10

Numbers are rendered as prose with the math shown: $139M / 1,000 = $139,000 per car, 57 days, 2 of 3 filings empty, 1 page vs. 30 pages. No invented precision; "about 1,000" is NHTSA's own estimate, and the article keeps it hedged. The pull stat carries the single most important number. Weakness: the article would be stronger with a small table (document / pages / content) rather than burying the docket inventory in paragraph prose; a reader skimming will miss that the only readable document is the firefighter guide. The FARS cross-tab opportunity is unused, but the article's data is NHTSA/press primary data, which is the right call for a regulatory story. Good.
