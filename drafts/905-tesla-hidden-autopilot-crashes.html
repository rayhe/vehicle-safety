Tesla's Data Says the Robot Was Driving. The Police Report Says Nothing.

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
September 15, 2026

On the evening of December 7, 2025, a 2021 Tesla Model Y left Alabama Highway 157 near the Cullman County line, struck two trees, and caught fire. Kayleigh Page, 29, died at the scene. Her 7-month-old daughter survived because a stranger who witnessed the crash pulled the baby from the burning car. State troopers worked it as a routine single-vehicle wreck.[2] Tesla's own filing with the federal government tells a different story: the car's driver-assist system was verified engaged at 55 mph when it left the road.[2] The police report never mentions Autopilot, Full Self-Driving, or any automation at all.

8 fatal crashes, 0 mentions of automation
Fatal Tesla crashes where Electrek matched the company's redacted federal filings to the public record. Tesla's data shows driver-assist engaged in every one; the police reports mention it in none.

Electrek's running investigation, launched September 1, works like a detective matching two sets of books. Federal rules require Tesla to report crashes involving Autopilot and Full Self-Driving to NHTSA under the agency's Standing General Order. Tesla complies, then redacts almost everything, arguing the data would cause it "financial harm" if made public.[3] Electrek takes those redacted filings and matches them, one by one, against fatal crashes that were covered in the news as ordinary accidents. Two columns: everything Tesla told the government, and everything the public was told.[1]

The confirmed list reads like a police blotter with a missing column. In Clute, Texas, 23-year-old Steven Alvarez died when his Model 3 left the road and hit a park pool. Police called it a possible medical episode; Tesla's data shows driver-assist verified engaged at 104 mph, a speed that appears nowhere in the public record.[1] In Lake Mary, Florida, a 43-year-old man died when his Model 3 sat stopped in a live lane of I-4 and a semi rear-ended it. Troopers said they could not explain why the car was stopped; Tesla's filing says the system was engaged at 0 mph.[1] Weeks later in Mesa, Arizona, a near-identical case ended the same way: a 2020 Model 3 stopped at 0 mph on Loop 202 in the early morning, rear-ended, driver killed, and Tesla holds the video.[1]

In Batavia, Illinois, 37-year-old Maggie Espinosa, a teacher and mother of five, died as a passenger when a Model Y turning left struck a pickup. Tesla's data shows driver-assist engaged mid-turn, a maneuver that points to Full Self-Driving, and the case is now under review by the Kane County State's Attorney's Office.[1] In Moraine, Ohio, 55-year-old motorcyclist Leonidas Davis was killed at an intersection, and every outlet called the car "a sedan." It was a Model X with driver-assist verified engaged. Tesla holds the EDR, the telematics, the video, and the police report.[1] In Buena Vista Township, New Jersey, a Model 3 ran a stop sign and killed 82-year-old Stephen Field as he turned left in his Honda Civic. Police reported only that the Tesla "failed to stop"; Tesla logged driver-assist engaged at 4 mph, a rolling-stop signature that points to Full Self-Driving.[1] In Pattonsburg, Missouri, a 2024 Model Y left I-35 and struck a guardrail, killing its 64-year-old driver. Troopers said she "veered off"; Tesla's data says the system was engaged at 73 mph.[1]

None of this proves the automation caused these crashes. That is the wrong question, and it is the one Tesla wants everyone to ask, because it is unanswerable without the data Tesla holds. The right question is what happens to the official record. Police crash reports feed FARS, feed state databases, and feed the investigations that become recalls, regulations, and court verdicts. When automation involvement is systematically missing from the record, every downstream number is built on a gap. The engineers designing the next recall and the jury hearing the next wrongful-death case are all working from paperwork that never learned the robot was there.

Tesla has fought to keep it that way. The company leans on NHTSA's confidential-business-information process to keep its crash filings redacted. In August 2025, NHTSA opened an Audit Query into whether Tesla was even reporting its crashes on time, after the company submitted a batch of late reports and blamed what it called a data collection error.[3] Meanwhile the federal Full Self-Driving investigation that began in October 2024, upgraded to an engineering analysis in March 2026, is still open: one of six Tesla-related probes currently on NHTSA's books.[4] Electrek is now filing public-records requests with the investigating agencies, asking the question nobody else has: did the police get Tesla's raw telemetry, or only Tesla's interpretation of it?[1]

Eight people are dead, and in every one of these crashes the public was told a story with no robot in it. Tesla's own paperwork says otherwise. The discrepancy is not a conspiracy; it is a filing system, filled out by troopers who have no way to know whether a driver-assist system was engaged. Until the record knows what the car knew, every fatal Tesla crash carries an asterisk nobody can see. Wheels down.
