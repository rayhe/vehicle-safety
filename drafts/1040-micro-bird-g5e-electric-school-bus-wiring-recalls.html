278 Electric School Buses Have a Wire Chafing Against a Metal Bracket. Five Electrical Recalls in Nine Months.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 1, 2026

In September, NHTSA published two recalls for the Micro Bird G5e electric school bus. A wiring harness inside the bus was routed so that a metal bracket can rub against it, wear through the insulation, and kill drive power. The filings cover 278 buses combined, and the repair is a free inspection.[1][2] If you route wiring for a living, that sentence should make you wince.

5
Electrical recall campaigns on the Micro Bird G5e platform in nine months: two chafing-harness filings, one dead power steering pump, two melting A/C power cables.

The September pair tells its own little story. NHTSA campaign 26V594 covers 197 buses, filed by Corp. Micro Bird, Inc.[1] Campaign 26V591 covers 81 buses, filed by Micro Bird USA LLC.[2] Same defect, same week, same fix, two corporate names for what the paperwork suggests is one Quebec company filing through both legal identities.[3] Letters go out November 10. Nobody has connected the dots on the docket.

Then there is the pattern, and the pattern is the story. In January 2026, the same G5e platform was recalled because its power steering pump can fail to start or quit without warning: 486 buses.[5] That same month came two more: 2,428 school buses and 513 transit buses, recalled because a loose air-conditioning power cable can overheat its control board and start a fire.[6] Add it up and you get five electrical recall campaigns in nine months touching 3,705 buses, all in one subsystem family, on one platform, carrying children.

Let us talk about wire routing, because this is where I get judgmental. A harness that chafes against a bracket is a failure you catch in a design review with a flashlight and a mockup, not in the field after delivery. Strain relief, grommets, standoff clips: these are the oldest lessons in automotive electrical engineering, older than the seatbelt. Power steering pumps that quit and power cables that melt complete the picture. This is not bad luck; it is an electrical architecture that keeps failing its own inspections.

Timing could not be worse: on September 24, the EPA released fresh numbers on its $5 billion Clean School Bus Program: of roughly 2,600 electric school buses ordered, about 1,100 have been delivered, 854 are behind schedule, and 140 will never arrive, as districts pull out over reliability.[7] So here is what to do. If your district runs 2025–2027 G5e buses, check the VINs at nhtsa.gov/recalls today rather than waiting for the November 10 letter, and call Micro Bird at 819-477-2012.[1] Parents: ask your district whether the recall work is done, because looking at the harness takes minutes.

In fairness: zero crashes or injuries are reported in the September filings, there is no do-not-drive order, and the two September filings may count some of the same buses twice.[4] Recalls are the safety system working as designed, and electric buses still displace diesel exhaust that harms kids’ lungs. But a school bus that loses drive power in traffic is a special kind of failure, because the cargo cannot drive itself home.
