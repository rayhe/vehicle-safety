# Research: Connected Vehicle Ban — Zero Deaths, Preemptive Action

## Angle
The Senate unanimously passed the Connected Vehicle Security Act out of committee (Jul 22, 2026) — banning connected vehicles linked to China, Russia, North Korea, or Iran. Zero Americans have died from vehicle cyberattacks. Meanwhile, NHTSA has delayed for decades on proven mechanical threats that kill 36,000+ annually. The contrast: Congress acts preemptively on hypothetical cyber risk faster than NHTSA acts on demonstrated crashworthiness failures.

## Primary Sources

### 1. Reuters — Senate Panel Approves Bill (Jul 22, 2026)
- Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026
- Co-sponsors: Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-OH) + Sen. Elissa Slotkin (D-MI)
- Passed Senate Commerce Committee unanimously
- After Jan 1, 2027: prohibits import/sale of connected vehicles made/designed in China/NK/Russia/Iran or by manufacturer with >15% ownership from those countries

- GM, Ford, Stellantis, UAW all support

### 3. NHTSA Delay History (Established Record)

- FMVSS No. 135 (door side-impact): decade-long rulemaking
- ISA (Intelligent Speed Assistance): not required until 2027 IIHS criteria (voluntary)

## Original Contribution
No other coverage frames this as a safety-regulatory paradox. Every outlet covers it as trade policy or national security. The Crash Report asks: why does hypothetical cyber risk get unanimous preemptive action when proven mechanical risk gets decade-long delays?

## Counterargument
Cybersecurity threats are structural — one exploit could theoretically affect millions of vehicles simultaneously (fleet-scale attack). Traditional crash defects kill one at a time. The preemptive posture is arguably proportional to the scale of potential harm.

## Limitations
- Zero confirmed US vehicle cyberattack deaths — the threat is theoretical
- Bill may not pass full Senate without Mercedes carve-out
- NHTSA delay comparisons are to different types of regulatory action (rulemaking vs. legislation)
