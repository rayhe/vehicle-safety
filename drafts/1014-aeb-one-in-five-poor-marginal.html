Your Car Has Automatic Braking. One in Five Systems Can't See a Pedestrian at Night.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 29, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. Your car's window sticker says automatic emergency braking. So do the stickers on 524 vehicles IIHS has rated for front crash prevention. 117 of them, more than one in five, score Poor or Marginal at the one job the system exists to do: stop before it hits something.[1] The feature is on the sticker. The capability is not guaranteed.

Start with the part that should make you look up your own car. Jeep fails 61% of the time: 14 of 23 rated vehicles. Ford fails half its lineup, 17 of 34. Chevrolet fails half, 14 of 28. Then the uncomfortable one for badge shoppers: Audi, 13 of 37.[1] Price predicts nothing here.

What predicts the rating is hardware generation. Camera-only systems go blind at night; camera-plus-radar systems hold up. Two model years of the same car can sit farther apart than two brands, because one got the new sensor suite and the other did not.[1] The trend is moving: the failure share fell from 26% of rated 2024 models to 19% in 2026. Progress, paid for in sensors and software.[1]

The test earned those failures on purpose. Old version: aim the car at another car in daylight at low speed. Everything passed, which told shoppers nothing. Current version: add a pedestrian, add darkness, add speed.[1] September's batch shows what that means in practice: IIHS tested seven new models and only two, the Kia Telluride and the Tesla Model Y, earned Top Safety Pick+. In that same batch, the GMC Canyon managed a Marginal in the updated moderate overlap test and a Poor in vehicle-to-vehicle crash prevention.[6] Harder questions, different answers.

Now the part the industry would rather you not connect. Washington has ordered exactly this capability into law. FMVSS 127 requires AEB and pedestrian AEB as standard equipment by September 2029, with pedestrian detection in daylight and at night and crash avoidance at up to 62 mph.[5] The automakers' trade group sued in January 2025, calling those speed requirements "practically impossible with available technology," and the case has sat in abeyance while regulators float a two-year delay.[4] Meanwhile the IIHS test is the rehearsal for that rule, and 78% of systems already pass it. Lobbyists say impossible. Test sheets say most of the industry already did the hard part.

Steel-man the industry's case, because it is not empty. IIHS's test is deliberately harder than the legal standard, so a Poor rating does not mean the system is decorative. It still brakes for a stationary car in daylight. Aggressive braking carries a genuine cost: phantom braking can cause the crash it was built to prevent. And camera-only physics at night is a real constraint, which means the mandate forces pricier hardware into every car, raising prices for the buyers who can least afford them. All true, and all worth weighing against the alternative.

What this data does not prove matters too. Figures come from a third-party tally of IIHS ratings, not an independent recount, and the ratings universe mixes model years and test generations. A front crash prevention rating grades one scenario family, not the whole car. Treat it as a measurement, not a verdict.

Three things. First, ignore the feature list and look up the front crash prevention rating for the exact model year, because a 2024 and a 2026 of the same nameplate can be different systems.[2] Second, weight hardware over badge: camera-plus-radar beats a prestige logo every time. Third, if your car sits in the Poor or Marginal column, treat its AEB as a daylight backstop, not a guardian. A pedestrian at night is precisely the test it failed.
