He Blew Past a Stopped School Bus and Missed a Child by Inches. Cameras in One Florida County Caught 27,000 More.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 12, 2026

The video is seven seconds long. A school bus sits on Douglas Road in Bedford Township, Michigan, red lights flashing, stop arm extended, an elementary student stepping off and starting across the street. A blue Malibu comes the other way and never slows down. It misses the child by inches, close enough that you can see the kid flinch. That was Wednesday afternoon. By Thursday night the clip was on ABC World News.[1]

27,000
Illegal passes of stopped school buses recorded by cameras in one Florida county over seven months

The Malibu's driver, a 19-year-old from Toledo, was tracked down with help from AI license-plate readers, and Monroe County is seeking reckless driving charges against him.[2] He is one data point. Here is the dataset: in Lee County, Florida, stop-arm cameras recorded 27,000 illegal passes in seven months.[4]

Do the division, because nobody else will. Twenty-seven thousand over seven months is roughly 130 a day, every day, or about 180 per school day. In one county. A driver blowing past a stopped school bus, reds flashing, children in the road, roughly every eight minutes of the school day. And those are only the passes a camera happened to be watching.

Now the part that should make you furious, then hopeful, in that order. The School District of Lee County says more than 90 percent of drivers cited for a first offense never do it again.[4] The punishment works beautifully. The coverage is the disgrace: most buses carry no camera, so most violators are never cited at all. We built an excellent witness and pointed it at a fraction of the crime.

Physics does not negotiate with any of this. At 35 mph a car covers 51 feet every single second, while a school bus stretches about 40 feet long, which means a child crossing in front of the bus is inside the kill zone for less than a second of a distracted driver's attention, and stopping from 35 takes well over 130 feet. The stop arm is a piece of reflective metal asking two tons of steel to respect arithmetic. Sometimes, as the Michigan video shows, the steel declines.

States are finally treating this as the enforcement problem it is. Kentucky's 2026 law lets districts mount cameras on buses: $300 for the first civil ticket, $500 after that, billed to the registered owner, no points on anyone's license.[5] Florida's camera tickets run $225, civil, no points, which sounds soft until you see what an officer can write instead: $200 and four points, $400 plus a mandatory court hearing for passing on the door side, suspension on the second conviction.[4] The camera is the polite version of enforcement. The polite version deters 90 percent of first-timers.

Now the honest part, because the numbers deserve it. Violations are not deaths. Buses remain the safest vehicles on the road, the overwhelming majority of drivers do stop, and the column trumpeting 27,000 violations publishes no injury count, because the carnage the number implies mostly never happens. The cameras are also surveillance: the Michigan driver was found by Flock license-plate readers, the same systems cities fight over on privacy grounds, and civil tickets bill the car's owner rather than whoever was driving.[1] Kentucky's law is months old with no outcome data yet. All true. Also true: every one of those 27,000 passes was a lottery ticket, and the kid on Douglas Road watched his number almost come up. When the yellows flash, get ready to stop. When the reds flash, stop, both directions, in every state, and do not roll until the lights die and the arm folds. Parents, ask your district whether its buses carry cameras. Ask the data and it answers plainly: the camera is the thing that makes the law real.
