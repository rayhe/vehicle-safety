Six in Ten Deaths Are in Cars Built Before the Stability-Control Mandate

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 19, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds after your tires stop agreeing with your steering wheel. A yaw sensor catches the disagreement, the computer brakes a single corner of the car, and the slide you were about to have becomes a lane you stay in, which is electronic stability control doing its one job. I ran a decade of NHTSA fatality data against the federal mandate that made it universal, and the result is stark: 62.3 percent of occupant deaths between 2014 and 2023 happened in vehicles built before the mandate existed.[1]

FMVSS 126 is the mandate, finalized in April 2007.[2] NHTSA phased it in over three production years starting September 2008, requiring 55 percent of each manufacturer's output, then 75, then 95, and from September 1, 2011, every light vehicle off the line. So every 2012-or-newer vehicle sold in America has stability control by law. IIHS measured what the mandate bought: fatal single-vehicle crashes down 56 percent, fatal multi-vehicle crashes down 32 percent.[3] NHTSA's own update found 55 percent fewer fatal single-vehicle car crashes, 50 percent fewer for light trucks, and 56 percent of fatal car rollovers and 74 percent of fatal light-truck rollovers preventable by the technology.[4]

Why does the pre-mandate fleet still dominate the body count? Because Americans have stopped buying new cars, and S&P Global Mobility's 2025 fleet analysis puts the average age of U.S. light vehicles at a record 12.8 years, with passenger cars averaging 14.5, which means the typical sedan on the road is roughly a 2011, built squarely inside the phase-in window.[5] In the crash data, the median death car is a 2006. Ranked by body count, the decade's five deadliest model years are 2005, 2004, 2003, 2006, and 2007, in that order. Fifteen years after the mandate, the mid-2000s fleet is still doing most of the dying.

Pickups are the extreme case, with three-quarters of pickup occupant deaths, 75.5 percent, in pre-mandate trucks. Work trucks get kept the longest and driven the hardest, and a 2005 Ranger with 200,000 miles is still somebody's daily driver. Among nameplates with at least 800 deaths, the Pontiac Grand Prix, Chevrolet S-10, Chevrolet Cavalier, Buick LeSabre, and Buick Century have 100 percent of their deaths in the pre-mandate cohort. They are zombie fleets: dead nameplates that can never receive a safety update, because they stopped being built.

What this does not prove, stated plainly, is that pre-mandate means no ESC. It was standard on 40 percent of 2006 models and optional on 15 percent more, so a large share of the cohort already had it.[3] I also cannot divide these deaths by miles driven, because nobody tracks mileage by model year, and older vehicles are driven less and by different drivers. Read 62.3 percent as a share of deaths, not a per-mile risk ratio. Finally, the mid-2000s were enormous sales years, which puts more of those cars on the road in the first place.

Strongest objection, at full strength: this piece is mostly a census. New-car sales peaked in the mid-2000s, so mid-2000s cars dominate everything, including crash involvement, and old cars are cheap cars, which selects younger, poorer, riskier drivers into exactly the cohort being measured. Strip out demographics and exposure, and the residual attributable to missing ESC is unknowable from this cross-tab. Fair. But the technology effect is not speculative: the IIHS and NHTSA numbers come from same-model, same-year comparisons, and they are enormous. Framing this as fleet age is the honest container for those numbers.

What to do with this: if you are buying used, treat model year 2012 as a hard floor for safety technology, not just a proxy for age. Verify the car actually has ESC: watch for the sliding-car icon at startup, the ESC-off button on the dash, or badging like StabiliTrak, AdvanceTrac, or VSC. If you already own a pre-2012 vehicle, keep the tires fresh and the speed down, because you are driving without the system that prevents the crash type most likely to kill you alone. And run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, since older fleets accumulate unrepaired recalls.
