The Chevy Astro Out-Drinks the Corvette

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 9, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, the most dangerously impaired vehicle in America is a box: the Chevrolet Astro van, last built when George W. Bush was in his second year, posts a 27.0% impairment rate among tested drivers in fatal crashes[1]. For comparison, the Corvette comes in at a mere 26.2%.

27.0%
of tested Astro drivers in fatal crashes were alcohol- or drug-impaired, 4th highest of any vehicle in FARS

Seventy-six of 281 tested Astro drivers had a BAC above zero or drugs in their system, which puts the humble work van fourth in the entire FARS toxicology ranking, behind only the Buick Park Avenue (31.7%), the old Chevrolet C/K pickup (28.0%), and the Audi A3 (27.1%)[1]. Against a fleet-wide average of 20.0% across nearly half a million tested drivers, the Astro runs a full 35% drunker than average.

Within its own class, the humiliation is worse. Closest van-class rival is the Ford Windstar at 23.1%; Chevy's own full-size Express manages just 19.0% across 1,778 tested drivers. Two vans from one brand, eight points apart: the Astro isn't just the drunkiest van in the dataset, it's a different species of van.

Here is the honest math: that 0.8-point edge over the Corvette is statistical noise, because 281 tested Astro drivers stand against 1,147 for the Corvette, a much tighter estimate. Call it a dead heat, then ask why the pairing feels scandalous: one is America's sports car icon and the other is a $3,000 work van nobody has profiled since 1998.

Which brings us to the camouflage hypothesis. DUI enforcement targets the obvious: loud cars, fast cars, cars with spoilers. Here is the anti-profile vehicle, a boxy gray rectangle with a 4.3-liter V6 and three rows of seats, last rolling off the Baltimore line on May 13, 2005[3]. Nobody watches the rectangle, which makes invisibility excellent cover.

Now the strongest case against my own thesis: the Astro did not make anyone drink. A 21-year-old work van costs a fraction of a Corvette, so its drivers skew toward work crews, rural miles, and late-night shifts, populations with higher baseline impairment no matter what they drive[2]. None of this is the van's fault; the people behind the wheel are the story.

NHTSA counted 12,429 alcohol-impaired fatalities in 2023, one every 42 minutes, and none of those reports blame a body-on-frame chassis[2]. Still, the data says what it says: the car you were warned about in driver's ed is the box with the sliding door.

What to do with this

Buying a cheap work van? The Astro's profile is a data point, not a prophecy, but budget for a dash cam and an honest accounting of how the vehicle will actually be used. Insurers price in exactly this kind of demographic signal, so expect the quote to reflect it. Shopping used vans more broadly means running the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls: toxicology says nothing about crashworthiness, only about who tends to be at the wheel. And if you size up DUI risk by vehicle, retire the sports-car stereotype. It was never about the car.

What this didn't prove

FARS toxicology covers fatal crashes only, so the impairment rate among all Astro drivers, alive and dead, is certainly lower than 27%. Nothing here controls for driver age, rural versus urban miles, vehicle age, or state-by-state testing differences. With 281 tested Astro drivers against the Corvette's 1,147, the ranking is a statistical tie at best. And the last Astro left the factory in 2005, so this is a historical profile of a dying fleet, not a warning about a product you can buy new.
