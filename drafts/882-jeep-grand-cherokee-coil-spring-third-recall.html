Jeep Has Recalled the Same Coil Spring Three Times. The Fix Is Still Coming.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 11, 2026

Before you sign that service loaner receipt and drive home feeling responsible, check whether your Grand Cherokee is on the list. Jeep just recalled 328,381 Grand Cherokees because the rear coil springs can detach from the vehicle while it is moving. Yes, the suspension can literally shed a spring at speed. And yes, this is the third time Jeep has recalled this exact defect.[1][2]

3
recall campaigns for the same rear coil spring since June 2023

The timeline reads like a warranty department's fever dream. June 2023: recall 23V413, more than 345,000 SUVs, springs installed wrong at the factory. January 2026: recall 26V051, 80,620 SUVs, because some of those repairs were done wrong, leaving springs seated incorrectly or the fix half-finished. September 2026: recall 26V562, 328,381 SUVs, because the problem outlived both of those campaigns. Run the division and it gets ugly: nearly a quarter of the vehicles from the first recall got pulled back in for the second. One in four "fixed" Jeeps wasn't.[3][4]

Now the math, because the numbers here are unusually honest. Stellantis estimates just 0.1% of the recalled population actually has a mis-seated spring. That is about 328 vehicles out of 328,381. Compare that to the company's own complaint log: 406 warranty claims plus 29 customer assistance records as of August 6, which works out to 0.12% of the population. The observed rate and the estimated rate agree. When a carmaker's lowball estimate survives an independent arithmetic check, credit where due. Nobody has reported an injury in 39 months.[2][5]

Fine. Now the part that should annoy you anyway. Owners get interim notification letters starting September 29, but those letters warn about a defect whose final remedy is not ready yet. You receive a letter that says your spring might fall off, and the letter essentially says: we will write again when we have the parts. If your SUV was already inspected under one of the two earlier recalls, you go back. The filing is explicit: previously repaired vehicles need the new remedy completed.[5] Somewhere there is a Jeep owner who visited the dealer twice for this spring and is about to visit a third time. That person is owed an apology, not a form letter.

Is there a defense of Jeep here? A real one. Three voluntary campaigns for a 0.1% defect with zero injuries is the recall system working exactly as designed. The company found the residue of its own bad repair, expanded the net, and kept digging until August. A manufacturer that chases a residual defect is better than one that stops at the first campaign and hopes. Counterpoint: the second recall existed because dealer techs botched the first one. The customer paid for that mistake in repeat visits, and NHTSA had to open an investigation in late 2025 before the third campaign materialized.[6]

What to do: own a 2021-2023 Grand Cherokee L, a 2022-2023 Grand Cherokee, or a 2022-2023 Grand Cherokee 4xe? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls; VINs became searchable on September 9, and air-suspension models are excluded. If you had the spring inspected under an earlier recall, schedule the visit anyway and tell the dealer you are there for campaign 89D. Get on the list now instead of waiting for the second letter. The remedy, replacing both rear coil spring lower isolators, is free. Bring a book.[1][5]

What this story does not prove: it says nothing about Grand Cherokee crash outcomes, since FARS plays no role in a recall mechanics story and the company reports zero injuries. The 0.1% defect estimate, the complaint counts, and the re-repair figures all come from Stellantis filings to NHTSA; nobody independent has verified them. And "detach while driving" is dramatic but underspecified: NHTSA's consequence language describes a road hazard to other vehicles, and we have no data on how often a shed spring causes a secondary crash.
