Tesla's Robotaxis Got a Human Backup Driver. Every Documented One Crashed.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 22, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: the backup driver might be the most dangerous driver in the fleet. Tesla's robotaxis come with a human safety net: when the car gets confused, a remote operator takes over. And according to Tesla's own filings with federal regulators, every documented remote takeover has ended in a crash.[2]

5 of 5
Documented remote takeovers that ended in a collision — all at walking speed

Five interventions, five collisions, and the two newest arrived in August: a Model Y in Dallas sat wedged between a concrete mixer and a parked SUV when a remote operator took over and nosed it into the SUV at 0.40 miles per hour, and in Austin a remote-driven Model Y threaded an alley between a utility pole and a dumpster before striking the dumpster at about 1 mph, with no passengers aboard either car. Both sit in NHTSA's Standing General Order data under Driver/Operator Type: "Remote (Commercial / Test)."[2]

The other three surfaced only after Tesla unredacted its crash narratives in May: in July 2025, an Austin robotaxi refused to move, so a teleoperator took over, accelerated, and drove it up a curb into a metal fence at 8 mph; in January 2026, a teleoperator helping with navigation guided one into a construction barricade at 9 mph; and in Houston, a Model Y stuck at a dead end hit a hidden tree stump at 2 mph.[3]

Nobody was hurt, which is genuinely good news wrapped around something cosmically funny: the backup human apparently only drives in situations so constrained that crashing means a curb, a dumpster, or a stump. Meanwhile the robot takes the highway at speed while the human gets the alley, so guess who has the cleaner record.

Then came October 15, when NHTSA's data release documented all five of these crashes, followed on October 21 by Tesla's first Robotaxi Safety Report: 3.1 million driverless miles, zero injuries, zero airbag deployments.[5] But Tesla's injury count only includes collisions where the robotaxi made first contact, two claimed injury events were excluded from the report, and those 3.1 million miles include trips where a remote operator was assisting or had taken over.[6] The zero in that headline is doing a lot of quiet work.

Waymo does it differently and said so on the record: in a February 2026 letter to Senator Ed Markey, the company stated its remote staff never perform the driving task, since they advise while the car accepts or rejects the guidance.[3] Tesla's approach lets the remote human drive the car through a screen, and the difference between advising and steering is, in the only data available, the difference between zero crashes and five.

Fairness demands the counterargument at full strength, because Tesla does not disclose how many remote interventions happen: five crashes are five numerators with no denominator, crash reports exist only when something crashes, and remote operators get summoned to the five hardest minutes of the fleet's day. You cannot build a failure rate from this data, and anyone who does is selling you something.

What this means for you

If you live in Austin, Dallas, Houston, Orlando, Tampa, or Miami, give a stuck robotaxi a wide berth, since the person about to drive it is watching through a camera feed with real latency, and history says the odds are not great. And if you don't live in a robotaxi city: remote driving has no FMVSS standard, no published safety rate, and no regulator asking for one. The industry's only published teleoperation statistic is five for five. Ask for the denominator, because everyone should be asking for it.
