The Gap

Congress Ordered a Hot-Car Rule by 2023. 98 Children Have Died Since.

Clara Rollover • Consumer Safety Desk
September 20, 2026

Earlier this month in Plantation, Florida, police were called to A World of Discovery Academy, a bilingual daycare, for a report of a deceased child in a vehicle; the academy owner opened the car door and found an 18-month-old boy in the back seat. His father had been supposed to drop him off that morning. He forgot the boy was in the car, drove to work, and came back in the afternoon believing his son was inside the school.

Before you buckle your kid in for the morning school run, you might want to see this. That father did not forget because he was careless in some special way. Kids and Car Safety, the advocacy group that tracks these deaths, says it plainly: in an overwhelming majority of hot-car deaths, it was a loving, responsible parent who unknowingly left the child. Nearly half of the unknowingly-left children were supposed to be dropped off at childcare. This father was the rule, not the exception.

Now here is the part that should make you angry. Congress saw this exact scenario coming. In 2021, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act ordered the Department of Transportation to act, and the Hot Cars provision required NHTSA to issue a final rule on rear-occupant detection by November 2023. That deadline passed 34 months ago. NHTSA has not even published a proposed rule. NHTSA's own estimate said the proposal would arrive in April 2025. That date came and went too, with nothing to show for it, according to Kids and Car Safety, which has been pushing for the standard for more than two decades.

During the three calendar years since the deadline year, 98 children have died in hot cars: 39 in 2024, by NHTSA's own count, plus 35 in 2025 and 24 so far in 2026, per NoHeatStroke.org, whose researchers updated the tally on September 15. Since 1998, the running total stands at 1,069. NHTSA puts the toll above 1,000 and says heatstroke kills around 40 children in cars every year.

More than half of those deaths, 52.9 percent across the 1,041 cases from 1998 through 2025, are classified as forgotten by caregiver: 551 children. That is precisely the failure Congress wrote the law to interrupt, a detection system that notices a child who cannot get out on their own and refuses to let the car sit silent. The Plantation case is a textbook instance. A machine does not have a routine to deviate from.

You might wonder why this keeps happening when new cars already beep at you about the back seat. USA Today reported this summer that a majority of 2025-model-year vehicles carry a rear-passenger alert under an industry-wide program. Yet 24 children have died in 2026 anyway. Here is why: most of those voluntary systems are rear-door logic. They notice a rear door opened at the start of the trip and nag you when the car parks. They do not detect a person. What Congress required was occupant detection, a system that senses a child who cannot exit independently. Door logic is a reminder. Detection is a rescue. Yet the voluntary experiment ran at scale, and the body count moved anyway.

Physics does not negotiate. A parked car gains about 20 degrees in 10 minutes. Interiors can reach 125 degrees in minutes even with windows cracked, and a 60-degree day can still kill a child left inside. A small child's body heats three to five times faster than an adult's, and more than half the victims are under two. On September 13, a 5-year-old boy died in Phoenix when it was 103 degrees outside. Texas leads the nation with 173 deaths as of 2025.

Methodology

Every number in this article comes from three places, and I want you to know whose number is whose. NoHeatStroke.org, a research project run by San Jose State meteorologists that compiles media reports of pediatric vehicular heatstroke, updated its counts on September 15, 2026: 24 deaths in 2026, 35 in 2025, 1,069 since 1998, with circumstance data covering the 1,041 deaths from 1998 through 2025. NHTSA's own press materials provide the 2024 total of 39, up from 29 in 2023, and the agency's figure of around 40 deaths per year. Kids and Car Safety, the advocacy group, tracks the rulemaking and publishes the November 2023 deadline, the missing proposed rule, and the missed April 2025 estimate. Thirty-four months is the calendar distance from November 2023 to September 20, 2026; the 98 is the sum of 39, 35, and 24.

Limitations

These counts are built from media reports, which means they get revised upward as late cases surface. Mid-year reporting put the 2025 total at 31; it now stands at 35, and the 2026 count of 24 will almost certainly rise. NHTSA and the advocacy groups do not always agree on annual totals, because they count slightly different things; I use each source's own number and say whose it is. I cannot prove the rule would have saved any specific child, and 98 is a death toll in the deadline's wake, not a prevented-deaths claim. Kelly Blue Book via USA Today supplies the voluntary-system penetration figure; I cannot independently verify how many of those systems are true occupant detection versus door-logic reminders, which is exactly the distinction this article turns on, so treat that penetration number as a ceiling rather than a floor.

The Counterargument

Now the honest pushback, because the anger needs it. A final rule would apply to new vehicles only, and the American fleet is old: S&P Global Mobility puts the average age of light vehicles on U.S. roads at a record 12.8 years in 2025. A rule issued tomorrow would take years to meaningfully penetrate the cars people actually drive, and much of the 2026 body count rides in vehicles no rule could have reached. Then there is the voluntary experiment: most new cars already chime about the back seat, and children still die, which suggests the failure is behavioral as much as technological. Alerts get disabled, dismissed as nagging, or tuned out; false positives teach drivers to silence the system. And 45.7 percent of deaths, the 21.9 percent knowingly left plus the 23.8 percent who climbed in on their own, are scenarios a rear-seat alert addresses only partly or not at all. A locked car cannot be climbed into, but no federal rule locks your car. Finally, rulemakings take years for reasons that are not all sinister: cost-benefit analysis, false-positive testing, hundreds of competing mandates. The deadline is a legal fact. The lives-saved counterfactual is not.

Fair enough. But the counterfactual cuts the other way too: the agency has not even published a proposal, so nobody has ever tested what a performance standard for true detection would do. You cannot evaluate a rule you never wrote. And while the fleet-turnover argument limits what a rule could accomplish quickly, it says nothing about why the process has not started at all.

What You Can Do

Do not wait for the rule. Put your phone, your bag, or one of your shoes in the back seat on every trip. NHTSA's own guidance says to keep whatever you need to start your day back there, so you cannot leave the car without opening the rear door. Open that door every single time you park, and make it muscle memory rather than memory: stop, look, lock. Call your daycare and ask them to call you if your child is not dropped off on schedule. Nearly half of unknowingly-left children were supposed to be at childcare that day, and a 9 a.m. phone call is the cheapest rescue system ever invented. Lock your car and keep the keys where children cannot reach them, because almost a quarter of victims got in on their own. And if you see a child alone in a car, call 911 immediately.
