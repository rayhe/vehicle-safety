8.3 Million Cars With No Locks. FARS Can’t Count the Dead.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
July 27, 2026

A USB cable and a screwdriver. That is what it takes to steal a 2017 Kia Soul, a 2019 Hyundai Elantra, or any of the 8.3 million Hyundai and Kia vehicles sold in the United States between 2011 and 2022 without engine immobilizers. NHTSA says the resulting theft epidemic killed at least 8 people.[1] The real number is unknowable, and the reason it is unknowable tells you more about American crash data than about Korean automakers.

FARS, the Fatality Analysis Reporting System, is the gold standard. It records every fatal crash on a US public road: vehicle make, model, model year, speed, impairment, restraint use, crash geometry. It does not record whether the vehicle was stolen.[2] I looked. The Person file, the Vehicle file, the Accident file. Nowhere. If a 15-year-old steals a Kia Forte, drives it into a telephone pole at 80 mph, and dies, FARS records a Kia Forte fatal crash involving a 15-year-old driver. It does not record a theft. The distinction vanishes into the database, and the automaker's liability vanishes with it.

Across FARS 2014–2023, the 11 theft-vulnerable Hyundai and Kia models account for 8,832 deaths in 15,338 fatal crashes. How many of those crashes involved a stolen vehicle? Nobody can tell you. Not NHTSA. Not the IIHS. Not the automakers who settled with 35 state attorneys general in December 2025 for creating the vulnerability in the first place.[3]

NHTSA itself once knew this mattered. When it wrote FMVSS 114, the federal anti-theft standard, it cited a Department of Justice study finding that stolen vehicles crashed at 200 times the normal rate.[4] Two hundred times. The agency considered vehicle theft serious enough to write a federal regulation preventing it. Then it built a fatal-crash database that does not track it.

Meanwhile, the numbers that FARS does capture paint a specific portrait. The Hyundai Veloster, one of the affected models, carries the highest fatality rate in the entire FARS dataset: 8.54 deaths per 100 million VMT, more than double the sports car segment average of 3.80. The conventional explanation is demographics: young male buyers, hot hatch, affordable speed. But the Veloster is also one of the easiest cars in America to steal, and FARS cannot distinguish between the two explanations.

The immobilizer gap itself was deliberate. By 2015, 96% of vehicles from other manufacturers shipped with electronic immobilizers standard. Hyundai and Kia had them on just 26%.[1] They installed immobilizers in vehicles sold in Canada and Mexico. In the US, they shipped the same cars without them.[5] Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison called it a crisis "that started in a boardroom." In Milwaukee, Hyundai and Kia vehicles accounted for 58% of all auto theft in 2022. Minneapolis saw an 836% increase. Chicago lost 7,000 in a single year.[6]

The software fix did not fix it. One man testified at the December 2025 settlement hearing that his car had been stolen nine times, including seven times after the software update.[5] The 35-state settlement now requires a physical hardware upgrade: a zinc-reinforced ignition cylinder protector. Estimated cost to the automakers: north of $500 million.[7]

Eight deaths is what NHTSA has in voluntary complaints. The FARS database, which covers every fatal crash in America, structurally cannot count the theft-related subset. That is not an oversight. It is an architecture.
