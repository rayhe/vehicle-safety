Mercedes Recalled Its New Cars 8 Times. The Smallest Is 10 Cars. The Biggest Can Roll Away.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 12, 2026

Thirteen brands into the recall census, Mercedes files eight. Eight campaigns, 455,948 vehicles, and 99.7 percent of them sit in just two campaigns. A door lock that can let the car roll away after you get out. A dashboard that goes blank while you drive. Three seat belts, one airbag, one bus door, and a 10-car recall for a door that cannot be opened from inside.[1][6][10][12]

8
NHTSA campaigns filed in 2026 touching a 2026 Mercedes-Benz. Thirteen-brand total is 152, and Mercedes filed about a third below the 11.7 average. Two campaigns hold 454,716 of the 455,948 vehicles.

Method first, because a count is only as honest as the query behind it. I ran 94 queries against the NHTSA recalls API using the official 2026 model list, then 130 more using the trim-level spellings NHTSA actually files under, then 43 more with AMG, Maybach, plug-in, and electric spellings. Most of them came back HTTP 400, not empty results but errors. Seventeen trim queries worked, and those seventeen found seven campaigns, but the eighth only surfaced through a press cross-check, because NHTSA files the electric CLA as "CLA 250+ (EV)" and the API has no working vocabulary for "CLA 250" at all.[1][2] That 16-car seat-belt recall was invisible to every standard spelling. Control queries prove the API itself works: the same spellings return real campaigns for 2025 models, including an S-Class brake-hose recall and a 2025 eSprinter campaign. A census that misses a recall and then finds it is worth more than a census that never looks for the miss.

Thirteen-brand average is 11.7 campaigns, which puts Mercedes about four below average, one above BMW, double Volkswagen, and a fifth of Ford. Count alone, that reads as a quiet year, but populations say something else. Mercedes filed 455,948 campaign-vehicle instances against 248,700 vehicles sold in the US through September, and the distribution is absurd: the two big campaigns hold 454,716 instances, while the other six hold 1,232 combined. Median recall size this year was 127 vehicles, against a mean of 56,994. That gap is the whole census.[15]

Start with the big one, because it is a masterclass in how a small supplier error becomes a five-year defect. Campaign 26V481, filed July 24, covers 310,667 cars from model years 2019 through 2026: CLA, A-Class, C-Class, CLE, GLA, GLB, and GLC. Inside the driver's door lock sits a microswitch whose job is to detect an open door, and the switch housing was made from the wrong material, a supplier development error that lets moisture permeate the housing over time. Corrosion follows. Once corroded, the switch can fail to detect an open door, which means the electronic parking brake and the Auto-Park feature may not engage when the driver gets out, which means the car can roll away after its driver has walked away from it. Mercedes logged 176 US field cases between March 2021 and June 2026, all comfort-function related at first, radios and lights not switching off when occupants exited, which is what a dying door switch looks like before it becomes a safety defect. No US rollaway is known, though rollaways have happened overseas. Fix is a new door lock, free, with a phased remedy and a production change that took effect May 20, 2026.[12] A microswitch is a part the size of a thumbnail. It took five model years and 176 field cases to recall it.

Next comes the one that started life as a software update. Campaign 26V281, filed May 1, covers 144,049 vehicles from 2024 through 2026: AMG GT, C-Class, E-Class, SL, CLE, and GLC. Infotainment control unit software can trigger repeated system resets as part of an intended fallback behavior, and each reset can blank the instrument cluster display while the car is moving, taking the speedometer and the warning lights with it. Mercedes had already rolled the fix out over the air starting in August 2025, and Korea demanded a recall that December, but the US filing only happened after NHTSA discussed the issue with Mercedes in April 2026 and, in the filing's own words, emphasized that other manufacturers had addressed instrument panel display issues through safety recalls. On April 27, Mercedes converted the remaining over-the-air campaign into a safety recall. Count the evidence behind that conversion: 721 warranty claims and 652 field or service reports in the US, and zero crashes or injuries known. Fix is a dealer software update, free.[10] Read that chronology twice. A blank dashboard was a software update until a regulator said it was a recall.

Now the micro-recalls, because three of the eight campaigns cover 46 vehicles combined, and they are the most honest recalls in the file. Campaign 26V134 covers exactly 10 GLA 250s, built across two days in the summer of 2025, because a bowden cable on the interior door release was not mounted correctly in its clip. A bowden cable is a steel wire inside a sleeve, the mechanical link between the interior door handle and the latch, and if it detaches, the door cannot be opened from inside. Ten cars, two days of production, and one pre-delivery inspection on an international vehicle that caught it. Campaign 26V172 covers 20 GLCs built across three days in September 2025, because Lear installed a center rear seat belt variant without the child-seat protection function, so the belt cannot lock a child restraint in place. Campaign 26V278 covers 16 electric CLAs, because the front passenger seat-belt retractor was built with a defect that prevents the child-seat safety function from deactivating, so the belt locks and refuses to extend. Zero US claims or field reports across all three. Zero injuries. Wrong part, wrong variant, wrong batch, found in production records, recalled, fixed.[6][7][9] That is what a recall looks like when the system works. Compare it with the door lock that took five years.

The remaining pair came out of Alabama, where campaigns 26V199 and 26V353 both cover 2026 GLE and GLS SUVs from the Tuscaloosa plant, and both were found by internal monitoring with zero US field reports. 26V199, filed March 27, covers 932 cars whose passenger airbag fabric was manufactured incorrectly, so the bag may not deploy as intended in a crash. 26V353, filed May 29, covers 203 cars built between January 21 and February 16, 2026, whose rear seat-belt bolts were not tightened to specification after a rework, so the belt may not restrain a rear passenger. One hundred percent of the 203 cars have the defect, not a statistical tail but every single one of them. Both fixes are free, and both defects are the kind that only matter in the worst 150 milliseconds of a car's life, which is exactly when a seat belt bolt and an airbag fabric have to work.[8][11]

And then there is the bus. Campaign 26V063, filed February 4, covers 51 Tourrider transit buses, because a door control valve may not release its self-holding mechanism and the door will not open. It is filed by Daimler Coaches, not by Mercedes-Benz USA, and it is the only 2026 Mercedes recall that involves a vehicle with no steering wheel in the usual sense and no owner to write to, only a transit agency, but a door that will not open is a door that will not open whether the vehicle carries one passenger or fifty, so count it anyway.[5]

Now the clean cars, because a census that only counts the guilty misses the pattern in the innocent. S-Class: zero. G-Class: zero. EQE: zero. EQS: zero. Sprinter: zero. Five model lines, including the flagship sedan, the G-Wagen, and the entire dedicated electric lineup, with no 2026 recall between them. GLA and GLC carry two each, GLE and GLS carry two each, and the cheapest car in the lineup is tied for dirtiest, though caveats apply in both directions: a 2026 EQE or GLC is still covered by a steering recall filed last August, 25V533, for a coupling bolt that can loosen and cost the car its steering, and a 2022 through 2025 S-Class is covered by the brake-hose expansion filed October 2, 26V638, for hoses that can leak fluid and cut braking in one circuit. Neither is in this census, because the census counts filings in 2026 and the steering recall was filed in 2025 while the brake-hose recall touches no 2026 car. Both are the scariest defects in the file for the cars they cover.[3][4][13][14]

Give the skeptics their due, because their case is real. Eight recalls is a small number, and a census that counts filings counts paperwork, not safety. Six of the eight cover 1,232 vehicles combined, three of them cover 10, 16, and 20 cars, and all three were found by internal monitoring with zero US field reports. A skeptic will say the honest headline is that Mercedes built a quiet year: the S-Class has zero recalls, the G-Wagen has zero, the entire EV lineup has zero, the GLA's 10-car recall is a rounding error, and the two big campaigns are a software update and a door lock, neither of which has killed anyone in the US. That reading is mostly fair, and the article should say so. Counterweight: a defect rate of 100 percent in a 10-car recall is only comforting if you are not one of the ten, and a door that cannot be opened from inside is a trap, not a statistic. And the two big campaigns are not small in the ways that matter: 26V481 is a five-model-year corrosion defect that took 176 field cases to recall, and 26V281 is a blank dashboard that Mercedes treated as a software update until NHTSA said otherwise. A recall count of eight is good news for Mercedes. A rollaway defect that took five years to find is not good news for anyone who owns the car.

What this story does not prove matters too. It counts campaigns filed January 1 through November 12, 2026, so a campaign filed tomorrow would not appear. No spelling tried for the EQS, the AMG GT, or the SL returns a result from the NHTSA API for any model year, so the zero for those three lines rests on independent trackers and on the fact that any campaign touching the C-Class, E-Class, GLC, GLE, or GLS would have surfaced in the seventeen successful trim queries, not on a single clean query. The 248,700 sales figure is US retail from January through September across all model years, including vans and 2025 models, so it is a denominator for scale, not a precise population. Campaign populations are the manufacturers' own numbers in their Part 573 filings, and the 455,948 total counts campaign-vehicle instances, not distinct vehicles: a 2026 GLA 250 in both 26V134 and 26V481 counts twice. A European EQS and EQE battery software recall from May 2026 and a Japanese eCall speaker campaign from the same month have no US equivalent in the file and are not counted.

What to do with this: own a 2020 through 2026 GLA or GLB? You are in 26V481, and the fix is a new door lock, free, but until it is replaced, set the parking brake yourself every time, because the car may not set it for you. Own a 2024 through 2026 AMG GT, C-Class, E-Class, SL, CLE, or GLC? You are in 26V281, and the fix is a dealer software update, free. Own a 2026 GLE or GLS? You are in two recalls, 26V199 for the airbag and 26V353 for the seat-belt bolts, both small, both free, and both worth checking by VIN. Own a 2026 GLC? You are in 26V172 and 26V281, and you are also covered by 25V533, the steering recall from last August, which is the urgent one. Own a 2026 CLA EV? You are in 26V278, sixteen cars, and the recall is so small it hid from the standard NHTSA search, so check your VIN even if the recall lists say your model has none. Own a 2026 GLA 250? You are in 26V134 and 26V481, and the 10-car recall is a door that cannot be opened from inside. Own a 2026 S-Class, G-Class, EQE, EQS, or Sprinter? As of today there is no safety recall on your car, but check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls anyway, and if your S-Class is a 2022 through 2025, you are in 26V638 for the brake hoses, with letters going out November 27.[1][6][7][8][9][10][11][12][13]
