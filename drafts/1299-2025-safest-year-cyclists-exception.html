2025 Was the Safest Year on American Roads in a Decade. Cyclists Didn't Get the Memo.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 27, 2026

36,640 people died on American roads in 2025. That number is a tragedy and, by the standards of the last decade, a genuine improvement. NHTSA's early estimate, published April 1, 2026, puts 2025 deaths 6.7% below 2024's 39,254, the fourth straight annual decline and the 15th consecutive quarterly decline.[1] Americans drove more miles and died less often doing it: vehicle miles traveled rose about 0.9% while deaths fell.[1]

Traffic deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled in 2025, down from 1.19 in 2024 and the second-lowest fatality rate on record, behind only 1.08 in 2014

Speeding deaths fell 11%. Rollover deaths fell 11%. Unrestrained occupant deaths fell 13%, ejected occupant deaths fell 13%, and deaths in vehicles under 10 years old fell 12%, nearly double the national rate of improvement.[2] Pedestrian deaths fell 8%, large-truck-involved deaths fell 7%, and deaths on urban arterials fell 12%.[2] Every major category in NHTSA's July 2026 breakdown moved the right way, except one.

Cyclist deaths rose 4% in 2025, the only increase in the breakdown.[2] Zoom out and the exception turns into a trend: bicyclist deaths climbed 29% from 2020 to 2025, from 891 to 1,148, while pedestrian deaths rose 5% over the same span, from 6,236 to 6,529.[3] People on foot and on bikes now account for 21% of all traffic deaths.[3] Nobody can say from these releases whether more people rode bikes or each ride got riskier, because NHTSA's figures here do not report miles ridden. Either way, the road got safer for people inside cars, and not for people on bikes.

Where you drive matters as much as how you drive: Rhode Island's fatality rate was 4.7 deaths per billion vehicle miles traveled, while New Mexico's was 15.9, a gap of nearly three and a half times.[4] Rural states dominate the deadly end of the list, Mississippi, South Carolina, Arizona, Arkansas, Montana, and West Virginia, and the pattern points at speed, distance, and how long an ambulance takes to reach a rural crash.[4] Fatalities fell in 39 states plus the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico, so the improvement is broad, but the map still punishes rural miles.[1]

TRIP puts the societal harm from fatal and serious crashes in 2025 at $1.75 trillion, $435 billion in economic costs and $1.32 trillion in quality-of-life losses, using NHTSA's crash-cost methodology.[3] Motorcyclists alone account for 16% of all traffic deaths on under 1% of vehicle miles traveled.[3] Work-zone crashes killed 4,481 people between 2020 and 2024.[3] Averages like these are easy to skim past. Each one is a family that did not get the safer year.

What you can actually do: if you ride a bike, pick the separated lane and the daylight route, because arterials got 12% safer for drivers and still killed cyclists at rising rates. If you drive in a high-rate state, slow down on rural two-lanes, since that is where the state gap lives. If you buy used, favor a vehicle under 10 years old, since that group improved nearly twice as fast as the national average. And buckle up in every seat: unrestrained and ejected deaths posted the two largest drops in the breakdown, 13% each, and the belt remains the cheapest safety device ever sold.[2]

What this article did not prove: 2025 figures are NHTSA early estimates, not final FARS counts, and revisions may move them. Category percentages come from a secondary summary of NHTSA's July 2026 report; the underlying research note was not read directly for this article. No single cause is proven for any decline: enforcement, vehicle technology, road design, and post-crash care all plausibly contribute, and crediting one would be guessing. No 2025 cyclist death count is claimed here, only the 4% change, because no verified count was found in these sources.

Fair counter, stated at full strength: 36,640 deaths is still 3% above 2015's 35,484, and the count exceeds every year from 2009 through 2015.[3] Calling 2025 the safest year in a decade rests on the rate, not the body count, and a rate is cold comfort to anyone counting bodies. True, and also the wrong frame to stop at: the rate is what adjusts for how much America drove, and America drove more in 2025 and died less per mile than in any year except 2014. Both facts belong in the same sentence. Progress is real, and 100 deaths a day is still a national failure.
