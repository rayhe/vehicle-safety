Twice the Limit, No Seatbelt: The Drunk-Driving Epidemic Is a 0.15 Problem

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 4, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and believe me there are a lot of them, America's drunk-driving problem is not what the public service announcements say it is. PSAs picture a decent person who had one glass too many at dinner. NHTSA's new 2024 figures, released in June, picture something else: a man in his early twenties, driving after midnight, unbelted, usually with a prior DWI already on his record, carrying a blood-alcohol level that would make a breathalyzer ask for a second opinion. Of the 11,904 people killed in alcohol-impaired crashes last year, 68 percent, 8,097 of them, died in crashes involving a driver at 0.15 or higher, nearly twice the legal limit.[1]

68%
Share of 2024 alcohol-impaired crash deaths involving a driver at BAC 0.15+, nearly twice the legal limit

Run the arithmetic and the epidemic stops looking like a drinking-culture problem. Those 8,097 deaths are 20.6 percent of every traffic death in America last year. One in five. Meanwhile the 0.08-to-0.14 band, the population the entire apparatus of .05-limit debates, checkpoint PSAs, and one-drink-an-hour folk wisdom is aimed at, accounts for the remaining 3,807. That is 32 percent. We built a deterrence machine for the minority and left the majority to the night shift.

Now look at the seatbelts, because this is where the numbers get personal. Among killed impaired drivers with known restraint use, 64 percent were unbelted. Split by BAC and a brutal gradient appears: 65 percent unbelted at 0.15 and above, 57 percent at 0.01 to 0.07, 41 percent stone sober.[1] Read that column top to bottom. Whatever erases the 0.08 line in a driver's head erases the belt line too. A seatbelt is the one safety device that works while its user is incapacitated, and the people who need it most are the ones who never click it.

65%
Share of killed drivers at BAC 0.15+ who were unrestrained, vs 41% of sober drivers killed

Everything else in the file is pure box-score material. Impairment rates triple at night.[2] The 21-to-24 bracket leads every age group at 28 percent impaired. Men outnumber women four to one among impaired drivers in fatal crashes, 8,889 to 2,209. And here is the stat that should end every debate about where to aim enforcement: impaired drivers in fatal crashes are three times more likely to have a prior DWI conviction, 6 percent against 2.[1] This is not a population discovering alcohol for the first time. It is a population the system has already met, already processed, and already released back onto the road.

Wipe the 0.15 tail off the road entirely and alcohol deaths fall 68 percent, the ceiling of any interlock-grade intervention. Compare that ceiling to what the current regime actually captures: ignition interlocks aimed at convicted offenders only, which the research puts at roughly 837 deaths averted per year.[7] The deployed tool reaches maybe a tenth of the addressable population. That gap, between the ceiling and the capture, is where 7,000-plus people a year are dying.

Honest objection first, because the file has one. That 68 percent is partly a measurement artifact. NHTSA imputes BACs where tests are missing, and missing tests are not random: surviving drivers get tested only 19 percent of the time, and the drivers who look drunk get tested first.[3] Chronic heavy drinkers also build tolerance, so a 0.15 alcoholic can be less functionally impaired than a novice at 0.08. The agency's own fine print says "involved" does not mean "caused"; a 0.15 driver rear-ended at a red light still counts.[1] All fair. But even heavily discounted, the tail dominates the count. And 3,807 deaths in the lower band is not an argument that the lower band is safe. It is an argument that we have two epidemics and one playbook.

What this does not prove: the 2024 numbers come from the Annual Report File, not the final FARS release, so expect revisions. The imputation model carries confidence intervals the fact sheet never publishes. FARS excludes private-property crashes entirely. And the file records convictions, not arrests, so that 6 percent prior-DWI figure is a floor; the true repeat rate is higher by an amount nobody can measure.

So what do you do with a tail? Stop aiming at the average. Your seatbelt is the cheapest intervention that survives intoxication: buckle up sober, leave the chimes on, and if you are riding with someone deep into the evening, your belt is the countermeasure that still works when their judgment does not. After midnight, drive like the other guy is at 0.15, because among the impaired ones he usually is. For the policy crowd, the .05 debate touches the 32 percent. The 68 percent responds to exactly one deployed tool, the ignition interlock. Check whether your state mandates interlocks for first offenders. Most still carve out exceptions, which is another way of saying the tail gets a head start.
