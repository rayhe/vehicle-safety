They Drifted a Mustang Next to Schoolchildren. Three Juries Charged $931 Million.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. In the summer of 2026, three juries in Pontiac, Michigan looked at the same 2023 car crash and kept adding zeroes. The crash happened at a test track in Auburn Hills owned by Continental Automotive Systems, during a field trip for a Detroit-area STEM summer camp. A Continental test driver, Linus Gugino, was demonstrating a Ford Mustang Dark Horse prototype on loan from Ford, and the plaintiffs say he spun it out to impress the children standing trackside.[1] He failed to regain control. The car snapped sideways into the guardrail where the kids stood, bounced, and landed on its roof. Two boys, eight and ten, were left with broken legs, brain injuries, and memory problems.[3] Three years later, the combined jury awards totaled $931,233,970.[2]

$931M
Combined jury awards across three trials, July through September 2026

Continental admitted fault in all three trials, which is corporate for we did it, let's just argue about the price. In late July, a jury awarded $896,467,970 to the brain-injured child, believed to be Michigan's largest single-plaintiff verdict ever; Continental's own lawyers had suggested $10.5 million.[1] The jury multiplied the company's number by eighty-five. In early August, a second jury gave the boy's father, Vincent Gibson, $2,066,000 for watching his son get hit, believed to be the state's largest bystander verdict. On September 11, a third jury awarded about $32.7 million to the other injured child.[2] Total: $931.2 million, from one afternoon at a test track.

What should keep Continental's board awake is not the zeroes; it is the zero settlement offers. The company never made one, not once, after admitting liability in all three trials. Plaintiff attorney Jon Marko told Courtroom View Network the cause was a breakdown between Continental's trial team and its German insurers, who carried roughly $450 million in coverage, and that the assistant general counsel handling the case had since left.[1] Judge David Cohen sanctioned Continental $1 million for lying about its insurance limits: a company facing nearly a billion dollars in exposure hid insurance from the court and got fined a rounding error, and Marko says defense teams in other Michigan cases started finding coverage the moment the order landed. $931 million is a verdict. $1 million is a signal.

Fairness demands the other side of the ledger, and the other side is sturdy. These were damages-only trials: no jury found the Mustang defective, no jury found a brake failure, and Continental's admission means the crash mechanics were never relitigated, so the plaintiffs' drift account stands unchallenged. Juries priced a child's destroyed brain and a lifetime of care, not a car's engineering. Marko himself conceded the sticker shock could trim the $896.5 million on appeal, and verdicts are not payments.[1] Ford, which loaned the prototype, was named in the original suit alongside the driver and the camp organizer, but these trials ran against Continental alone.[4] Dismiss this as a litigation story rather than a safety story, and the facts will let you.

But one sentence should be laminated inside every test facility on earth. Continental is a brake and chassis company, and the defect in this story was not a brake. It was crowd control, at the company's own test track, where a 500-horsepower prototype was allegedly slid sideways in front of schoolchildren behind a guardrail. Setback distance is the cheapest safety device in motorsport, and it costs nothing. Organizers: excitement is the hazard, and parents, if your kids get invited to a test track, ask where they stand. Admit liability, fight only on damages while your insurers are not answering the phone, and $10.5 million becomes $896.5 million in a single summer.
