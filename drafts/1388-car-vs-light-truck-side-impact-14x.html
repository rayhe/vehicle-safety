The Gap

A Truck's Front End Hits Your Car's Side. The Death Ratio Is 14 to 1

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 4, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. In 2024, passenger cars and light trucks met in 3,309 fatal two-vehicle crashes, and the body count inside those crashes split 2,687 car occupants dead against 955 light-truck occupants, a ratio of 2.8 to 1 that NHTSA publishes in its October 2026 Passenger Vehicles fact sheet.[1]

13.9 to 1
Car occupants killed vs light-truck occupants when a truck's front end hits a car's side, 2024

Head-on, a car occupant died 2.7 times as often as a light-truck occupant in 2024, which sounds bad until you rotate the crash ninety degrees. When a light truck's front end hit a passenger car's side, car occupants died 13.9 times as often as light-truck occupants, a figure that held between 12.4 and 14.2 across every year from 2020 through 2024.[1] Divide 13.9 by 2.7 and the side-impact ratio runs more than five times the head-on ratio for the same two vehicle classes, in the same year, from the same dataset.

Flip the geometry once more and the story flips with it. When a car's front end hit a light truck's side, light-truck occupants died 1.4 times as often as car occupants in 2024, the single configuration in the fact sheet where the heavier vehicle comes off worse.[1] Mass alone cannot explain a ratio that reverses direction when you swap which bumper does the hitting, which means structure, ride height, and crash circumstances are all loading into numbers that look, at first glance, like pure physics.

Meanwhile the fleet keeps sorting itself into the heavier half. Light-truck registrations reached 184.1 million in 2024, up 3 percent, against 98.8 million passenger cars, down 3 percent, or nearly two light trucks registered for every car.[1] And cars still post the worse overall fatality rate per registered vehicle, 10.81 deaths per 100,000 cars against 6.54 for light trucks, because that rate counts every crash type including single-vehicle crashes where there is no heavier opponent to blame.[1] Car occupants made up 73.8 percent of all deaths in car-versus-light-truck two-vehicle fatal crashes, and those crashes averaged 1.10 occupant deaths apiece.

Give the skeptics their due, because these ratios are raw fatality counts, not a controlled experiment. NHTSA's fact sheet does not adjust them for speed, belt use, driver age, alcohol, or how many people were riding in each vehicle, and it does not publish the crash counts behind each configuration ratio, so 13.9 cannot be decomposed any further from this document alone. Both pair-crash death counts fell roughly 10 percent in 2024, car occupants from 2,982 to 2,687 and light-truck occupants from 1,052 to 955, so the asymmetry persists inside an improving total rather than a worsening one.[1] One more caveat belongs in the open: 2024 figures come from NHTSA's Annual Report File, the first release, and will move slightly when the Final File lands.

What, exactly, do you do with a number like 13.9? If you drive a car, shop the IIHS side-impact rating and side curtain airbags as hard as you shop horsepower, because the side of a car is the weak axis against a truck's front end and no amount of defensive driving changes the mass ratio once the truck is already in your door. Wear the belt on every trip: NHTSA credits belts with cutting fatal-injury risk 45 percent for car occupants and 60 percent for light-truck occupants.[1] If you drive a light truck, do not mistake mass for immunity, since the one configuration where truck occupants die more often is a car striking your side. At intersections, treat a stale green light and a turning truck as the highest-lethality ordinary situation a car driver faces, and check any specific model at IIHS ratings before signing anything, since fleet-level ratios do not rank individual models.[3]
