# Research: 1275 — Nine Percent, Half the Deaths

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
AAA's September 2026 mixed-methods study finds the same inversion NHTSA's fatal-crash
data has shown for years: about 9 in 10 adults buckle up, yet unbelted occupants account
for nearly half of traffic deaths. The article turns that inversion into an original
share-ratio calculation, then states the strongest case against it: the unbelted 9%
are not a random sample, they are the same people driving impaired, at night, on rural
roads, in pickups, in the back seat.

## Verified facts (with attribution, no fabrication)
- General-population belt use: 91% of adults buckle up (AAA release); 90% in Safety+Health
  summary of the same study. Use "about 9 in 10" to cover both.
- FARS analytic sample (2017-2022): belt use 72% among drivers and passengers in fatal
  crashes, vs 91% general-population adult front-seat use (NCSA, 2025) — AAAFTS technical
  report, Appendix comparison.
- 28%: share reported as unbelted among drivers/passengers involved in a crash resulting
  in a fatality in the past 30 days (AAAFTS summary wording; report it exactly as AAA
  states it, do not re-interpret the item).
- Passengers vs drivers in FARS sample: 31% of passengers unbelted vs 27% of drivers.
- NHTSA 2022 (known restraint use): 50% of killed passenger-vehicle occupants were
  unrestrained vs 14% of survivors unrestrained. 11,302 unrestrained passenger-vehicle
  occupants died in 2022.
- Effectiveness (NHTSA, Kahane 2015): lap/shoulder belts cut fatal injury risk 45% for
  front-seat car occupants and 60% for front-seat light-truck occupants; moderate-to-critical
  injury risk down 50% / 65% respectively.
- Ejection 2022: 1% of belted occupants in fatal crashes were totally ejected vs 26% of
  unrestrained occupants; 83% of totally ejected occupants were killed.
- Self-report: 70% of drivers and 57% of passengers say they ALWAYS wear a belt; 3% of
  drivers and 6% of passengers say they rarely wear one.
- Top non-use reasons (30-50% of sometimes / rarely / never users): "I'm only driving a
  short distance," "I forget to put it on," "I'm traveling on low-speed roadways." Also
  cited: light traffic, hassle, wrinkled clothes, discomfort, low crash probability, peers
  unbelted, "don't like being told what to do."
- Lower-use groups: males, younger adults, rear-seat passengers, pickup-truck occupants,
  nighttime travelers, local/rural-road travelers, people engaging in alcohol/drug use
  and other risky behaviors. Older motorists and children use belts more.
- AAA strategies: Education, Encouragement, Enforcement, Equity (targeted, profile-based).
- SECONDARY ONLY (do not headline): NHTSA estimate that belts prevented 14,955 deaths in
  2022 and ~375,000 lives since 1975 (via secondary aggregators citing NHTSA; not verified
  against the primary TSF PDF this run).

## Original contribution (the article's own calculation)
Crude share-ratio, computed from the two cited shares, methodology stated in-article:
- Unbelted share of the driving population ≈ 9% (100 - 91). Unbelted share of occupant
  deaths ≈ 50% (NHTSA 2022 killed share). Per-person death-rate ratio ≈ (50/9) / (50/91)
  = 91/9 ≈ 10.1. In plain terms: an unbelted occupant dies at roughly 10 times the
  per-person rate of a belted occupant, before any controls.
- Cross-checks from the same sources: FARS-sample overrepresentation 28% vs 9% ≈ 3.1x;
  killed-vs-survived share ratio 50% vs 14% ≈ 3.6x. All three point the same direction
  with different magnitudes, which is itself the point: the gap is real, its exact size
  depends on which denominator you pick.
- Why the crude 10x is NOT the causal number: NHTSA's controlled effectiveness estimates
  (45% cars / 60% light trucks) already account for crash severity. The gap between 10x
  crude and ~2x controlled is the confounding the article must name, not hide.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)
The unbelted 9% are not 9% of drivers chosen at random. AAA's own profiles show they
concentrate exactly where everything else that kills also concentrates: impaired driving,
nighttime, rural roads, pickups, rear seats, risky behavior. A belt does not fix the
2 a.m. drunk rural crash; it only improves the odds inside it. Buckling everyone tomorrow
would not cut deaths in half, because the deaths and the non-use share common causes.
The honest claim is narrower: among people already in a fatal crash, the belt is the
difference between a 1% ejection rate and a 26% one.

## Limitations (dedicated accounting, not hedging)
- FARS captures fatal crashes only; it cannot speak to injury-only crashes, where belt
  effectiveness differs.
- Restraint use in FARS has unknown/missing codes; NHTSA's 50%/14% split is "based on
  known restraint use" only.
- The 72% FARS-sample figure covers all occupants; the 91% comparison figure covers adult
  front-seat occupants (NCSA 2025). The samples are not identical; the comparison is
  directional, not exact.
- Survey self-reports ("always," "rarely") carry social-desirability bias; "always" is a
  stricter bar than observed use at a single moment, which is why 70% "always" coexists
  with 91% observed use.
- The "past 30 days / fatal crash" 28% item is reported in AAA's wording; its survey
  construction is not fully unpacked in the summary, so the article quotes AAA rather
  than re-deriving it.
- The crude 10x ratio is an arithmetic illustration from aggregate shares, not a
  regression-controlled risk estimate; confounders listed above apply.

## Actionable takeaways (publishing gate)
- Buckle on every trip including short and low-speed ones: those are the two most-cited
  excuses, and short trips are where "forget" happens.
- Rear-seat passengers: belt use is lowest there (31% unbelted in FARS sample); drivers
  should treat an unbelted rear passenger as their own risk, not someone else's choice.
- Pickup occupants, nighttime and rural-road drivers, young men: the profiles AAA flags
  are the ones who should hear this, and they are the least likely to be reached by a
  generic campaign, which is why AAA pushes profile-targeted enforcement and outreach.
