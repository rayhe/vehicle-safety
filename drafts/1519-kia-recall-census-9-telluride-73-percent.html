Kia Filed Nine Recalls in 2026. The Telluride Was 73 Percent of Them.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 15, 2026

Nine times in 2026, Kia walked into NHTSA with a new defect. Add up the nine campaign populations and you get 775,093 vehicles.[1] Now check the box score, because the scoring is not spread across the roster. One nameplate, the Telluride, accounts for 568,715 of them. That is 73.4 percent of Kia’s entire recall year, carried by a single SUV, and the SUV in question is the one Kia sells to families who comparison-shopped safety ratings before they signed anything.

775,093
Campaign-vehicles across Kia’s nine 2026 NHTSA recalls. Populations overlap in principle, so this counts recall actions, not unique vehicles.

January opened the scoring with the cluster. Campaign 26V046: 42,677 vehicles across eleven models, K4 through Carnival, with instrument panel software that can blank the speedometer, tire pressure, and warning lights while the car is moving.[11] February added the first Telluride entry, 26V105: 85,515 2025 Tellurides with power front seats, where a recliner built wrong by supplier DAS North America means the seatback may not hold the occupant in a rear impact.[2] March brought two in thirteen days, both on the brand-new second-generation 2027 Telluride. Campaign 26V135: 14,870 vehicles, a third-row center seat belt anchor buckle that may not latch, built by Samsong Mexico.[6] Campaign 26V173: 568 Telluride Hybrids in the two most expensive trims, where the second-row power seats may not detect a person and keep moving during the one-touch tilt-and-slide, a defect family shared with Hyundai’s Palisade recall.[3] April brought the fuel leak, 26V232: 141,032 Carnivals, model years 2022 through 2026, with crossover fuel pipe nuts that were never torqued to specification.[7]

June brought the second belt failure on the same new Telluride. Campaign 26V356: 6,264 2027 Tellurides and Telluride Hybrids, where the driver’s seat belt emergency locking retractor may lock from an incorrect sensor, so the strap will not extend at all, which is regulator-speak for the driver’s seat belt does not exist.[1] July brought the two biggest stories of the year, forty-eight hours apart. Campaign 26V430: 462,869 Tellurides from model years 2020 through 2024, the front power seat motor that can overheat and catch fire, the same 462,869 vehicles Kia already recalled for the same defect in 2024.[5] Campaign 26V431: eight vehicles, seven EV6s and one EV9, with high-voltage battery cells whose electrodes may be misaligned, which can short and catch fire.[10] August closed the year’s filing with 26V545: 21,290 Georgia-built EV9s, where a misrouted tube under the front passenger seat can keep the airbag from switching off for a child.[4]

Shares first, because shares are the story: the seat-motor re-recall, 59.7 percent of the year’s total; the Carnival fuel leak, 18.2 percent; the Telluride seatback recliner, 11.0 percent; the blank cluster, 5.5 percent; the EV9 airbag sensor, 2.7 percent; the belt anchor buckle, 1.9 percent; the driver belt retractor, 0.8 percent; the power second-row seats, 0.07 percent; and the battery, eight vehicles, 0.001 percent. One campaign is bigger than the other eight combined, 462,869 against 312,224, and it is a re-recall. Strip it out and the year still totals 312,224 across eight campaigns, which is a respectable season on its own. Keep it in and the headline writes itself, because the year’s biggest number is a 2024 fix that did not hold.

Now the Telluride, because the Telluride is the story inside the story. Five campaigns touch it: the 2025 seatback recliner, the 2027 belt anchor, the 2027 hybrid power seats, the 2027 driver belt retractor, and the 2020 through 2024 seat motor. Five campaigns, 568,715 vehicles, 73.4 percent of the brand. Narrow the lens to the brand-new second-generation 2027 model and the picture gets sharper: three campaigns, 20,331 vehicles, all of them about the seat or the belt, all filed before most owners had their first oil change. A family hauler whose restraint systems are failing at every attachment point, simultaneously, on the newest metal in the lineup, is not a coincidence. It is a supplier and design-review problem wearing a seat belt.

Zoom out one more level and the pattern holds across the whole brand. Six of the nine campaigns are about the restraint system: the seatback that may not hold you, the anchor buckle that may not latch, the power seats that may not detect you, the retractor that may not extend, the seat motor that may catch fire, and the sensor that may not switch the airbag off for a child. Together they cover 591,376 campaign-vehicles, 76.3 percent of the year’s total. Three of the nine are fire risks, the fuel leak, the seat motor, and the battery, and two of those carry park-outside advisories. Kia’s 2026 is not a random scatter of defects across a lineup. It is a restraint-system year with a fuel leak and a battery fire riding along.

And then there is the 1 percent club, which is where the counting gets honest. Four of these campaigns carry Kia’s own estimate that roughly 1 percent of the recalled vehicles actually carry the defect, and the EV9 airbag sensor carries a 6 percent estimate, about 1,277 vehicles. Add the estimates together and you get roughly 8,810 actually-defective vehicles hiding inside 775,093 recalled ones. That is the real scoreboard. Kia mailed three-quarters of a million letters to find fewer than nine thousand broken cars, and it had to, because nobody can tell which 1 percent is which without looking. Anybody who quotes the 775,093 as if it were the number of dangerous Kias on the road is either innumerate or selling something.

Two corrections to the tally, because a census that does not correct itself is just a press release with arithmetic. First, our own site counted Kia at four campaigns on November 12, using the 2026-model-year method, and that count missed five campaigns, including the year’s biggest number. A base-name-only census prints eight, not nine, because campaign 26V173 hides under the name “Telluride Hybrid” and never appears in a plain “Telluride” query. Second, one of the nine “2026 recalls” is not a new defect at all. Campaign 26V430 replaces 24V407 from June 2024, same 462,869 vehicles, same seat motor, same fire risk, because the 2024 repair, a reinforcing bracket and a new slide knob, did not hold: Kia’s own chronology counts 18 incidents after the fix, seven fires and eleven melted motors. So the count of genuinely new 2026 campaigns is eight, not nine, and the year’s biggest number is a two-year-old problem that outlived its first fix. Meanwhile, one supplier keeps showing up in the small print. Samsong Mexico built the anchor buckle that will not latch in March and installed the wrong sensor in the driver belt retractor in June, two belt failures, three months apart, on the same new Telluride.

One more link in the chain deserves its own paragraph, because it is the only campaign in Kia’s year that touches a fatality, and it does not touch it directly. Campaign 26V173 shares its defect family with Hyundai’s Palisade power-seat recall, 26V160, which is tied to the death of a two-year-old in Ohio on March 7, 2026, when a power seat kept moving after contacting the child. Kia’s version covers 568 cars, the two most expensive Telluride Hybrid trims, with no incidents known to Kia, and a stop-sale while the software fix was finished. Keep the line bright: the death belongs to Hyundai’s campaign, not Kia’s. But the two campaigns share a platform, a supplier ecosystem, and a failure mode, which is that a power seat cannot tell the difference between an empty seat and a small person.

Fairness requires the other side of the ledger, stated at full strength. Strip the re-recall and Kia’s genuinely-new 2026 total is 312,224 across eight campaigns, and the Telluride share drops to about a third. Four campaigns estimate that roughly 1 percent of the recalled vehicles actually carry the defect, so the real defective population is around 8,810 cars, not 775,093. An eight-car battery recall is a rounding error with a park-outside advisory attached to it. No deaths or injuries are tied to any of the nine campaigns in the filings, and the Palisade death is Hyundai’s, not Kia’s. Recalls are the safety system working, not failing: Kia found these defects, filed them, and pays for the repairs, which is precisely what the law asks a manufacturer to do. Two of the nine are software updates with no parts to replace, and every fix is free at the dealer. A reader who walks away thinking Kia builds dangerous cars has misread the data. That said, a seat-motor fix that fails and has to be recalled again is not the system working, and a brand-new Telluride recalled three times for its seats and belts before its first oil change is not a rounding error either. Both of those sentences are true at the same time.

If you own a 2020 through 2024 Telluride, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for 26V430, Kia SC374, and park outside and away from structures until the electronic fuse assembly is installed. If you already had the 2024 fix, you are in this one too, and the old fix is the reason for the new recall.[5] If you own a 2025 Telluride with power front seats, check for 26V105, Kia SC362: a loose seatback or a grinding noise when you recline is the warning sign.[2] If you own a 2027 Telluride or Telluride Hybrid, check for all three, 26V135 (SC364, third-row center belt anchor), 26V356 (SC372, driver belt retractor), and 26V173 (SC366, second-row power seats, hybrid SX Prestige trims only), and before your next drive pull the driver’s belt all the way out, because if it locks and will not extend, the belt is not a belt.[6][3] If you own a 2022 through 2026 Carnival, check for 26V232, Kia SC368: a fuel smell inside or outside the van is the warning sign.[7] If you own a 2025 or 2026 EV9 built in Georgia, check for 26V545, Kia SC379, and do not put a child or a child seat in the front passenger seat until the occupant detection mat is inspected.[4] If you own a 2022 through 2024 EV6 or a 2024 EV9, check for 26V431, Kia SC375: only eight vehicles are in it, but if yours is one, park outside and cap charging at 80 percent until the battery assembly is replaced.[10] If you own a 2025 K4, a 2025 or 2026 Sorento, a 2026 Sportage, a K5, an EV9, or a Carnival, check for 26V046, Kia SC361: the cluster software update is free and can arrive over the air, and if the screen blanks, the car still drives, so pull over rather than guessing your speed.[11] Buying a used Kia? Confirm every open recall was completed, ask for the service record instead of taking the seller’s word, and remember that a Telluride that had the 2024 seat-motor fix still needs the 2026 one. The second fix is the one that counts.
