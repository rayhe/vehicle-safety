Jaywalking Didn't Kill 7,080 Pedestrians. Drivers Who Didn't Yield Did.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 28, 2026

October is Pedestrian Safety Month, which means another round of lectures about looking both ways. NHTSA's final 2024 data tells a different story, and it is not about pedestrians at all. Failure to yield right of way shows up in 3,690 pedestrian deaths, 52.1% of the 7,080 total. Improper crossing, the behavior jaywalking laws exist to punish, shows up in 1,484. One of these numbers is the crisis, and it is not the one cities write tickets for.

How often failure to yield appears vs. improper crossing in fatal pedestrian crashes

Do the division yourself. 3,690 divided by 1,484 is 2.49. Add darting into the road (696) to improper crossing and the pedestrian-behavior buckets still trail failure to yield by 1,510 deaths. Even if every jaywalker in America had stayed on the sidewalk in 2024, the majority factor in pedestrian deaths would have been untouched. That is not a rounding error; it is the story upside down, and the inversion matters because enforcement budgets keep following the smaller number while the bigger one keeps killing people at a rate of ten a day.

Now the caveat, stated at full strength, because this table deserves it. FARS related factors are police-reported at the scene, not court findings. A failure-to-yield code describes a crash sequence, not a verdict. A pedestrian can dart out mid-block, in dark clothing, and still be coded failure-to-yield if the driver did not stop, which is exactly why the table measures sequences rather than blame and why both the jaywalking-first story and its mirror image deserve the same skepticism before anyone turns a factor code into a policy. The buckets overlap, which is why the percentages sum past 100%, and 19.0% of cases carry no coded factor at all. All of that is true, and none of it rescues the jaywalking-first story, because the same police reports that code pedestrian behavior also code driver behavior, and on that shared record, drivers not yielding dominate.

The rest of the table sharpens the point rather than softening it. Not visible, dark clothing or no lighting, accounts for 977 deaths, the third-largest factor. A pedestrian can be exactly where the law wants them, in a crosswalk, and still be coded not-visible. Meanwhile 72.1% of pedestrian deaths happen away from intersections, and 47.3% happen between 6 p.m. and midnight, which is what darkness plus commuting plus drinking hours looks like in a table. In single-vehicle pedestrian crashes, light trucks killed 2,990 people against 1,988 for passenger cars, and the pattern underneath all of it is bigger vehicles, darker hours, drivers not yielding. Pick any column and the pedestrian's crossing location is rarely the sharpest edge. Read the table.

None of this makes jaywalking safe, and nobody serious claims crossing a highway at night is wise. But policy follows the biggest bucket or it follows vibes, and right now it follows vibes. Cities that spent 2024 ticketing pedestrians were aiming at 21.0% of the problem while the 52.1% bucket drove past them. Yield enforcement, lighting, and slower speeds after dark target the factors that actually dominate the table. Jaywalking crackdowns target the factor that lets everyone feel like the dead person did something wrong.

What to do with this, concretely. Drivers: treat every unmarked crossing as a yield zone, and double that vigilance after dark, when nearly half of pedestrian deaths happen, because the difference between a pedestrian who makes it home and one who becomes a row in the FARS table is usually a driver deciding, in about two seconds, whether to lift off the accelerator. Pedestrians: conspicuity helps, since 13.8% of deaths involve not being visible, but no reflective vest fixes a driver who never looked. Buyers: pedestrian-detecting automatic braking cut pedestrian crash rates 27% in an IIHS study, so check whether a used car's AEB detects people, not just other cars, before assuming the badge means anything. And cities: if the enforcement budget only covers one campaign, the factor table has already voted, 3,690 to 1,484, and the margin is not close enough to argue about.
