Toyota Found a Crash Defect Without a Single Crash

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
Oct 30, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. In a hard frontal crash, that is when the seatbelt pretensioner fires, yanking the belt tight before your body has finished realizing the car stopped. On 228,367 Toyota Sienna Hybrids, that same explosion of precisely engineered violence can shake the second-row seatback right out of its locked position.[1] The recall is real, and the crashes it would prevent have never happened.

228,367
Sienna Hybrids recalled for a defect nobody has ever seen in the real world

The defect lives in the outboard recliner of the second-row seat. It locks with interlocking teeth, a cam and pawls that click into engagement the way a ratchet does. When the pretensioner fires, the violence does not just travel down the belt; it rings the whole seat frame, and in certain combinations of crash type, seatback angle, and deployment timing, the cam and pawls start oscillating against each other. The oscillation walks the cam around until the cam walks out and the recliner unlocks — no overload, no broken part. A timing problem wearing a mechanical failure’s clothes.[2]

Every automaker that waits for bodies should feel embarrassed by how Toyota found this. From January to November 2025, engineers were sled-testing a modified seatbelt and second-row seat assembly for a future design when the rear seatback’s recliner kept disengaging. A formal investigation ran from December 2025 through August 2026: more sled tests, internal friction implicated, and field recoveries where Toyota pulled seat assemblies out of real Siennas to check their condition. Crash tests in September and October validated the sled findings. Then, on October 27, Toyota voluntarily recalled 2022 through 2026 Siennas fitted with pretensioners from one supplier — vans built from September 2021 through July 2026, twenty-one months of lab work ending in zero field reports, zero injuries, and zero warranty claims.

The remedy is a software update to the supplemental restraint system ECU, retiming the pretensioner so its firing moment no longer excites the oscillation. About 200 vans with the Mobility as a Service package get new seatbacks with a different recliner; everyone else gets a flash.[3] Credit where it is due: catching your own defect in a lab and recalling a quarter-million vans anyway is the safety system working. But a latch that can be walked out of engagement by vibration is a latch whose safety depends on timing, and for 99.9 percent of these vans the latch geometry is not changing. The software moves the resonance; it does not redesign the teeth. Owner letters go out December 7, and the repair is free.[1]

For perspective, the Sienna’s FARS fatality rate is 0.49 deaths per 100 million vehicle-miles — mid-pack among minivans, between the Pacifica’s 0.19 and the Odyssey’s 0.93, in a vehicle class that is already among the safest on the road.[4] This recall is about tail risk, and the honest math says it might prevent zero injuries over the life of the fleet: no combination of crash, seatback angle, and pretensioner timing in the real world has ever produced this failure. That is the strongest case against this article, and it deserves to be stated at full strength — this could be a lab artifact of modified test hardware, and the recall could be a very expensive abundance of caution. But the abundance-of-caution reading is also the point. Recalls usually begin at a morgue; this one began at a sled rig, which is what good looks like.

What to do: Own a 2022–2026 Sienna Hybrid? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls; you do not have to wait for the December 7 letter. Until the remedy, keep second-row seatbacks upright and locked, because the defect needs the recliner in play, and buckle the kids anyway. If you are shopping a used Sienna, verify the recall status and ask for the SRS service record rather than just a VIN check, because this fix is a software version. And if your automaker ever recalls something it found in a lab instead of a morgue, that is the recall system working. Say so.

What this didn’t prove: This desk has not independently read Toyota’s Part 573 filing; the defect description and chronology come through press reporting of the NHTSA documents, with figures consistent across outlets. Public reporting does not name the pretensioner supplier. How far the SRS retimes the pretensioner is not public. The roughly 200 Mobility as a Service seatback replacements is a press-reported figure. The 31 percent fleet share (228,367 recalled of an estimated 743,750-van fleet) uses FARS estimated fleet figures with the dataset’s own estimation uncertainty.
