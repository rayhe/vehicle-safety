The Radar Thinks the Truck Is Further Left Than It Is. 6,185 Semis Brake for Ghosts.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 20, 2026

A semi truck does not know where it is. Not the GPS kind of knowing. The radar kind. PACCAR filed recall 26V744 with NHTSA on November 11, 2026, covering 6,185 model-year 2027 Kenworth and Peterbilt tractors whose forward-looking radar can mistake stopped or slow-moving traffic in the left lane for a vehicle directly ahead, and brake for it.[1] The truck is not hallucinating a car. It is misplacing itself.

6,185
Model-year 2027 Kenworth and Peterbilt tractors recalled for a radar that tracks the wrong lane.

Here is the mechanism, straight from the filing. A parameter calculator produces a software data set for the radar, and that data set is not written correctly into the radar's memory.[2] When the radar never learns the truck's real parameters, it defaults to a lateral position too far left of where the truck actually sits. Picture the radar's mental map shifted half a lane sideways: every vehicle in the left lane now looks like it is directly in front of the truck, and the truck's collision system does exactly what it was built to do. It brakes. The defect is not a failed sensor. It is a localization error in the one function the system exists to perform.

Two models carry almost the whole recall. The Kenworth T680 accounts for 2,790 trucks and the Peterbilt 579 for 2,708, together 5,498 of the 6,185, or 89 percent.[3] The rest split across the T880, W990, 567, and 589. All were built between February 11 and October 8, 2026, and the recall population is defined by the Bendix radar part numbers shipped to PACCAR, minus the trucks corrected while still at the plants.[2] That matters, because it means the defect is present by construction. Sixteen reports reached PACCAR by November 4, eight warranty claims and eight field reports, with no injuries, but sixteen is a report rate, not an occurrence rate.[2] Most phantom-brake events never get reported. The trigger is not wear or age. The trigger is slow traffic in the left lane.

Now the part that should bother you more than the defect. PACCAR's safety team learned of the field complaints on October 16 and held trucks at its plants the same day. Corrected software was available October 19 for trucks still at the plants and in production.[2] The safety committee decided to recall on November 4. Owner letters and NHTSA VIN searchability do not arrive until January 10, 2027, 67 days after the decision and 83 days after the fix existed.[3] For two months, 6,185 trucks drive with a known phantom-brake defect, and no owner can confirm by VIN whether their truck is in it, because the database has not loaded the campaign yet. A "0 recalls" result before January 10 means the campaign is not in the system, not that the truck is clear. Fleets have to go by build date and model, and the only way to get the fix sooner is to call the dealer and cite campaign 26PACL.

This is also the fifth PACCAR recall on 2027 trucks since July, by FreightWaves' count of NHTSA records.[2] The 2027 Kenworth and Peterbilt launch has now produced recalls for an oil-contaminated power distribution center, a seat ventilation harness that can short-circuit, bunk restraint webbing that was never secured, a driveline snap ring, and now a radar that cannot find its own lane. One recall is a defect. Five in four months is a launch. And the radar one lands in the middle of a larger argument NHTSA has not finished having with itself.

That argument is the one this site covered in September. NHTSA is writing a rule to mandate automatic emergency braking on every new heavy truck in America, projecting it would prevent 19,118 rear-end crashes and save 155 lives a year.[4] Meanwhile the agency's own Office of Defects Investigation has an open engineering analysis, EA25006, into false AEB activations on heavy trucks, with 347 reports reviewed, 24 crashes, 13 injuries, and one fatality.[5] The mandate's benefit math counts only crashes where the truck is the striking vehicle. A phantom-braking event makes the truck the struck vehicle, a stopped obstacle in a live lane, and that crash category appears nowhere in the 155-lives projection. Now a manufacturer has recalled trucks for the exact failure mode the probe is studying, in the exact vehicle class the mandate will cover, with a Bendix-supplied radar. The two stories are not the same investigation. They are the same failure, wearing two different case numbers.

Grant the other side its full weight, because it is heavy. Sixteen reports, zero injuries, a software fix ready sixteen days before the recall decision, and a manufacturer that quarantined trucks the day it learned of the problem. A radar that brakes for a perceived threat is doing what automatic emergency braking was designed to do; the error is a calibration parameter, fixed with a download. The mandate math still favors the mandate by orders of magnitude: 19,118 crashes prevented a year against 24 induced in the probe data. Phantom braking is a known, rare failure mode of a technology that prevents far more crashes than it causes. What that defense cannot answer is the gap in the math itself. NHTSA has never published an induced-harm estimate, so the "155 lives" figure is gross, not net. And a safety system that cannot tell which lane it occupies is not a calibration footnote. It is a localization failure in the exact function the mandate will require every new truck to have.

There are limits to what this recall proves. No crashes or injuries are attributed to 26V744 in any verified source. The specific Bendix system name is not in the public filing summaries, so this article does not name it. EA25006 covers 2017-2022 Daimler trucks, not 2027 PACCAR trucks, and the link between them is the shared failure mode and the shared regulatory context, not a shared investigation. The 0.26 percent report rate is a floor, not an occurrence rate, and the true rate is unknown and higher. What the recall does prove is narrower and worse: the failure mode NHTSA is still investigating as a possible defect is already shipping, already braking, and already being recalled, while the rule that would mandate it on every new truck is still being written.

If you run 2027 Kenworth T680, T880, W990 or Peterbilt 567, 579, 589 tractors built between February 11 and October 8, 2026, brief your drivers that the truck may brake for slow or stopped traffic in the left lane, and think about the vehicles behind you, because a phantom stop turns your truck into the obstacle.[1] Call Peterbilt at 1-940-591-4220 or Kenworth at 1-425-828-5888, cite campaign 26PACL or NHTSA 26V744, and book the software update. Do not wait for the letter. If your truck brakes with nothing ahead of it, file the event with NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation, because the 347 reports in EA25006 are the investigation's data supply, and unreported events do not exist to the regulator.[5] And if you are buying a 2027 PACCAR truck, ask whether the 26PACL radar update has been applied before you take delivery, and ask which radar generation the truck ships with. The radar that cannot find its own lane is not the one you want watching the road.
