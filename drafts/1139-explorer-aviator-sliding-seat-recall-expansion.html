Ford Recalled 387,911 Explorers and Aviators Because the Back Seats Can Tip While Driving. Its First Fix Failed.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

Before you hand the keys to your teenager, check the second-row seats in your Explorer. Ford just recalled 387,911 Explorers and Lincoln Aviators because the easy-entry second-row seats can unlatch, tip forward, or slide while the vehicle is moving.[1] This is the second recall for the same defect, and the first one, issued thirteen months earlier, did not fix it.[1]

387,911
2020-2026 Ford Explorers and 2020-2027 Lincoln Aviators covered by the expanded recall, NHTSA campaign 26V-451.

The villain here is a button. Each second-row outboard seat has an easy-entry switch that folds and slides the seat for third-row access, and the switch sits inside a decorative plastic bezel. Ford found that the bezel can shift out of alignment during ordinary use, and when it does, the button binds or stays stuck in the pressed position while the seat releases from its floor latch without being asked.[5] A piece of trim is now the weakest link in the chain that is supposed to hold your seat down in a crash.

In June 2025, the first recall blamed incorrectly installed bezels from a seat supplier, instructed dealers to inspect and replace damaged components, added production checks, and declared the problem contained.[1] Then spring 2026 brought new field reports: some from vehicles built after the supposed cutoff, four from vehicles that had already received the repair.[2] By June 16, Ford had logged 14 reports of unintended seat movement; six had been through the original fix, and eight were never in the recall population to begin with.[2] The bezel design that started it all was finally pulled from production on June 24, 2026, more than a year after the first recall.[2]

Run the arithmetic. Fourteen reports across 387,911 vehicles is 3.6 per 100,000, about 0.0036 percent, and the absolute risk is tiny. NHTSA's official consequence line is not: a seat that moves unexpectedly "may not properly restrain an occupant during a crash."[1] And here is the part Ford would rather you skim. Interim letters went out August 19, 2026, but the final remedy is not expected until January 2027, which is roughly five months of knowing your seat might come loose and having no fix to book.[1] For perspective, the 2026 Explorer carries 15 recalls, the most of any SUV this year, according to RecallChecks data compiled by Road Ethos.[6]

Fairness demands the other side, and it is strong. Nobody has been hurt.[3] Fourteen reports over nearly 388,000 vehicles is a rounding error, and the seat only unlatches after the easy-entry switch binds, which mostly happens when you press the button to reach the third row, not while the car is cruising down the freeway. Interim-then-final campaigns are standard NHTSA practice when a redesigned part has to be tooled and shipped. Most owners will never touch that button at speed. All true. But a seat that cannot be trusted to stay put is not a wear item, and you cannot inspect a bezel alignment in your driveway and know your car is the safe one.

So run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today instead of waiting for the January letter.[7] If you had the 2025 repair done, you are still in scope, so check again, because the fix you already received was one of the things that failed. Watch for the tells: a button that sticks or stays depressed, a seat that will not click back into its locked position after you fold it. And every time you use easy-entry, confirm the seat latches firmly before you drive. The failure mode is an unlatched seat, and your thumb is the final inspector. Children belong in the second row of a family SUV, but only on a seat that is locked where it belongs.
