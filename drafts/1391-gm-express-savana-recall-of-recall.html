GM Recalled the Vans. Then It Recalled the Recall

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 4, 2026

General Motors recalled the same vans twice for the same loose nut, and the second recall exists because the first recall's inspections were done wrong.[1] On October 29, 2026, GM filed campaign 26V718 covering 12,715 model-year 2025-2026 Chevrolet Express and GMC Savana vans.[1] At issue is a steering gear nut that was never properly tightened, a failure that can separate while driving and take steering control with it.[1]

12,715
Vans recalled a second time for the same loose steering gear nut, because the first recall's inspections were not done properly

June's recall, 26V399, covered 26,541 of these vans.[2] Supplier Nexteer Automotive's faulty sensor let 51 steering gear assemblies leave the factory with nuts tightened by hand, not torqued to spec.[3] GM knew the 51 serial numbers but could not say which vans got them, so every van in the population needed its serial number checked.[3] That inspection was supposed to end it. Instead, NHTSA's summary for 26V718 states plainly that vehicles not properly inspected under 26V399 will need re-inspection under 26V718.[1] Somebody audited the inspections. They failed. Now owners who complied in the summer are recalled again for a nut they were already told was fine, a clearance that lasted barely four months before the same agency file that issued it demanded it back.

Do the math. 12,715 is 47.9 percent of the June population, which means nearly half the vans from round one are back for round two. GM estimates only 1 percent of the round-two vans, roughly 127 vehicles, actually carry a loose nut.[4] Everyone else is there for paperwork, which beats the alternative: driving a van pronounced fine by an inspection that was not. And this is the third steering recall these vans have collected in 2026 alone, after May's 26V345 covered 232 vans whose gear selectors could falsely signal Park.[2] Three steering defects, one supplier family, one calendar year, and one van platform GM has built, with updates, since 1996, which means the oldest vehicle GM still sells new has now produced a steering recall in May, another in June, and a recall of that recall in October, all without a single reported crash to force any of it.[3]

Fairness demands the other side of the ledger. No crash or injury appears in either campaign's federal file.[1][2] A manufacturer that re-recalls its own recall is confessing to a failure it could have buried at the next oil change, and a serial-number inspection is cheap, with replacement free when the serial matches.[1] Still, the federal summary never says how many inspections were improper or why, whether dealer error, a bad procedure, bad paperwork, or a supplier record that never matched the vans on the lot, and that gap matters because 47.9 percent is a population ratio, not a confirmed botched-inspection rate, and because every owner in the round-two population is left guessing whether their van was mis-inspected, never inspected, or inspected correctly and swept into the net anyway. GM's 1 percent defect figure is an estimate, not a measurement, and the FARS death counts for these nameplates span all model years and cannot be pinned on the recalled vans.[6]

If you own a 2025 or 2026 Express or Savana, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today, even if your van already passed its June inspection.[5] A June clearance is now stale paper. Dealers will inspect the steering gear serial number and replace the assembly if necessary, free, but owner letters are not expected until December 14.[1] VINs have been searchable since October 29, so waiting for the letter means waiting 46 days to learn something you can learn tonight. And if you are buying one of these vans used, ask one question: was it re-checked under 26V718, not merely inspected under 26V399? Only the second answer is current.
