The Headlight Complaint Boom vs. the Crash Data. The Crash Data Wins.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 8, 2026

Two days ago, NHTSA announced it will rewrite the federal headlight standard for the first time since 1997.[1] The current rules predate the iMac, the DVD, and, in spirit, the modern headlight entirely. On paper, this is the kind of thing your humble correspondent covers with confetti.

Then the numbers walked in, wearing the smirk.

0.1–0.2%
Share of nighttime crashes where police coded headlight glare as a factor, across 24 million crash reports from 11 states. Most "glare" crashes happened in daylight.[2]

IIHS ran the largest glare study ever done, covering 2015 to 2023, and found the glare crash rate essentially flat for a decade while headlights got dramatically brighter.[2] Meanwhile, complaints about blinding LEDs have exploded to the point that 70,000 people signed a federal petition to "properly regulate" them,[6] Britain's transport department found 96 percent of respondents think some headlights are too bright, and India is weighing a legal cap on headlight color temperature.[4]

So the complaints are up, the measurable glare is flat, and most glare-coded crashes happened in daylight with the sun low on the horizon. Headlights are catching blame for what a star does.[2]

Here is the part the petition-signers don't want to hear. While they were being dazzled, the brighter headlights were saving lives. Vehicles with IIHS Good-rated headlights have roughly 19 percent fewer nighttime single-vehicle crashes and 23 percent fewer nighttime pedestrian crashes than vehicles with Poor-rated ones.[3] Night driving is about three times as risky as daytime driving, so that 19 percent is real bodies, not rounding.[3]

The institute offers the cruelest little explanation in road safety this year: one car's headlights help exactly one driver see, while adding perceived glare to everyone else.[4] The beneficiaries are invisible, the annoyed are legion, and satisfaction doesn't sign petitions, which is why the ledger looks so lopsided.

Even in the lab, actual excessive glare fell from 21 percent of tested headlights in 2017 to 3 percent in 2025, while IIHS ratings went from one Good system out of 80-plus in 2016 to about half of 2025 models.[4] Complaint volume and measured glare are not just uncorrelated; they moved in opposite directions.

None of this means the dazzle is imaginary. Drivers over 70 are the most affected, and the people who quietly stopped driving at night because of it never appear in crash statistics.[4] NHTSA itself admits it has no quantified relationship between glare dosage and crash risk.[4] Police coding of glare is subjective, which means 0.1 percent is a floor, not a ceiling. Discomfort is a real welfare loss even when it doesn't kill.

Still. This rewrite is overdue, for the opposite reason most people think. "Today's standards do not allow automakers to deploy the latest safety-improving lighting technologies," NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison said.[1] Translation: the 1997 rules are blocking adaptive beams and smarter lighting that would help you see better. The thing killing people at night isn't other people's headlights; it's the dark.

What to do: Shop the IIHS headlight rating like you shop the crash rating. Vehicles with Good-rated lights have ~19% fewer nighttime single-vehicle and ~23% fewer nighttime pedestrian crashes than Poor-rated ones; check your car or the one you're buying at iihs.org/ratings. Turn on automatic high beams if you have them, they're exactly what the new rules want more of. If you drive a tall truck or SUV, your lamps sit at car-driver eye level, which makes the glare geometric. Not personal.
