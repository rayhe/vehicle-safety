November’s Smallest Recalls: 1,539 Vehicles, Zero Crashes, One Loose Nut

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 16, 2026

On September 22, 2026, a torque wrench at Ford’s Michigan Assembly Plant returned a number that did not look right. One low residual-torque reading on a nut nobody thinks about, the nut that holds the driver-side wiper arm to its pivot shaft, turned into a federal recall of 330 Ford Broncos, every one built across two days in September, because that nut was not tight enough.[1]

1,539
Vehicles covered by November 2026’s three smallest NHTSA recall campaigns combined, with zero crashes, zero injuries, and zero consumer complaints.

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a recall, because these three campaigns are the rare kind where nothing bad happened first. Ford’s Part 573 report for campaign 26V725 lists the estimated percentage of the recalled population carrying the defect as 100 percent, not 1 percent, which is the usual manufacturer estimate, but all of them. Two days of production, September 21 and 22, 2026, and every Bronco built in that window goes back for a wiper arm nut. As of October 12, Ford counted zero warranty claims, zero field reports, zero customer reports, and zero Vehicle Owner Questionnaires related to the problem. Nobody complained because nobody knew. A plant audit found it, Ford’s Critical Concern Review Group opened the case on October 8, and the Field Review Committee approved the recall on October 27, thirty-five days after the wrench flagged the reading.

Smallest of the three is the Mack, and it is my favorite kind of defect: pure software telling a lie. One hundred fifty-one medium-duty electric trucks, model years 2024 through 2026, left the factory with a hill start assist message enabled that should never have been switched on. A driver on an incline sees a message saying the truck will hold, trusts it, and the truck rolls back, though no crash has been reported. Fix is a dashboard software update plus a correction pasted into the owner’s manual, which tells you everything about how seriously the industry takes a wrong line of code versus a wrong torque value, and owner letters go out December 28.

Autocar’s entry is the oldest-fashioned failure of the bunch: a glue joint. One thousand fifty-eight Legend vocational trucks, model years 2020 through 2026, carry an exterior mirror held on by an adhesive bond that can degrade until the whole assembly falls off, cutting the driver’s visibility and dropping a mirror into traffic as a road hazard. About 151 trucks per model year, which is a rounding error in a vocational fleet, and yet somebody at Autocar inspected the joint, found the failure mode, and filed the paperwork. Dealers reinforce or replace the mirrors free of charge, and letters go out December 21.

Scale is the point, so add all three campaigns together and you get 1,539 vehicles, which is 0.21 percent of BMW’s 720,796-vehicle water-pump recall filed earlier the same month, a recall-of-recall with no remedy ready and a park-outside advisory. BMW owners are parking in the driveway while the company invents a second fix for a defect it already fixed in 2024. Bronco, Mack, and Autocar owners are getting letters about problems found before anyone got hurt, which is the version of the recall system that actually works, and it is worth noticing precisely because it is so quiet.

Fair hearing, because the other side has one. A 330-vehicle recall costs Ford almost nothing, and the audit only happened because a torque reading looked wrong on a random check, not because Ford runs a torque audit on every nut on every car. Ford’s own report shows the concern sat in review for five weeks between discovery and approval. Tiny recalls are cheap to file and cheap to fix, which means manufacturers have every incentive to file them promptly and none of the disincentive that slows down the big ones. Do not mistake a cheap recall for a working system. Mistake it for a system that works when the fix is cheap.

Honest limits, because this census has them. It covers campaigns that surfaced in NHTSA’s public records between November 1 and 13, 2026, and a smaller campaign filed late in the month could still appear. Only Ford’s Part 573 report states zero accidents and injuries explicitly; the Mack and Autocar letters list no crash figures at all, so the zero for those two is an inference from the absence of any complaint-driven investigation, not a stated number. A 100 percent defect rate on 330 Broncos sounds catastrophic until you remember the failure needs rain, or at least a driver who turns the wipers on, to matter at all. And none of these three has a completion rate yet, because two of the three owner-letter mailings have not even happened.

What to do: if you bought a 2026 Bronco built September 21 or 22, your wiper arm nut is on the list, so check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now; the fix is free and letters go out November 16 through 20. Mack MD Electric owners can check their VINs today, since they have been searchable since November 5, and the fix is software. Autocar Legend owners should watch for a letter in December. And if you ever get a recall notice with no complaints attached to it, do not assume it is a mistake. It usually means somebody inside the factory found the problem first, which is the best possible reason to get a letter from a car company.
