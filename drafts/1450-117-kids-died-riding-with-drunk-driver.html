The Drunk Driver Killed the Kid in His Own Back Seat

Dale Impactor III • Toxicology Desk Chief
November 9, 2026

Picture the drunk-driving victim and you will picture two people: the driver, and the stranger in the other lane. NHTSA's 2024 numbers say the most defenseless victim is a third person, sitting where you cannot see them. In 2024, 221 children aged 14 and younger died in alcohol-impaired-driving crashes. Of those, 117 were passengers in the drunk driver's own car.[1] One child every 3.1 days. They never picked the driver. They never got a vote.

117
Children killed as passengers of alcohol-impaired drivers in 2024, one every 3.1 days (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 815)

Break the 221 down and the shape of the problem changes. 117 were passengers of the impaired driver, 53 percent. 74 were occupants of other vehicles, 33 percent. 28 were pedestrians or cyclists, 13 percent. Two were child drivers, 1 percent.[1] That last number is the one to sit with: 219 of 221 children were killed by someone else's drinking. Only two were behind a wheel themselves.

Now the ratio that makes this a story instead of a statistic. Children are 2.6 percent of all traffic deaths, 1,032 of 39,254. But they are 7.9 percent of the passengers who died riding with an impaired driver, 117 of 1,483. Divide one share by the other and children are overrepresented among dead passengers of drunk drivers by a factor of 3.0.[1][2] For adults, riding with a drunk driver is 12 percent of the alcohol death toll. For children, it is 53 percent, 4.4 times higher. A child in a drunk driver's car cannot open the door at 70 miles per hour, cannot take the keys, cannot call a cab. Exposure is the whole story, and exposure is the one thing a child cannot control.

None of this is marginal drinking. 68 percent of alcohol-impaired fatalities in 2024 happened in crashes where at least one driver blew .15 or higher, nearly twice the legal limit.[1] Impairment among drivers in fatal crashes runs 31 percent at night against 10 percent in daylight, and 28 percent on weekends against 15 percent on weekdays. Drivers aged 21 to 24 post the highest impairment rate of any age group, 28 percent. Texas led the states with 1,676 alcohol-impaired deaths, followed by California at 1,311 and Florida at 769.[1] And while total traffic deaths fell 4.3 percent in 2024, child deaths rose 1 percent, from 1,023 to 1,032. The country got safer. Children went the other way.[2][3]

Fairness demands the other side, because the honest version of this story is narrower than the headline. 117 is a small number next to 11,904 alcohol deaths, and it arrived in a year when alcohol deaths fell 3.9 percent. FARS does not record the relationship between driver and child passenger, so the adult who was supposed to get them home is a description of the role, not a verified fact in each case. BAC figures are partly imputed, not measured, and an alcohol-impaired-driving crash means an impaired driver was involved, not that alcohol caused the crash. DUI law, child endangerment statutes, and ignition interlocks already cover this conduct in most states. There is no new statute hiding inside this table. What the table does is correct the mental image: the drunk-driving victim is not only the stranger in the other lane. Sometimes the victim is buckled into the back seat, and the driver is the only adult in the car.

So what do you do with it? If you are hosting and someone has been drinking, do not let them drive home with kids in the car. Take the keys, call a ride, offer a couch. If you are a passenger and the driver has been drinking, get out and take the kids with you. If you see a car weaving at night with small heads visible in the back, call 911: impairment in fatal crashes runs three times higher after dark. And if you are a parent, know that a car seat cuts fatal injury by 71 percent for infants and 54 percent for toddlers, which is real protection, but no restraint undoes a drunk driver.[2] Check your state's child endangerment enhancement for DUI with a child passenger before you need it. Then check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because a car with an open recall is a bad place to put a kid even with a sober driver.[4]
