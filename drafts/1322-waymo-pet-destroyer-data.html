Musk Called Waymo's Robotaxis "Pet Destroyers." The Federal File Says His Did It Too

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
Oct 29 2026

Elon Musk stood at Giga Texas on October 10 and called Waymo's robotaxis "pet destroyers."[1] He allowed that Waymo had maybe "made some improvements on that front," then announced that his own engineers had already solved the night problem: gray kittens on gray tarmac, low visibility, done deal. The federal crash file tells a different story: nine animal strikes from Waymo, one from Tesla, and seven of Waymo's nine happened after dark. That last part is the pattern nobody wrote up.

78%
of Waymo's reported animal strikes happened between 8 p.m. and 3 a.m.

Every company running automated driving systems on public roads files crash reports under NHTSA's Standing General Order.[2] In the current file, submissions from June 16, 2025 onward, last updated August 17, 2026, Waymo logged nine reports with the crash partner coded "Animal." Six involve a dog, a cat, or what the company calls a "domestic animal" in San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Austin, and in five of the six the animal was injured. The other three were wildlife or unidentified: two geese taken at 63 mph on a South San Francisco highway, an animal at 48 mph in Burlingame at 1:03 a.m., another at 49 mph outside Austin at 3:02 a.m.[1]

Read the narratives and a rhythm emerges. An animal darts from between parked cars or off a curb; the car brakes and steers; contact follows anyway. In a June 2026 report from San Francisco, timed 11:18 p.m., Waymo's car was doing 16 mph when a dog crossed from between two parked cars. The car slowed, steered left, and the dog still struck the right front corner, the leash ending up lodged under the front right tire.

One entry has a name: KitKat, a gray tabby who lived at Randa's Market on 16th Street in the Mission, who died in October 2025 around 11:40 p.m. when a Waymo car stopped at the curb for a passenger pickup began to pull away. The cat had come off the sidewalk and sat partially under the car; the form codes the vehicle as moving straight at 5 mph.[1] Waymo later said the cat ran under the car and donated to a local animal rights organization. A federal file, a donation, and a bodega without its cat.

Now, the punchline Musk skipped: Tesla filed one animal report in the same file, among 26 total Tesla reports: a 2026 Model Y with its automated system "verified engaged" struck a dog at an Austin intersection at 1:08 p.m. in September 2025, roughly three months after Tesla began robotaxi rides in the city. A safety monitor was aboard; no passengers.[1] Tesla originally redacted the narrative as confidential business information, then unredacted it in March 2026, and the account reads like Waymo's: the dog ran in from the right at a green light, the car slowed and steered left, the dog hit the bottom of the front bumper. Note the paperwork telling on itself: the form codes the car's pre-crash movement as "stopped" and its speed as 27 mph in the same report. Nobody at the federal level proofreads the confession.

The timing is worth noting, too: Musk's interview came one day after NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Defect Petition DP26010 on October 9, alleging that 2026 Tesla robotaxis, Model Y and Cybercab, exceed posted speed limits in active school zones.[3] The petition, backed by three video links, names an estimated population of 1,000 vehicles. A petition is a request, not an investigation; the agency has 120 days, roughly to January 21, 2027, to grant or deny it, which is probably why he picked that week to call his competitor a pet destroyer. Coincidence is a luxury defendants buy in bulk.

Here is the math nobody ran: Waymo says it has driven 271.3 million rider-only miles through June 2026.[1] Nine reported strikes across that distance is one per roughly 30 million miles, a back-of-envelope figure with misaligned denominators, so do not file it as a rate. For scale, American drivers kill on the order of a million animals every single day; against that carnage, nine robotaxi strikes in a year is not an epidemic, it is a rounding error that happens to be federally filed. The most striking fact in the data is how rare the events are, and how visible, because every one of them sits in a public database.

Which brings us to the counterargument, stated at full strength. Nine strikes, zero human injuries, against 271 million miles: this is noise, not a defect pattern. A human driver hits a deer and calls insurance; a robotaxi hits a cat and it lands in a NHTSA CSV. The Standing General Order exists to find defects, and nine curb incidents at city speeds are what urban driving looks like when the drivers are dogs. If anything, the file flatters the machines, since it captures contacts too small for any human driver to bother reporting. Take the whole "pet destroyer" frame and it collapses into a story about how thoroughly robotaxi failures are documented, which is the opposite of the accusation.

So what do you do with all of this? If you walk a dog near robotaxi routes after dark, keep it leashed at curbs and between parked cars, because that is where five of the six domestic-animal strikes began. If you are evaluating robotaxi safety claims, demand mileage-normalized rates instead of anecdote counts: a company reporting one strike out of 26 total reports is describing its paperwork, not its safety. And when a CEO tells you the night problem is solved, ask for the data, because the file is public and his evidence is not.

Limitations: The SGO file is not a census of robotaxi animal strikes; only crashes meeting damage, injury, or vulnerable-road-user thresholds are reportable, so low-speed contacts with no injury or damage never enter the file and the counts are a floor, not a rate. NHTSA collects no fleet-size or mileage data, so the 271.3 million rider-only miles figure (Waymo's self-reported total through June 2026) is a misaligned denominator for the nine strikes, not a true exposure base. Dates are reported as month and year only; times come from company-written narratives. The agency explicitly warns against comparing companies on these reports. The "million animals a day" figure is a widely cited wildlife estimate, not a federal statistic.
