# Research Notes — #1202: California Passed Four Tougher DUI Laws. It Killed the One Proven to Work.

## The hook (5 days old)

Sacramento, October 2026: Gov. Gavin Newsom signed four DUI bills in September that make drunk driving in California more punitive — a murder-charge warning for DUI defendants who plead down and kill again, felony charges for a third DUI within 10 years, more DMV violation points for intoxicated vehicular manslaughter, and longer license revocations for repeat offenders. Missing from the package: **AB 1830**, the bill that would have required every first-time DUI offender to install an ignition interlock. It passed the Assembly **74-0**, then died in the Senate Appropriations Committee, where chair **Sen. Sabrina Cervantes (D-Riverside)** tacked on a late amendment making interlocks optional for first-timers who hadn't injured anyone — letting them take a license suspension instead. Source: Jeff Horseman, The Press-Enterprise, via Police1, published Oct 12, 2026.

The political texture, all from that same reporting: Cervantes offered no public explanation for the amendment and her spokesperson didn't respond to comment requests. In 2025 Sacramento police cited Cervantes on suspicion of DUI after a crash near the Capitol; prosecutors declined to file charges after a blood test showed no drugs or alcohol, and she has sued the Sacramento Police Department alleging misconduct. Her sister, Riverside Councilmember Clarissa Cervantes, has two DUIs (most recent 2023) and entered rehab. Kellie Montalvo, a Corona mother who lost her son to a 2020 hit-and-run by a driver prosecutors said had been drinking and smoking marijuana, is Cervantes' constituent and asked for an explanation she never got.

## The numbers (all sourced)

1. **1,311** — alcohol-impaired-driving traffic fatalities in California, 2024 (FARS). Second-highest raw count in the nation after Texas (1,676). Source: NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts 2024: Alcohol-Impaired Driving, Table 4.
2. **11,904** — national alcohol-impaired deaths, 2024, ~30% of all traffic deaths; down 3.9% from 12,382 in 2023. Source: NHTSA / WRD News summary of the 2024 report.
3. **67%** — median reduction in re-arrest rates for alcohol-impaired driving while ignition interlocks are installed (13 studies, systematic review). Source: CDC Community Preventive Services Task Force, "Reducing Alcohol-Impaired Driving: Ignition Interlocks."
4. **5.7 million** — drunk-driving attempts blocked by interlocks since 2006; **336,000 in 2025 alone**, one every 95 seconds. Source: MADD ignition interlock report, June 25, 2026.
5. **34 states + DC** require interlocks for all drunk-driving offenders. California is in the bottom tier: judicial discretion only, one of 6 states (CA, IN, NV, ND, MT, SD). Source: MADD June 2026 report; legalclarity.org state breakdown citing NCSL.
6. **+17%** — drunk-driving deaths increased nationally 2019 to 2024. Source: MADD June 2026 report.
7. **74-0** — AB 1830's Assembly vote before the Senate amendment gutted it.
8. **2033** — California's extended IID pilot program now runs through Jan 1, 2033 (AB 366), but only mandates interlocks for repeat offenders and first-timers who cause injury crashes. First-time non-injury = judge's discretion or ≤6 months.

## Original contribution (not just synthesis)

- The **penalty-vs-prevention inversion**: four punitive measures signed, the one evidence-based measure killed. Quantify: the four bills extend punishment; the CDC's 67% figure is the only cited intervention in this package backed by a systematic review of 15 studies.
- The **lobby asymmetry**: supporters of AB 1830 included MADD, the California Police Chiefs Association, and AAA SoCal. Opponents: ACLU California Action and the CA Public Defenders Association — a civil-liberties coalition on one side, a safety coalition on the other. The gutting came from a single committee chair, 74-0 notwithstanding.
- Dale math: MADD's 336,000 blocked attempts in 2025 = one every 95 seconds nationally. California's 1,311 dead is 11% of the national 11,904 — proportionally, that's roughly one blocked attempt that would have mattered in CA every ~14 minutes. (Illustrative scaling, labeled as such.)

## Limitations (honest accounting)

- Interlocks only work **while installed**: CDC's own review found re-arrest rates revert to no-interlock levels after removal (11 studies). The device manages risk, it doesn't cure alcoholism.
- The device can be **circumvented** (having a sober person blow), and doesn't address drug-impaired driving at all — CA's own drug-involved crash data (2,271 drug-impaired crashes in 2024 per SWITRS via victimslawyer.com) is outside interlocks' reach.
- California's 1,311 figure is an NHTSA estimate (BAC imputation when test results unknown); testing rates vary by state.
- No causal claim that AB 1830 would have prevented X deaths — that's counterfactual modeling the site won't do. The claim is narrower: the evidence-based tool was available, and the legislature chose penalties over it.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength, not strawmanned)

The San Francisco Public Defender's office, quoted in the AB 1830 legislative analysis: the mandate "contradicts available evidence about reducing vehicular fatalities, and broadly increases (the) likelihood that a person with a first DUI conviction will never be able to reinstate their license." It "exacerbates financial harm on low-income communities with numerous new fees and costs," and they would "only support evidence-based fatality reduction measures that can be implemented successfully and equitably." This is a serious objection: interlock installation runs $70–150 plus $60–80/month monitoring, and a first-time offender who can't pay may end up driving unlicensed and uninsured — trading one risk for another. The article must carry this, not wave it away. Dale's take: the equity problem is real and argues for subsidized devices, not for abandoning the device.

## Actionable insights

- If you live in California: first-offense DUI currently means a judge decides whether you get an interlock. The pilot program (through 2033) mandates them only for repeat offenders and injury-causing first offenses.
- If you're shopping the policy debate: the four signed bills add punishment; none adds the device the CDC reviewed across 15 studies. MADD's state report card grades the interlock laws — California earns a failing grade.
- If you're a first-time offender anywhere in the 34 all-offender states: you already know this drill.
