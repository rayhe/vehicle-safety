One Burning Cadillac Took Out Seven More. The Rule Says Park Them 50 Feet Apart.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
Friday, November 12, 2027

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. On Friday, October 9, around 5 p.m., Fort Worth firefighters were dispatched to Frank Kent Cadillac on West Loop 820 for a vehicle fire. They found one SUV engulfed and the flames moving into the SUVs parked beside it. Then somebody determined the vehicles were electric. Six electric SUVs were destroyed, at least two more were damaged, and the last fire truck did not go back in service until 10:30 the next morning: seventeen hours and thirty-three minutes on scene.[1] Nobody was hurt. Everything else about that night deserves your full attention.

17 hours, 33 minutes
One dealership fire, first dispatch to last unit back in service

Ten weeks earlier, General Motors had filed one of the smallest recalls in recent memory. NHTSA campaign 26V494, filed July 30, 2026, covered eleven vehicles: two Cadillac Escalade IQs, three Escalade IQLs, four Chevrolet Silverado EVs, one GMC Sierra EV, and one 2022 GMC Hummer EV pickup. A press machine at GM’s Factory Zero, the plant that builds the battery modules, had been misadjusted during scheduled downtime. Some sense line assembly fingers inside the high-voltage packs were not fully secured, a loose piece could move, damage the battery, and the battery could catch fire.[2] The consumer advisories on the filing were the two words no owner wants to read: do not drive, park outside. The fix is a full battery replacement, free, for all eleven trucks. GM told NHTSA it knew of zero field incidents. The suspect population was so narrow that GM traced it with photographic manufacturing records and a build window of about ten days in May.[2] It is a genuine masterclass in traceability. A machine sneezes during maintenance and eleven trucks on three nameplates get grounded within two months. That is how the system is supposed to work.

Now the part where I put the brakes on myself. Nobody has tied the Fort Worth fire to that recall, and the honest reading of the public record says you should not try. Five recalled Cadillacs are spread across the country. Fort Worth officials have not said which models burned. Investigators have not identified the cause. The Auto Wire’s own reporting, which is the best account we have, states it plainly: tying this fire to the recall is, on the record as it stands, a guess.[1] Forget the recall for a moment. The part of this story that is certain is worse, because it is not about one defective battery. It is about every parking lot in America.

GM publishes emergency response guides for its electric models, written for firefighters and tow operators. The guide for the Escalade IQ says to use large amounts of water, not to bother with an ABC dry chemical extinguisher because it will not put out a battery fire, and to expect the battery to reignite. After the flames are out, the guide says, store the vehicle about 15 meters, call it 50 feet, from other vehicles, because the hazards continue through towing and storage.[1] The reason has a name. The NTSB coined it in a 2020 safety report: stranded energy, the charge left inside a damaged battery after the fire is out, which can shock responders and restart the fire, and which firefighters have no way to measure on scene.[3] In one case the board studied, a Mountain View crash, the battery reignited at the impound lot five days later. That lot did not have enough room for the 50 feet of clearance the manufacturer recommended either.

So let us do the arithmetic that nobody at a dealership has done. A burned EV needs 50 feet of clearance. A dealer lot parks its most expensive inventory, including every electric Cadillac it has, roughly five feet apart, in rows, for acres. Fire codes regulate how dealers store gasoline. Nothing regulates how tightly dealers can park electric vehicles. On West Loop 820, firefighters made separation the hard way, dragging fire blankets over the burning SUVs to hold the fire in place while crews rotated through the night and other vehicles were moved out of the fire’s path.[1] The fire department’s public information officer told Firehouse.com that tactics have to keep current as vehicle construction changes, and that outdated tactics on these new fires may be the difference between life and death.[1] Seventeen and a half hours is not a crew dragging its feet. It is what the NTSB described. Knocking down the flames is one job. Standing by until a battery pack stops heating up is another. On a lot full of similar vehicles, there is nowhere to put a smoldering one.

Full strength for the other side, because it earned it. This was one lot, one Friday night, and the cause remains unknown. It could have been a battery. It could have been a charger, building wiring, or a perfectly ordinary gasoline car parked in the wrong row. GM’s containment here was exemplary: eleven vehicles, ten days of production, photographic records, zero known field incidents, a recall decision inside ten weeks of the first suspect module. Electric vehicles catch fire far less often per vehicle than gasoline cars do; what Fort Worth shows is not that they ignite more, but that when they do, the fire is a different animal, hotter, longer, and immune to the extinguishers on the wall. And it is worth saying plainly that six destroyed SUVs at Cadillac EV prices, $49,995 to $127,405 at sticker, means this one night vaporized somewhere between $300,000 and $764,000 of inventory before options.[1] Expensive fires have a way of getting regulations written for them.

What this story does not prove: it does not prove any defect in any Cadillac. It does not prove the 26V494 recall is connected to the fire. It proves that the 50-foot rule cannot be followed on any dealer lot in America, and that nobody has written the rule that would close the gap. If you own or are shopping for one of the eleven recalled vehicles, a 2026 Escalade IQ or IQL, a 2026 Silverado EV, a 2026 Sierra EV, or a 2022 Hummer EV pickup, run the VIN through NHTSA’s recall lookup today.[4] GM’s campaign number is N262562600, the fix is a free battery replacement, and the advisories are do not drive and park outside, the two that mean the manufacturer is not joking. Cadillac customer service is 1-800-333-4223. If you run a dealership or a fleet lot, check what your extinguishers are rated for, because the dry chemical ones will not touch a battery fire, and ask yourself where you would put a burned EV that needs fifty feet of clearance. Fire codes already tell you how to store gasoline. It might be time they said something about the cars that park five feet apart and burn for seventeen hours.
