NHTSA Gave the Altima Five Stars; the Road Gave It 4,787 Bodies

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
June 14, 2027

Somewhere between the laboratory and the highway, the Nissan Altima becomes a different car. In NHTSA crash testing it earned five stars overall, with four for frontal impact, five for side, and five for rollover resistance, yet on actual roads federal data shows it kills at 2.88 deaths per 100 million miles, 56 percent higher than the Toyota Corolla in the next lane over.[1]

56%
Higher per-mile death rate than the Toyota Corolla, its direct competitor

Start with the body count, because that is where every investigation starts: ten years of FARS data record 4,787 dead in Altimas against 4,945 in Corollas, practically the same carnage until you check the mileage, where the Corolla fleet logged roughly 61 percent more miles to produce its matching pile of bodies. The Altima does not kill more people; it kills the same number of people while driving far less, and that distinction was the first thing that made me sit up.

Suspect number one is drunk drivers, because every detective checks the toxicology first, but Altima drivers in fatal crashes tested impaired 20.0 percent of the time against 19.2 percent for Corolla drivers, with alcohol readings within a rounding error of each other. Dead heat, and somebody else's theory just died in the parking lot.

Suspect number two is one bad generation, a single cursed redesign dragging the average down, but the model-year buckets refuse to cooperate: deaths ran 2,050 in the 2000s, 1,367 in the early 2010s, and 1,185 from 2015 through 2023, elevated in every generation for twenty years. It is not one bad Altima; it is all of them, and the siblings are no help either, with the Maxima posting a 5.11 death rate and the Sentra 2.13 while only the humble Versa behaves.

Then the independent witness walked in, because IIHS keeps its own books in driver deaths per million registered vehicle years, adjusted for driver age and sex, and the Altima's entry reads 113 against a study average of 38, which is nearly triple.[2] Two completely different datasets with two completely different methodologies deliver the same verdict, and when the lab says five stars while both real-world ledgers say triple the deaths, the lab is measuring something other than what kills people.

That leaves the last suspect, the one I cannot arrest: the drivers themselves. IIHS adjusts for age and sex but not for speed, mileage, or behavior, and the Institute's own president attributes muscle cars' grim rankings to how they are driven.[3] The Altima is a value-priced midsize sedan that sells heavily to younger buyers and rental fleets, FARS records the crash but not who bought the car or why, and it is entirely possible this is an average car purchased by above-average-risk drivers. Nothing in my data can rule that out, so the case stays open.

What you can do with an unsolved case: if you drive a 2013-2022 Altima, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change, because the gap here sits in crash occurrence rather than crashworthiness, which means following distance and speed buy you more protection than the star rating does.[4] If you are shopping midsize sedans, learn what each number actually measures, since the stars grade the laboratory while FARS and IIHS grade the road, and on the road the Altima and Corolla are not the same car at all. Somebody should figure out why, and until they do, the numbers sit in the file, smirking.
