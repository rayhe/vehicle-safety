4,456 People Were Thrown From Their Cars in 2024. The Curtains Couldn't Catch Them.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 28, 2026

Twelve people a day. That is the pace at which Americans were thrown from their cars and killed in 2024. Not killed in the crash. Killed by leaving the car during the crash, hurled through a window or a door opening onto asphalt, guardrail, or the road their own vehicle was still rolling across. Federal crash records counted 4,456 of them last year, one in five of all passenger vehicle occupants who died.[1]

4,456
Passenger vehicle occupants ejected and killed in 2024, 20% of all occupant deaths

Start with the improvement, because it is real and it deserves a sentence. Ejection deaths fell 11 percent in 2024, down 537 from 4,993 in 2023, the sharpest percentage improvement among the headline categories NHTSA tracks.[1] Rollover deaths fell too, by 380, to 6,250. Electronic stability control, stronger roofs, and curtain airbags are doing their jobs, and the numbers say so. But scale has a way of humbling percentages. Even after the best year on record for this particular way to die, 4,456 ejected deaths still outnumbered the 3,208 deaths in distraction-affected crashes by 39 percent. Distraction gets the campaigns, the PSAs, the statehouse hearings. Ejection gets a shrug, as if being thrown from a car were weather.

Here is what ejection means in practice, stripped of euphemism. NHTSA's 2024 passenger vehicle fact sheet reports that 84 percent of occupants totally ejected in fatal crashes were killed.[2] Four out of five. A car is a cage engineered to keep its occupants alive through forces that would kill an unprotected body, and the moment you leave the cage, you are an unprotected body at highway speed. Side curtain airbags exist for exactly this failure mode. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 226, in force for every new vehicle since September 2017, requires curtains or glazing strong enough to keep a test headform within 100 millimeters of the window during a rollover.[3] That rule engineered the window. It never claimed it could engineer the occupant. Put the two counts side by side and the scale is stark: 4,456 ejected deaths against 6,250 rollover deaths in the same year, and not every ejected death happened in a rollover, which means ejection is also killing people in crashes that never rolled at all.[1]

That is where the belt comes in, and where the data gets blunt. Of the 22,713 passenger vehicle occupants killed in 2024, 48 percent were unrestrained, and the comparison that should end arguments sits in the same report: among the people who survived those same fatal crashes, only 13 percent were unrestrained.[1] Same crashes, same violence, same roads. Occupants who died were unbelted at nearly four times the rate of those who lived. At night the split is worse, with 56 percent of the dead unrestrained against 41 percent in daylight. And the vehicle that most often throws its occupants out is the pickup: 39 percent of pickup occupant deaths happened in rollovers, ahead of SUVs at 32 percent, vans at 23 percent, and cars at 20 percent.[2] A pickup buyer is buying the rollover risk and the ejection risk in one package, which makes the belt more important in a pickup, not less.

Give the other side its due, at full strength. An 11 percent drop in ejection deaths is the largest single-year improvement in the recent record, and the system behind it is genuinely working. Stability control stops rollovers before they start, roofs hold up better than they did a generation ago, and the curtains required since 2017 cover windows that used to be open holes in a rollover. Most of what remains concentrates among the unbelted, and no window technology can substitute for a belt. Two caveats, both load-bearing. One: the 2024 counts come from the Annual Report File, which NHTSA revises when it finalizes the data, so small numbers may shift. Two: the report publishes no cross-tab of ejection against restraint use for 2024, which means nobody can say exactly how many of the 4,456 were unbelted, only that the belt is the single variable that separates the dead from the survivors in the same crashes. An older agency estimate that 72 percent of rollover deaths involved unbelted occupants dates to 2008, before the curtain rule existed, and it should not be quoted as a 2024 fact.[4]

So here is what to do with it. Buckle up on every trip, including the five-minute one, because the belt is the only safety device in the car that costs nothing, weighs nothing, and changes your odds by a factor of nearly four. If you drive a pickup, treat the belt as part of starting the truck, not as an optional accessory. If you are shopping for a used car, check the build date: anything built after September 2017 meets the ejection-mitigation standard, and anything older may not. And buckle the back seat too. Passengers in the back are counting on the same physics, and the same belt, and they do not get a vote on whether you remember.
