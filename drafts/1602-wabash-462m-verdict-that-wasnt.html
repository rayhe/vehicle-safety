The $462 Million Verdict That Wasn’t

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

Somewhere on the internet right now, the $462 million Wabash verdict is still alive. It gets quoted in law firm ads, in trucking newsletters, in angry threads about runaway juries. It is a zombie number. A St. Louis jury did award $462 million in September 2024, and then the legal system spent a year taking most of it back. A judge cut the punitive damages from $450 million to $108 million. Wabash settled the case in October 2025 for an undisclosed sum covered mostly by insurance, with the company contributing $30 million of its own money. Add $4.6 million in bond and penalty-interest costs and the company's total cash outlay comes to about $34.6 million.[1][2] That is the whole story in one ratio. Everyone remembers the verdict. Nobody remembers the ending.

7.5%
Share of the $462 million verdict Wabash actually paid out of its own pocket: $34.6 million in settlement contribution and bond costs.

Start with the crash, because the crash is the part nobody disputes. In 2019, a Volkswagen sedan with an unobstructed view struck the back of a nearly stopped 2004 Wabash trailer on a Missouri road.[1] Two young fathers, Taron Tailor and Nicholas Perkins, were killed when the car's front end slid underneath the trailer bed.[7] Their families sued Wabash, the trailer's manufacturer, not the trucking company that owned and operated it. Their theory was design defect: the trailer's rear impact guard, a two-post steel bar known in the industry as the "U.S. RIG," was a design from 1985 that Wabash had barely changed in two decades, and the company had not crash-tested it at all between 1985 and 1996.[3] Other manufacturers, the plaintiffs argued, had already moved to stronger four-post designs. Wabash's answer was simpler. Its guard complied with every applicable federal standard. No guard could have saved two men decelerating from highway speed to zero in the length of a trailer bed. Meeting the government's own rule, the company said, cannot be a defect.

Missouri law then did something that shaped everything after it. Missouri has no statute of repose for product liability claims, so the families could sue over a trailer sold in 2004 for a crash in 2019, fifteen years later.[4] And the court ruled that comparative fault does not apply to driver error that amounts to foreseeable misuse of the product, which meant the jury never heard that the driver had a blood alcohol level over the legal limit, or that neither occupant was wearing a seat belt.[4] Wabash's compliance defense went to the jury alongside evidence of the untested guard, the four-post alternative, and decades of industry lobbying against stricter guard standards. On September 5, 2024, the jury awarded $6 million to each family in compensatory damages and $450 million in punitive damages.[1]

Do the ratio math, because the ratio is where the verdict died. Punitive damages of $450 million on $12 million of compensatory damages is a 37.5-to-1 ratio. In State Farm v. Campbell, the Supreme Court said punitive awards beyond a single-digit ratio to compensatory damages will rarely satisfy due process.[8] On March 20, 2025, the St. Louis circuit court agreed, ruling that the $450 million award "did not comport with the Company's constitutional rights" and cutting it to $108 million, with compensatory damages left at $11.5 million after an offset for the families' settlement with a separate defendant.[1] That left an "Adjusted Award" of $119.5 million, a 9.4-to-1 ratio, just above the single-digit line the Supreme Court sketched. One judicial order erased 74.1 percent of the headline number before any settlement talks concluded. Wabash recognized a $342 million reduction to the $461.5 million charge it had taken in the third quarter of 2024.[1]

Seven months later the case ended the way most nuclear verdicts end: quietly, confidentially, and mostly on someone else's money. On October 9, 2025, Wabash finalized a settlement covered by insurance except for a $30 million company contribution, and recognized a further $81.2 million reduction to the original charge.[2] Bond and contingent penalty-interest expenses totaled $4.6 million. No admission of liability. Matter closed. Run the charge unwind all the way down and the $461.5 million charge shrinks to $38.3 million: $342 million reversed in the first quarter of 2025, $81.2 million more at settlement. Wabash's own cash costs, the $30 million contribution plus $4.6 million in bond and interest expenses, come to $34.6 million. A jury said $462 million. A judge said $119.5 million. An insurer and a $30 million check said the rest.

None of this makes the guard safe. It makes the verdict unreliable as a measure of anything, which is a different problem, and a more interesting one. Consider the paradox at the center of the case. Wabash's guard met the federal standard in force when the trailer was built. NHTSA itself has since decided that standard was not good enough: its July 2022 final rule, effective January 2023, requires rear impact guards to protect occupants of compact and subcompact cars hitting at 35 mph, at center and at 50 percent overlap, adopting requirements similar to Canada's.[5] A company can follow the federal rule to the letter, watch the government rewrite the rule because the old one was inadequate, and still be told by a jury that following the old rule was the defect. Compliance is a floor, not a shield, and the floor moved underneath a trailer that had already been on the road for fifteen years.

In the same month the Wabash story resurfaced in a November 2026 legal analysis, a Los Angeles jury awarded $21.9 million in a different underride case, Gallegos v. Consolidated Disposal, over a garbage truck's rear guard that was not compliant with existing law.[9] Same device, opposite facts. In Gallegos the guard failed the law as written; in Wabash the guard passed it. Both juries found liability. Put the two verdicts side by side and the pattern is not that guards keep failing. It is that the law keeps arriving after the crash, the standard keeps moving after the trailer ships, and the only number that ever sticks is the one nobody reports: what actually got paid.

Here is the strongest case against everything above, stated plainly. A drunk, unbelted driver hit a nearly stopped trailer he could see clearly at 40 to 55 mph. Wabash's experts testified that no guard on the market, not even the modern four-post design the plaintiffs held up as the alternative, would have saved two men in a car stopping from highway speed in the length of a trailer bed, because the human body cannot survive that deceleration.[4][7] Missouri law kept the alcohol and the seat belts out of the courtroom. The $450 million punitive award was 37.5 times the compensatory damages, a ratio the trial court itself found unconstitutional and cut by three-quarters. And the confidential settlement means nobody outside the parties knows what the families actually received, while the insurer paid most of it. Read this way, the case is not a safety story at all. It is a story about a sympathetic pair of young fathers, a runaway jury, and a manufacturer that followed the federal rule to the letter and got punished for the rule being old.

That reading has real force, and it is why the judge cut the award, and why the case settled. But it does not answer the question the plaintiffs asked, which is whether a manufacturer that never crash-tested a safety device for eleven years, while other manufacturers upgraded theirs, gets to call the federal minimum the ceiling. The jury said no. The judge said the price was wrong, not the verdict. Both can be true, and the $34.6 million is what both truths cost.

If you drive a car, you cannot crash-test a trailer's guard from your lane. You can refuse to tailgate a truck whose rear guard is visibly bent, damaged, or missing, and you can report it: a missing or damaged rear guard on a commercial truck is a reportable defect to the carrier and to FMCSA's National Consumer Complaint Database. If you run a fleet, remember the 2022 NHTSA rule covers new trailers, not the 2004 trailer still hauling freight, and FMCSA's 2021 rule put rear guards on the annual-inspection checklist, so a missing inspection record is evidence, not paperwork. Audit your guards against FMVSS 223/224 as written, and against the 35-mph compact-car test, not the 1998 standard. And if you buy a trailer or spec a fleet, ask for the four-post guard design and the crash-test data behind it. Wabash's own trial record included the fact that it had not tested its two-post design for over a decade. A guard that has never been tested is a decoration with a federal stamp on it, and the stamp is not the test.
