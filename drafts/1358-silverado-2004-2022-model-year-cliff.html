Generation Gap

The 2004 Silverado Out-Killed the 2022 by 15 to 1. Same Badge, Different Species.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 1, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens when the same badge hides two completely different vehicles. A 2004 Silverado and a 2022 Silverado share a name, a grille, and roughly nothing else about how they keep you alive. I pulled FARS death counts by model year for ten of America’s best-selling nameplates, 2014–2023, and the old designs didn’t just lose. Old designs: 6,912 deaths. New ones: 656.[1]

10.5x
Deaths in 2004–2005 designs vs 2021–2022 designs, same ten nameplates, same ten-year window

The truck numbers are the most brutal. Silverado, old design: 1,242 deaths. New: 82 (15.1x). F-150: 1,075 to 60 (17.9x). Sedans tell the same story at lower volume: Accord 863 to 59, Camry 677 to 103, Altima 465 to 66, Corolla 490 to 141, Civic 532 to 65, Malibu 583 to 53. Even the Mustang, a car people buy specifically to be a little bit unsafe, drops from 296 to 27. Ten nameplates, ten cliffs.[1]

There is a dividing line between these two eras, and it has a docket number. In April 2007, NHTSA issued the FMVSS 126 final rule requiring electronic stability control on every light vehicle sold in America: phase-in over model years 2009–2011, 100 percent for vehicles built on or after September 1, 2011.[2] The 2004–2005 designs predate that mandate by the better part of a decade. IIHS measured ESC cutting fatal single-vehicle crash risk 49 percent and fatal single-vehicle rollovers by roughly three-quarters.[3] NHTSA estimated ESC saved 2,202 lives in 2008–2010 alone.[4] In the first 150 milliseconds of a crash, a GMT800-era truck and a 2022 platform are different species.

Honesty section, because I refuse to let the 10.5x do work the data can’t support. Raw model-year counts mix design with exposure: the old cohort had roughly ten years of road time in the window versus two for the new one, so after scrappage the exposure ratio is maybe four or five to one. Observed death ratio: 10.5 to one aggregate, 15 to 18 to one for trucks. The old designs are still on the order of two to four times deadlier per unit of exposure, and that remainder lines up with the ESC mandate and two decades of structural progress. Not 15x per mile. Not nothing, either.

Shopping used? Draw the line at model year 2012: the first year federal law put ESC on every light vehicle, and ESC alone roughly halves your fatal single-vehicle crash risk. A 2009–2011 vehicle probably has it too, thanks to the 55/75/95 percent phase-in, so check the specific VIN against NHTSA’s ESC equipment list rather than trusting the model year.[2] That cheap 2004 truck on the lot is not “just an older version.” It is the same badge on a previous century of crash science. Price it accordingly, or keep looking.
