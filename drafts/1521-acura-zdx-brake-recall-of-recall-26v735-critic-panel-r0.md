## 1. General Editor — 8.5
Lede earns its keep: 4,174 vs 14,648, same defect, same fix, 3.5x the cars, all in the first paragraph. Structure is clean: mechanism, the seven-day pattern, the EA26006 accounting, the dead-car kicker, the counterargument, the action list, the limitations. The seven-day pattern twice is the piece's best discovery and it sits where a reader will find it. At 972 words it is on the long side for a single-recall story, and the EA26006 paragraph is the densest stretch, four large numbers in five sentences, but each number earns its place and the paragraph ends on the Prologue warning, which is the sentence that justifies the arithmetic. The headline is long at sixteen words, but every word is load-bearing. Dock is the middle: the mechanism paragraph and the calendar paragraph both explain the GM connection, and a sentence of overlap survives between them.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0
Mia is present and distinct: the piece opens on her signature register ("Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds") and then actually delivers the milliseconds, walking through false ABS activation and pressure release the way an engineer would, without dumbing it down. "Not degraded, not spongy, but inoperative" is the kind of triplet she writes. Rhythm metrics pass with wide margin after the fragment merge, and the merge improved the prose rather than just the metric. Zero banned phrases, zero em dashes, zero "The" starters. One seam: "Credit where it is due" is a generic throat-clearer that any journalist could write, and the "What to do" list's question-led format is a series template rather than Mia's own device. Neither breaks the voice. Ship it.

## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0
There are no victims to exploit here: zero crashes or injuries reported in a ZDX over this defect, and the article says so twice rather than implying a body count. Blame lands on Acura's scoping decision and on GM's shared brake family, never on owners, and the call to check VINs is legitimate civic action with a real mechanism behind it. The discontinued-car thread is handled without schadenfreude: the article tells existing owners their service network and warranty coverage continue only by implication, and a reader shopping used could use one explicit sentence on that point, but the action list already tells them to verify all six campaigns before paying, which is the information they actually need. No self-congratulation, no moralizing.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0
The headline is built to travel, and the pull stat (14,648) is the number people will quote, with "three-quarters of every ZDX ever built" as the line that survives a screenshot on its own. Quotable lines are spread through the piece rather than front-loaded: "When the company that built your car finds a brake problem, your recall arrives the following week," "Its biggest recall is arriving after its funeral," and the 7-day pattern table in prose form. The piece loses a point only because the most shareable context, that the car is already dead and this recall covers most of its entire production run, sits in the fifth paragraph rather than the lede, where a skimming reader might miss why a 14,648-car recall is a story at all. Fine for the site's audience; the lede's 3.5x figure carries the hook.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.5
Every checkable claim verified against primary documents. The 26V735 record (14,648, AWD only, EBCM software, CQC, expands 24V614, letters December 14, 2026) matches the NHTSA API record verbatim and the TTAC reporting of November 14. The 24V614 figures (4,174, 1 percent estimated with defect, below-25-mph mechanism, GM 24V589 trigger on August 8, 2024, zero Acura claims as of that date) match the Part 573 filing. GM's 26V717 (67,380, October 29, 2026) matches the #1387 research file. EA26006 scope (1,164,820 vehicles, 745 reports, 21 crashes or fires, six injuries) matches Autoblog's August 25 account. Two nits: reference [6] is the generic FARS link, and the article contains no FARS data, so the citation is decorative rather than load-bearing; and the body sentence "18,911 sales" omits the "through August 2025" qualifier that the limitations paragraph and reference [7] carry, so a reader who stops at the body gets a slightly firmer number than the source supports. Neither is a factual error. Both are recorded here.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0
Original contribution is real and stated: the 3.51x expansion ratio, the 77.5 percent of all ZDXs ever sold, the 812-day gap, the repeated seven-day GM-to-Honda lag in 2024 and 2026, and the EA26006 accounting (82,028 recalled of 1,164,820, 7.0 percent, 1,082,792 still waiting), all computed from primary sources, not quoted. Limitations are explicit and honest: the 26V735 Part 573 chronology was not available at research time and no complaint count is invented for it, the 1 percent defect estimate from 24V614 is not carried forward, the two recalls are different modules in the same family rather than the same defect, and the sales denominator is through August 2025. The counterargument is genuinely steelmanned ("a recall that expands is not automatically a recall that failed") and granted in full before the answer. Methodology caveat is in the disclaimer as well as the body. Seven references, four of them primary NHTSA documents plus the live API.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0
The pull stat is the thesis number and it is correct: 14,648, with the 77 percent context in the label. All numbers are consistent between body, pull stat, and disclaimer: 14,648 / 4,174 = 3.51x, 14,648 / 18,911 = 77.5 percent, 67,380 + 14,648 = 82,028 = 7.0 percent of 1,164,820, remainder 1,082,792, August 15, 2024 to November 5, 2026 = 812 days, October 29 to November 5 = 7 days, August 8 to August 15, 2024 = 7 days. The campaign-population vs unique-vehicle distinction is stated in the disclaimer. The six-campaign count for the 2024 ZDX matches the API sweep. No chart needed: the story is a ratio and a calendar, and both are shown in prose.
