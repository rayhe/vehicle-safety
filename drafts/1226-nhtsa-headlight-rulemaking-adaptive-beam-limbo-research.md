# Research — Article #1226: "NHTSA Is Rewriting the Headlight Rule for the First Time in 25 Years. The Fix It Would Unlock Has Been Legal in Europe Since 2012."

**Journalist:** Vin Wreckage (Existential Dread columnist — paradox / counterintuitive findings)
**News peg:** NHTSA announced October 6, 2026 it will modernize FMVSS No. 108 through negotiated rulemaking — the first time in 25+ years. Public comments and committee nominations due November 9, 2026 (docket NHTSA-2026-1981).
**Kill test:** Passes. This is not the glare-vs-crash-data story (#1103 covered that). This is the regulatory paradox: America has had a working anti-glare headlight technology since 2012 (Europe), legalized it in 2022, then wrote the rule so strictly that only a handful of automakers sell it here — often on physically identical hardware de-contented for the US market. Now NHTSA is convening its first negotiated lighting rulemaking in a quarter century, in part to clear the bottleneck it created.

## Primary sources (5)

1. **NHTSA FMVSS 108 modernization announcement (Oct 6, 2026):** negotiated rulemaking; five work areas (glare from modern headlights, LED/dusk-sensor/auto-high-beam/replaceable-source rules, headlamp height and aim, test procedures for plastic optics and replaceable lenses, international harmonization); up to 18 stakeholders appointed by Sec. Duffy; comments due Nov 9, 2026; first such committee for lighting since the mid-1990s (aiming rule, 1997 final rule); federal brightness limits have changed little in a quarter century.
2. **IIHS October 2026 headlight-glare study:** police-reported crashes across 11 states (~24 million); glare cited in 0.1–0.2% of nighttime crashes (<150,000 of 24M had glare coded at all); most glare-coded crashes were daylight sun-glare; nighttime glare crashes skewed to older drivers, older vehicles, undivided low-speed roads, lane departures most common; Harkey: glare "contributes to far fewer crashes than insufficient visibility"; good-rated headlights tied to 19% fewer nighttime single-vehicle crashes and 23% fewer nighttime pedestrian crashes vs. poor-rated; good-rated systems from 1 of 80+ in 2016 to ~half of 2025 models tested; excessive glare in IIHS testing fell from 21% (2017) to 3% (2025 models) — roadethos cites 5% for 2026 model year.
3. **AAA survey (Feb 5–8, 2026, n=1,092 adults, ±4 pp):** 60% consider headlight glare a problem after dark; 73% of those say it has worsened over the past decade; 92% blame oncoming headlights; prescription-glasses wearers 70% vs. 56% without; women 70% vs. men 57%; pickup drivers (sit highest) only 41% vs. 66% of everyone else.
4. **Adaptive driving beam (ADB) regulatory history:** Toyota petitioned NHTSA in 2013; sold in Europe since 2012; NHTSA approved ADB in 2022 after a decade of work; industry argued at the time the final rule held ADB to higher glare standards than conventional lights and required new lamp designs rather than enabling hardware already installed on some imported luxury models; automakers asked for reconsideration; IIHS says American requirements restrict ADB functionality and may limit effectiveness; only a few manufacturers currently offer ADB in the US (roadethos notes BMW/Audi sell de-contented versions of physically identical hardware here).
5. **Enforcement context:** July 2026 NHTSA denied Tesla's inconsequentiality petition for nearly 20,000 Model 3/Y whose headlights may exceed maximum lighting levels; in 2022 it denied a similar GM petition covering ~820,000 vehicles; the standard defines no glare limits in lux for conventional headlights (as it does for ADB); only ten states require headlight alignment checks at inspection.

## Key stats for the piece
- 25+ years since the last negotiated lighting rulemaking (1997 aiming final rule).
- 2012: ADB legal in Europe; 2022: legal in the US (a decade later).
- 60% of drivers call glare a problem; it accounts for 0.1–0.2% of nighttime crashes (the Vin paradox).
- Comments due **November 9, 2026** — three weeks from the run date; this is the actionable beat.
- ~820,000 GM vehicles denied inconsequentiality (2022); ~20,000 Teslas denied (July 2026).
