## Story in one line
On November 11, 2026, California's DMV barred Waymo's driverless cars from every freeway in the state, calling them an "imminent hazard to public safety" after three robotaxis drove into an active construction zone on U.S. 101 and another tried to pass CHP cruisers running a traffic break on I-280 — and within hours Waymo voluntarily pulled its robotaxis off freeways in every U.S. market, so one state's order grounded the national fleet.

## Key verified facts
- **The order:** California DMV issued operational restrictions November 11, 2026; made public November 17. Covers Waymo's deployment AND driverless testing permits. Bars driverless operation and testing on all California freeways. No end date; remains until Waymo demonstrates "effective corrective actions" to address the deficiencies.
- **The trigger incidents:** November 5 — three Waymo vehicles entered an active construction closure on U.S. 101 near Burlingame, workers present. November 6 — a Waymo on I-280 in San Francisco appeared to try to pass two CHP cars running a traffic break (officers weaving across lanes with lights on to slow traffic). Video of the I-280 incident went viral. No injuries or collisions in either incident, per Waymo.
- **The quote:** DMV: "imminent hazard to public safety due to recurring incursions by its vehicles into active construction zones where construction workers, vehicles and equipment are present, despite a prior restriction and corrective actions implemented following similar incidents that occurred on May 18, 2026."
- **The spillover:** Waymo "temporarily restricted freeway operations across its other operating markets" — Bay Area, Los Angeles, Phoenix, Miami, Austin. Surface-street rides continue in California. Supervised freeway testing (trained specialists behind the wheel) continues.
- **The history:** Freeways were supposed to be the easy domain. Waymo opened public freeway trips November 2025 (Phoenix, Los Angeles, Bay Area; Miami later). May 18, 2026: similar construction-zone incidents. May 21, 2026: Waymo self-paused freeway operations. June 18, 2026: NHTSA recall 26E035, 3,871 vehicles, software could enter closed freeway construction zones and continue at speed. July 2026: back on freeways. November 11, 2026: banned.
- **The counterargument (stated at full strength):** Waymo's own data says across 270M+ driverless miles its vehicles had 82% fewer injury-causing crashes and 95% fewer serious-injury-or-death crashes than human drivers in the same cities. Freeways carry about half of California's miles. If the ban keeps a safer system off the safest roads, it may cost lives rather than save them. The DMV's answer, implicit in the order: the aggregate rate does not cover the specific failure — a system that cannot read a construction zone or a police traffic break is dangerous in exactly the places where the humans around it are most exposed.
- **Regulatory context:** DMV's April 2026 rules let it impose targeted limits on AV manufacturers (fleet size, location, speed, weather). Since July 1, 2026, California police can issue a formal notice of AV noncompliance to the manufacturer, which must report it to the DMV. This is the first time the DMV has used the "imminent hazard" framing against a driverless operator.

## Original contributions (show the work)
1. **The fix lasted four months.** Waymo returned to freeways in July 2026 after the June 18 recall. The DMV banned it again on November 11. July to November is roughly four months of freeway service between a software fix and a state ban for the identical failure mode. A recall remedy that survives one summer is not a remedy.
2. **Three actions, 177 days, one failure.** May 18, 2026 incidents → November 11, 2026 ban = 177 days. Inside that span: a voluntary pause (May 21), a federal recall (June 18, 3,871 vehicles), a return to service (July), and a state ban (Nov 11). Every step addressed construction zones. Every step failed to end construction-zone incursions.
3. **May was voluntary. November was not.** In May, Waymo pulled the plug itself. In November, a regulator did it, and the restriction covers testing as well as rides — Waymo cannot even test its way back onto California freeways without a driver. That is the difference between a company managing a PR problem and a state managing a hazard.
4. **One state's order grounded five markets.** California's restriction applied only to California freeways. Waymo's response applied to all five freeway markets nationwide. The company decided, or was forced to admit, that a construction-zone failure in Burlingame is not a California problem — it is a Waymo problem, everywhere its cars drive at highway speed.

## Limitations
- No injuries or collisions in the November incidents; the "hazard" is prospective, based on recurrence and exposure of workers, not on a body count. The article must not imply anyone was hurt.
- Incident dates: AP reports Nov 5 (US 101) and Nov 6 (I-280); some secondary summaries cite Oct 28 for the US 101 closure. AP's dates are used; the discrepancy is noted, not resolved.
- Waymo's 82%/95% safety figures are the company's own analysis of its own data, not an independent evaluation. Treat as the company's claim, and present the DMV's recurrence evidence alongside it.
- NHTSA's school-bus recall query RQ26006 (Nov 6, 2026) is a separate, concurrent action — mentioned only as context that November was a bad month for Waymo's regulators, not conflated with the freeway ban.
- Work-zone fatality context: roughly 850 people a year are killed in U.S. work zones (see site article #work-zone-850-killed-motorists); that figure covers all work-zone deaths, not Waymo-related ones.

## Actionable takeaways
- If you ride Waymo: freeway rides are paused nationwide for now — airport trips and cross-town freeway routes will run on surface streets, slower and longer. Check the app before booking a freeway-dependent trip.
- If you work in a highway construction zone: the DMV's order is an admission that current driverless systems cannot reliably read your work zone. Treat any approaching driverless vehicle the way you would treat a distracted driver.
- If you drive near a robotaxi on a freeway: a traffic break is a police maneuver, not a suggestion — the Waymo that tried to pass the CHP cars is the reason the state acted. Do not follow a driverless car around a traffic break.
- If you follow AV regulation: watch what the DMV accepts as "effective corrective actions" — that standard, once set for Waymo, becomes the bar for every operator that wants California freeways.
