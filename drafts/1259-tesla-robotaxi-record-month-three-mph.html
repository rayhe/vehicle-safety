Tesla's Robotaxi Had Its Worst Crash Month Ever. The Speedometer Barely Noticed.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
December 23, 2027

The federal government posted the worst month in Tesla robotaxi history on October 15: nine crash reports in August, a record.[1] Buried in the fine print: seven of the nine list a pre-crash speed of three miles per hour or less. One of the crashes was with a utility meter, at 0.45 miles per hour. Numbers don't lie; they do, however, occasionally smirk.

3 mph
Pre-crash speed in 78% of the August robotaxi crashes

Consider the rap sheet. An Austin alley, one mile per hour, a dumpster. A Dallas residential street, a parked SUV, 0.40 miles per hour. In Tampa and Dallas, two robotaxis followed their navigation straight into low-hanging metal chains strung across parking lot driveways, at 4 and 3 miles per hour. Another made low-speed contact with a utility meter at 0.45 mph. Another kissed a guardrail at 0.25.[1] A brisk walk is about 3 mph. Most of this month's record was a parade of parking-lot grazes, logged by cars that report everything because their owner can see everything.

And then there is the Cybercab's federal debut. That steering-wheel-free purpose-built robotaxi, the one with no pedals and no mirrors, made its first appearance in NHTSA's crash database by driving into a water-filled pothole in an Austin parking lot, "as fast as 5MPH," with a safety monitor aboard and no passengers,[1] defeated by a puddle with ambition.

Only two of the nine involve real traffic. In Dallas, a pickup ran a stop sign into a robotaxi; Tesla's narrative says its car slowed from 27 mph to 3 and steered right before the truck struck its left side. In Austin, an SUV rear-ended a robotaxi stopped at a yield sign, with three passengers inside, and nobody was injured in either. In fact, nobody has been injured in any of the 36 Tesla ADS reports filed under the current SGO version, across roughly fifteen months of operation.[1] So far, the injury risk in Tesla's robotaxi program belongs entirely to human drivers doing human things: running stop signs, rear-ending stopped cars.

0
Injuries across all 36 Tesla robotaxi crash reports in federal data

Now for the part the headline writers skipped. On June 16, 2025, the third-amended Standing General Order took effect, days before the Austin launch, and it deliberately added a property-damage reporting threshold for automated driving systems.[2] This whole fifteen-month reporting history exists under a regime that was built to catch exactly these grazes. NHTSA's own documentation says crash counts are not normalized by fleet size or miles traveled, and that a company with superior telemetry will simply report more crashes because it knows about more crashes.[2] Tesla filed nine August reports. Texas records listed 769 Tesla robotaxi vehicles by mid-October, including 349 Cybercabs, and Tesla publishes no mileage figures at all.[1] The "record month" is the reporting regime working as designed, during the quarter the fleet was expanding into Dallas and Tampa,[3] which is not exoneration but arithmetic.

Strip out the adjectives. August was the busiest filing month in a reporting system that cannot compute rates, for a fleet whose mileage is undisclosed, with 78% of incidents occurring slower than a jog. A Cybercab hit a pothole, which tells you something real about perception systems and water. Those two traffic crashes tell you something real about the other drivers in the geofence. What the other seven tell you: a car with telemetry files paperwork when it touches a dumpster, and most federal "crash data" headlines deserve to be read twice.

What to actually do with this

Before citing any robotaxi crash-count headline, check three things in the SGO data: the pre-crash speed, whether the report is property-damage-only under the June 2025 threshold, and whether any denominator exists (it never does; NHTSA doesn't collect mileage). For riders, the data says low-speed property damage dominates federal robotaxi filings, and the injury risk so far comes from human drivers sharing the road. The Cybercab matters more than the pothole: Dallas Cybercab rides launch next month and Tesla's answer to NHTSA's self-certification audit is due October 30, the same agency weighing a defect petition over school-zone speeding.[4]

Source: NHTSA Standing General Order ADS incident data through Sept 15, 2026, via Tesla News Wire. Speeds are self-reported pre-crash speeds, not measured impact speeds; fleet size and mileage are not published, so no crash rate can be computed. See methodology for caveats.
