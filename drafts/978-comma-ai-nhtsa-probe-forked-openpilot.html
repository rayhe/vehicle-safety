Existential Dread

NHTSA Is Probing Comma.ai's Driver-Assistance Kit. Good Luck Finding the Manufacturer.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 23, 2026
☕ 4 min read

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Somewhere on the highway next to you, a car may be steering itself with software forked on GitHub by a stranger, flashed onto an aftermarket box, and never crash-tested by anyone. On Wednesday, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation announced a probe into Comma.ai, the startup behind the open-source openpilot driver-assistance system, after five crashes killed three people and seriously injured eleven, every one of them a vehicle driving into a stopped or slow-moving vehicle it somehow never saw.

The part that should keep regulators up at night is not the five crashes. It is the February crash in Ascension Parish, Louisiana, where a 2022 Toyota RAV4 running FrogPilot, a community fork of openpilot, drove into a stopped first-responder vehicle and killed the two people riding in its own back seat. Forks can rewrite braking tuning, detection thresholds, engagement logic, and how strictly the driver-monitoring camera nags you about watching the road. So here is the question NHTSA now has to answer with a straight face: who manufactured the defective product? Comma built the box and wrote the stock software, a stranger on the internet wrote the fork, and the driver installed both in a driveway. The federal defect-investigation machine was designed for factories, VIN lists, and recall letters. It has no procedure for a GitHub repo.

0.75
Deaths per 100M miles on Comma's claimed 402M openpilot miles, vs. 1.20 nationally in 2024. The math favors the robot, but the caveats are doing a lot of work.

Before anyone drafts the eulogy, do the division the company's critics would rather you skip. Three deaths across 402 million claimed miles is 0.75 deaths per 100 million miles, against a national rate of 1.20 in 2024. If Comma's odometer is honest, a mile driven under openpilot supervision is, statistically, a safer mile than the average American mile. Maybe five crashes in a 30,000-vehicle fleet is noise, or maybe highway-heavy engaged miles flatter the number. Maybe enthusiast drivers in newer cars are just better at not dying. All true, all worth saying, and none of it changes the part about the two people in the back seat of the RAV4. Rates are cold comfort when the failure mode is your car failing to notice a fire truck.

This is also a sequel: in 2016, NHTSA told Comma to prove its self-driving gadget was safe or stop selling it. Founder George Hotz canceled the device, said the company would chase "other products and markets," and California's DMV sent a cease-and-desist for good measure. Ten years later the products and markets turned out to be 30,000 cars running openpilot on public roads, and the federal government is back exactly where it started, except now the software is open source and anyone with a compiler can edit the braking logic, and you cannot cease-and-desist a fork.

The failure mode itself is the industry's shared nightmare. Teaching a camera-and-radar system to tell a stopped car from a road sign, a guardrail, or an overpass shadow is genuinely hard, and it is the same wall Tesla's Autopilot probes kept hitting, the same one that earned Ford's BlueCruise a public NTSB scolding in March for letting distracted drivers stay distracted through two fatal crashes, because physics does not read changelogs. A stopped emergency vehicle at highway speed is an unforgiving test, and it does not grade on a curve for open-source idealism.

If you run openpilot or one of its forks, today's probe makes your software choices federal evidence. Know exactly which build you flashed, because stock openpilot and a community fork are now different legal universes, and forks can loosen the driver monitoring that stands between you and a stopped fire truck. Aftermarket driver assistance was never crash-tested, never NCAP-rated, never FMVSS-certified the way factory systems are, and there is no recall infrastructure for a fork. None of this means ripping the box off the windshield tonight. It means treating a forked driving stack with the suspicion you would give any safety-critical software written by volunteers: pin your version, read the changelog before updating, and keep your hands near the wheel, because the driver-monitoring camera is only watching you as closely as the fork's author decided was enough.

The universe has a sense of humor, and it is a dark one. A decade ago the government asked a hacker to prove his self-driving box was safe. He open-sourced it instead, and now the government must investigate a product whose manufacturer might be a username. Somewhere in the defect-investigation manual there is a flowchart for this; there is not. That is the whole story, really: the flowchart ends, the road does not, and 30,000 cars are driving on the far side of the map's edge.

What this doesn't prove: crash, death, and injury counts, and Comma's 30,000-vehicle and 402-million-mile figures, are from NHTSA's announcement and the company's claims as reported September 23, 2026. ODI had not published an investigation resume with a case number at press time, and Comma's mileage is unaudited. The 0.75-per-100M-mile figure divides Comma's claimed engaged miles into three deaths and compares against the all-VMT national rate of 1.20; engaged miles skew highway, enthusiast drivers skew careful, and three deaths is a tiny numerator, so treat the comparison as directional. Which crashes ran forked versus stock software is per ODI's preliminary account. FARS does not code aftermarket ADAS engagement, so no FARS cross-tabulation was possible.
