Traffic Deaths Fell 2% in the First Half of 2026. Last Year They Fell 13%.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 5, 2026

Eighteen thousand four hundred sixty people died on American roads in the first six months of 2026. That is the National Safety Council's preliminary count, released September 9, and it is down 2 percent from the revised first-half 2025 total of 18,846.[1] Americans drove 0.8 percent more miles over the same stretch, and the mileage death rate fell to 1.12 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, down 3.4 percent from 1.16.[1]

2%
Decline in first-half 2026 traffic deaths, vs. 13% in first-half 2025

Good news, right? Run the same release from a year ago. First-half 2025 deaths fell 13 percent, and its rate fell 13.5 percent.[3] I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. In twelve months, the improvement rate collapsed by roughly three-quarters, from the largest first-half drop since 2008 to a rounding error with a press release attached.[4]

Here is the arithmetic that makes the stall concrete. Had last year's 13 percent pace held, first-half 2026 would have landed near 16,396 deaths, against an actual count of 18,460, a gap of about 2,064 people who would be alive under last year's pace. Call it a rough counterfactual, not a forecast. Thirteen percent was measured against a different base year, so the gap is approximate, but two thousand lives is still the difference between a trend and a memory.

Underneath the national number, the state table is chaos. California posted the second-best improvement in the country in the first half of 2025, down 43 percent. Twelve months later it is up 14 percent. D.C. went from down 67 percent to up 133 percent. Hawaii flipped from up 46 percent to down 30 percent. Oklahoma, Kansas, West Virginia: same story, same direction, same whiplash.[1][3] Six jurisdictions swung from one extreme list to the other in a single year.

Some of that is noise. D.C. kills roughly 30 to 40 people a year on its roads, so a swing of twenty deaths in six months reads as triple digits. State-level half-year estimates are small numbers wearing big percentages. But noise cuts both ways. If state numbers are too jumpy to crown a winner, they are too jumpy to declare a loser. Only the national series is stable enough to read, and the national series just slowed to a crawl.

One more wrinkle. NHTSA's own first-half estimate was supposed to arrive in late September, according to its first-quarter report.[5] As of November 5, it has not appeared. NSC's number is currently the only national first-half 2026 estimate in existence. That is an observed absence, dated today, not a prediction. When the federal number lands, it will use a different methodology anyway. NHTSA counts deaths within 30 days on public roads. NSC counts deaths within 100 days, including parking lots and driveways. That is why NSC's first-half 2025 count (18,720) runs about 1,600 higher than NHTSA's (17,140).[3][4] Never mix the two series in one sentence. Deceleration survives the caveat because it is built entirely inside the NSC series: 13 percent, then 2 percent.

Fair counter, stated at full strength. A 2 percent decline is still a decline. Sixteen consecutive quarters of improvement since the second quarter of 2022, per NHTSA, and the rate sits 10.4 percent below 2024.[5] You cannot fall 13 percent every year forever. Denominators shrink and easy wins get used up. Fleet turnover and post-pandemic normalization did the heavy lifting in 2024 and 2025, and now the lifting gets harder. Maybe this is regression to the mean, not a stall.

Maybe, but losing three-quarters of your improvement rate in one year, while miles driven kept rising, is a different regime from the 2022-2025 run. Improvement is no longer broad-based: eleven jurisdictions got worse by double digits. Practical read: stop outsourcing your safety judgment to the national headline. Check your own state's trend. Belt use and speed remain the two levers you control, and the 2025 data showed unrestrained occupants down 13 percent and speeding deaths down 11 percent. Those numbers moved because people changed behavior, not because the trend did it for them.

Limitations: All first-half 2026 numbers are preliminary estimates, subject to revision as data matures. NSC's own track record: its initial first-half 2024 estimate ran 3.1 percent above the final count, and its initial first-half 2025 estimate ran 0.7 percent below the provisional count. Treat 18,460 as accurate within a few hundred deaths, not to the person. FARS 2014-2023 cannot speak to 2026; comparisons here use published early estimates, not model-year data.
