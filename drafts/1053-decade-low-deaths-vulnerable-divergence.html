The Gap

Road Deaths Hit a Decade Low in 2026. Bicyclist Deaths Hit a 40-Year High.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 2, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, because the first pass looked like an error in my spreadsheet. NHTSA estimates 7,770 people died in traffic crashes in the first quarter of 2026, down 4.3 percent from a year earlier, at a fatality rate of 0.99 per 100 million miles driven, matching the lowest first-quarter rate since 2014.[1] The full year 2025 landed at 36,640 deaths, down 6.7 percent, the fourth straight annual decline, with the second-lowest annual rate ever recorded.[1] By the arithmetic, 2026 is on pace for a fifth.

Bicyclists killed in 2025, up 4 percent, near the highest level in more than four decades, while total deaths fell 15 percent from the 2021 peak.[3]

The National Safety Council's preliminary half-year tally puts the first six months of 2026 at 18,460 deaths, down 2 percent despite a 0.8 percent rise in miles driven, with a rate of 1.12, down 3.4 percent from 2025 and 10.4 percent from 2024.[2] But the national average is doing a lot of rhetorical heavy lifting here. Thirteen states cut deaths by more than 10 percent (Hawaii at minus 30, Illinois at minus 24), while ten states plus the District of Columbia went the other direction by double digits: DC up 133 percent, Delaware up 24, California up 14.[2] A national decline that contains a state increasing 133 percent is less a trend than a weather report.

And here is the part the trend line refuses to show. While total deaths fell from the 2021 peak of 43,230 to 36,640, pedestrian deaths rose 5 percent from 2020 to 2025 (6,236 to 6,529) and bicyclist deaths rose 29 percent (891 to 1,148).[3] Together they now account for 21 percent of every traffic death in the country, up 8 percent combined over a span when the national total dropped by double digits.[3] Do the subtraction: the ped-and-bike backslide added 550 deaths in 2025 versus 2020, which erased 8.3 percent of the entire 6,590-death decline since the 2021 peak. The roads got safer for everyone inside a steel cage; the people outside it never got the memo.

Motorcyclists live in their own grim category. 5,758 riders died in 2025, up 2 percent from 2020, while motorcycle travel accounted for just 0.7 percent of all miles driven but 16 percent of all deaths.[3] The per-mile fatality rate for motorcyclists was 28 per 100 million miles in 2024, against 0.78 for passenger car occupants: 36 times higher.[3] Helmet non-use among the fatally injured spiked to 29 percent in 2021 and has since fallen back to 18 percent, which is one of those rare moments where the gear chart and the death chart agree with each other.[3]

The strongest case against the celebration is that most of it is regression to the mean. The three pandemic-era years, 2020 through 2022, stacked up 124,751 deaths against a 2019 baseline of 36,355 a year, which works out to roughly 15,700 excess deaths purchased with empty roads, reduced enforcement, and worse behavior behind the wheel.[1] Even now, 2025's 36,640 deaths sit 3 percent above 2015's 35,484, and 2015 was a year nobody described as a golden age of safety.[3] We are not witnessing a breakthrough so much as the long, slow climb back to a plateau we already found unacceptably high.

What can you actually do with this? If you walk or bike, understand that the national trend is not your trend, and route selection dominates your personal risk: fewer than 20 pedestrian or bicyclist deaths per year occur on 20-mph streets, while the most common posted limit in a fatal pedestrian crash is 45 mph and in a fatal bicyclist crash is 55 mph, usually an overtaking driver on an arterial.[5] Take the slow street. If you ride a motorcycle, wear the helmet; the data is not subtle about this. And if you live in one of the states that fell by double digits, the honest question is what Hawaii and Illinois did that Delaware and California did not, because the celebration is premature until we know what we are celebrating.
