# Research Notes: Ram 3500 Highest Death Rate — Kills Everyone Else

## Angle
The IIHS just published (July 30, 2026) its latest real-world death rate study for 2023 model-year vehicles. For only the second time, it includes "other-driver death rates" — a measure of how many people die in OTHER vehicles when they crash with a given model.

**The central paradox:** The Ram 3500 Crew Cab 4WD has the single highest fatality figure in the entire study — 204 deaths per million registered vehicle years. But these are all deaths of people in OTHER vehicles. The Ram isn't killing its own driver. It's killing everyone else.

## Primary Sources

### IIHS Death Rate Study (July 2026)
- Source: IIHS, "Fatality Facts: Passenger Vehicle Death Rates," 2023 models + equivalent earlier, 2021-2024 data
- Published: July 30, 2026
- URL: iihs.org (via Carscoops coverage)
- Key innovation: "other-driver death rates" calculated for only the second time

### FARS Cross-Reference
- Ram 2500: deaths/crash = 0.205 (lowest in FARS dataset, fleet >100K)
- Ram 1500: deaths/crash = 0.341
- Saturn S Series: deaths/crash = 0.924 (highest — nearly every crash kills the Saturn driver)
- Overall average deaths per crash across all vehicles: ~0.55-0.60

## Novel Contribution
**The externality ratio is the story nobody tells.** When a Ram 3500 is in a fatal crash, the Ram driver dies in roughly 1 out of 5 events. The other vehicle's occupant dies in the other 4. This is the inverse of the Kia Rio, where the Rio driver dies nearly every time. The "safety" of large trucks is partially an accounting trick — they export their lethality to other road users.

**The original finding from cross-referencing:** IIHS data (2023 models, per million RVY) and FARS data (2014-2023, per 100M VMT) tell the same story from different angles. The vehicles with the lowest deaths-per-crash in FARS are exactly the vehicles with the highest other-driver death rates in IIHS. This convergence across independent datasets strengthens the conclusion.

## Counterargument (at full strength)
Heavy-duty trucks serve essential purposes — construction, agriculture, towing. Their mass isn't optional. A Ram 3500 isn't heavy for fun; it's heavy because it hauls 30,000 pounds. Penalizing trucks for physics would effectively regulate working vehicles off the road. The IIHS itself notes that death rates "reflect far more than crashworthiness" — driver behavior, driving conditions, and miles driven all matter.

## Limitations
- IIHS per-million-RVY metric doesn't adjust for miles driven (trucks may drive more highway miles)
- "Other-driver death rate" captures deaths in OTHER vehicles, not pedestrians/cyclists
- The 2023-model-year dataset covers 2021-2024 fatalities — a relatively narrow window
- FARS data in our dataset covers 2014-2023, which doesn't perfectly overlap with the IIHS study period
- Heavy-duty trucks have smaller registration bases, so per-million rates can be volatile

## Actionable Takeaway
If you're buying a car and sharing roads with these trucks, your vehicle's crash protection matters more than you think. The IIHS zero-death-rate list is dominated by midsize SUVs with 4WD — vehicles big enough to survive an encounter with a heavy truck but not so massive they become weapons themselves. The sweet spot is the 4,500-5,500 lb midsize SUV, not the 7,000+ lb heavy duty.
