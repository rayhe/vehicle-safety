America's Roads Got Safer, and Illinois Did a Third of the Work

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

I ran the numbers on the National Safety Council's first-half 2026 fatality estimates, and then I ran them again, because the first pass produced a headline the press release didn't. The headline says traffic deaths fell 2 percent nationally, to an estimated 18,460 lives lost from January through June.[1] The spreadsheet says something ruder: Illinois alone accounts for more than a third of the entire national decline.

Here is the arithmetic the press release buried. Thirteen states cut deaths by 10 percent or more and saved a combined 499 lives. Eleven states plus the District of Columbia went the other direction and added 378. Net those two blocs against each other and you get minus 121, which means the celebrated national decline of 386 is not a uniform national improvement but merely what survived a tug-of-war.

And then there is California, the country's most populous state, which added 178 deaths, a 14 percent increase that singlehandedly erased Illinois's entire 134-life contribution and then 44 more. Illinois did the work; California un-did it, with Kentucky adding 49, Alabama 57, and Mississippi 30 of its own. If you live in one of those states, the national press release about declining deaths is describing somebody else's roads.

Read the scary percentages next to their numerators before panicking, though, because the District of Columbia's 133 percent increase is 12 deaths, Rhode Island's 22 percent is 4, and Vermont's 18 percent is 4. Percentages on small denominators are how a quiet half-year in Providence becomes a crisis in a headline, so always read the absolute count.

The tables taught me two more things, the first being that the mileage-adjusted rate is genuinely good news and should not get lost: 1.12 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles, down 3.4 percent from last year and down 10.4 percent from the first half of 2024, with Americans driving 0.8 percent more miles.[1] That is a real exposure-adjusted improvement, and NHTSA's own first-quarter data agrees, with a 0.99 quarterly rate, the lowest since 2014.[3] Second, and this one nags at me: NHTSA's early estimate for the first half of 2025 was 17,140 deaths, while NSC's revised count for the same six months is 18,846. That is a 1,706-death, roughly 10 percent gap on the same country and the same calendar.[1][4] The two organizations count different things, FARS traffic crashes on public roads versus NSC's state-reporter estimates of all motor-vehicle deaths, so part of the "decline" you read about is a function of who is holding the clipboard.

None of this means the progress is fake. Thirteen states with double-digit drops is not nothing, the rate trend is two years of genuinely better, and June's monthly data swung the other way, with 28 states plus DC reporting more deaths than June 2025, which smells like volatility rather than reversal.[2] Before you decide the national decline applies to your commute, look up your state's six-month trend, because the national average is not describing the same road for everyone. The honest headline is not "America got safer." It is "Illinois, Tennessee, and Oklahoma got safer; California, Kentucky, and Alabama did not; the arithmetic netted out positive."
