Drivers 85 and Older Crash More Per Mile Than Teenagers. They're Not Worse Drivers. They're Just More Breakable.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026

Here’s a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Drivers age 85 and older are involved in fatal crashes at a rate of 7.9 per 100 million miles driven. Teenagers, the demographic we built an entire graduated-licensing bureaucracy to restrain, sit at 7.5. The olds have officially lapped the youths. Sit with that.[1]

5,679
People 70 and older killed in crashes in 2024, up 50% since 1975. The per-capita death rate fell 47% over the same span.

Read that twice, because it sounds impossible and it is the entire story. Fifty percent more dead bodies, forty-seven percent less risk per person, both true. Since 1975, the 70-and-older population nearly tripled, from 14.6 million to 41.7 million, and older Americans are keeping their licenses longer and driving more miles than any previous generation. Fifty-nine percent of people 85 and older still held a license in 2020, up from 50 percent in 2000, and across all drivers 65 and older the licensure rate climbed from 78 percent in 2001 to 89 percent in 2021, which means the fastest-growing segment of the driving population is also the most breakable. There are simply more of them out there, which means the absolute toll climbs even as each individual driver gets safer.[1][2] This is what a demographic wave looks like when it hits a guardrail at highway speed: the body count rises because the population rose, not because the driving got worse.

Now the part that actually matters, the part the “take grandpa’s keys” crowd gets entirely backwards while feeling very righteous about it. IIHS researchers decomposed that elevated fatality rate and found it is overwhelmingly not about crashing; it is about dying in the crash. Fragility, meaning the probability of death given that a crash already happened, accounted for 77 percent of the elevated fatality rate for drivers 75 to 79, and 68 percent for drivers 80 and older, relative to middle-aged drivers. What kills them is grimly specific: older chests. Bones, not behavior. Ribcages that fracture under forces a 40-year-old absorbs, plus medical complications that turn survivable injuries into fatal ones.[1][4] A teenager totals a Civic doing something stupid and walks away swearing. An 82-year-old gets T-boned at an intersection at half the speed and does not.

Which reframes the moral panic completely, because the victims are not who you think. In crashes involving a driver 70 or older, 57 percent of the people who died were the older drivers themselves. In crashes involving a driver under 30, only 37 percent of deaths were the young driver; nearly half were occupants of other vehicles, motorcyclists, bicyclists, or pedestrians. Young drivers are a danger to everyone around them, which is exactly why the state makes them log dozens of supervised hours before they solo. Old drivers are, overwhelmingly, a danger to themselves.[1] Confiscating licenses protects the wrong population. The person most at risk from an 85-year-old driver is the 85-year-old driver.

And here is where it becomes a design problem instead of a fate problem. Riding around in the least protective cars: the most breakable drivers on the road. IIHS found that drivers in their 70s and older are significantly more likely than middle-aged drivers to be piloting vehicles at least 16 years old, smaller sedans instead of SUVs, cars without electronic stability control, cars without head-protecting side airbags. That nostalgia carries a quantifiable penalty: no ESC means 37 percent higher odds of driver fatality for the 70-plus crowd, and no standard side airbags doubles the odds. Drivers 75 and older are about four times as likely to die as middle-aged drivers in a side-impact crash.[3] The “retirement vehicle,” the paid-off sedan someone intends to drive for the rest of their life, is the worst possible machine for the body driving it.

One more number, because intersections deserve their own warning label. Among drivers 80 and older in fatal crashes, 43 percent were multiple-vehicle intersection crashes, compared with about 24 percent for drivers 20 to 64. Left turns across traffic, gap misjudgment, the whole choreography of the four-way stop becomes the single deadliest thing an old driver does.[1]

What to do: If you are over 70, or love someone who is, the highest-leverage safety decision is not a driving test. It is the car, not the calendar. Electronic stability control, side curtain airbags, a larger and heavier vehicle with a top IIHS rating: those three choices attack the fragility penalty directly, and side airbags in particular help older occupants disproportionately, which is exactly the population driving the cars least likely to have them. Retire the 16-year-old sedan before it retires you. Plan routes around protected left turns, and treat any used-car purchase for an aging parent as a safety purchase first: run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because older cars have had more years to accumulate unrepaired recalls.[2][3]

The honest caveats, because the data deserves them. That 85-plus per-mile rate rests on a small mileage denominator from survey data, so treat 7.9 as fuzzy. Cicchino’s fragility decomposition comes from 2005-to-2008 data, and the fleet is safer now. And the intersection numbers are a reminder that this is not purely fragility: gap misjudgment is a real driving error that rises with age. It is both. They crash somewhat more per mile, and they die far more per crash. Everybody involved in this debate, from state legislators to the adult children having the keys conversation at the kitchen table, should hold both facts at once, because the policy that follows from only one of them is wrong.
