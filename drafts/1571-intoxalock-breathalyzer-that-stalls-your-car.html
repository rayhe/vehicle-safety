The Breathalyzer That Can Stall Your Car

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 24, 2026

On November 19, 2026, federal regulators opened a file on a device whose entire job is keeping drunk drivers off the road. NHTSA's Defect Petition DP26014 targets the Intoxalock Model 1001A ignition interlock, installed in an estimated 150,000 cars, over allegations that the breathalyzer can stall the engine while driving, cut power steering and braking assist, and distract drivers during rolling retests.[1]

Incidents in NHTSA's own failure report summary for DP26014. Zero crashes, zero injuries, zero deaths.

Start with what the petition actually claims, because the distinction matters. Mouthpieces can detach, and the device can fail to register a breath sample during a rolling retest, which means a driver is fumbling with a handset at highway speed while the car demands a second try. Excessive current draw while the vehicle sits parked can drain the battery flat, leaving the car unable to start. Consumer accounts cited in the petition go further: engine stalling while driving, loss of steering and braking assistance, delayed restart, crashes and near-crashes blamed on the shutdown or the retest.[1]

Now the number that cuts the other way. NHTSA's own failure report summary for DP26014 counts exactly one incident. Zero crashes, zero injuries, zero deaths.[1] A defect petition is not an investigation, an investigation is not a defect finding, and most petitions get denied. Interlocks work: they cut drunk-driving recidivism while installed, and drunk driving kills roughly 10,000 Americans a year. A rolling retest is a nuisance for a sober driver, and a dead battery is a tow bill.

But one incident is not the whole record, and the whole record is the story. Virginia's Commission on VASAP terminated Intoxalock's vendor contract in March 2023 and moved 1,692 clients to other vendors in 90 days.[2] Commission meeting materials show the company held about 19.5 percent of Virginia's interlock business while accounting for 96 percent of the state's interlock complaints since November 2022. Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel issued a cease-and-desist the same month over mailers designed to look like court documents, calling the practice "the lowest form of deceit," and closed the matter in May after the mailer changed.[3] A federal lawsuit alleging battery damage went to arbitration in August 2024, so the merits were never decided.[5] In March 2026 the devices went dark nationwide, with Oklahoma's Board of Tests calling it a nationwide service disruption and local reporting counting impacted devices in 45 states, while the company disclosed a cybersecurity event the same month.[4] A California class action over recorded calls settled for $1,747,500.[6]

So here is what to do. If you have a 1001A in your car, keep a dated log of every failed retest, lockout, stall, or dead battery, because the petition exists only because someone filed paperwork, and NHTSA's Vehicle Owner Questionnaire accepts complaints about equipment, not just vehicles.[7] If a rolling retest fails while you are moving, pull over before retrying. A missed retest is a violation, but a crash is worse. If the car will not start after sitting, keep the tow receipt and the service record. And ask your state's monitoring authority whether Intoxalock is still an approved vendor. Virginia's answer, since 2023, is no.
