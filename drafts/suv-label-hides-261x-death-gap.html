The SUV Label Hides a 261x Death Rate Gap

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
August 17, 2026

A Chevrolet Tracker and a Tesla Model Y are both classified as SUVs. NHTSA calls them SUVs. IIHS calls them SUVs. Your insurance company calls them SUVs. One has a fatality rate of 7.83 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled. The other: 0.03.[1] That is not a rounding difference. That is a 261-to-1 gap inside a single vehicle category.

3.8×
Weighted average death rate of body-on-frame SUVs vs. unibody crossovers in FARS data, 2014 to 2023.

Sort every SUV in the FARS database by construction type and the pattern is immediate. Eighteen body-on-frame models average 1.45 per 100 million VMT. A hundred and three unibody crossovers average 0.38. Frame-on-body trucks wearing an SUV badge killed 19,464 people across the decade. Crossovers killed 26,978, but from a fleet roughly four times larger.[1]

Same-manufacturer comparisons should make you uncomfortable. Ford sells both the Expedition (2.31 per 100M VMT) and the Bronco Sport (0.05). A 46x gap within the same showroom. Toyota sells the Land Cruiser (6.27) and the RAV4 (0.19). Chevrolet built the Tracker (7.83) and the Traverse (0.20). Both vehicles earned the same three-letter designation on their window sticker.[1]

46×
Death rate gap between Ford's deadliest and safest SUVs: Expedition vs. Bronco Sport.

Physics explains part of it. Body-on-frame SUVs sit on truck chassis with higher centers of gravity and rollover propensity that NHTSA has flagged for decades.[2] Unibody crossovers are structurally closer to sedans, with crumple zones that absorb impact energy rather than transmit it through a rigid ladder frame. But fleet age matters enormously too. The Tracker was discontinued in 2004. These are 17- to 22-year-old vehicles accumulating fatalities against shrinking VMT denominators, inflating their per-mile rate. Comparing a 2004 Tracker to a 2020 Model Y is comparing different eras of crash engineering. The 261x number is the ceiling, not the central estimate. The weighted 3.8x gap is the honest comparison.

Counterargument: You could argue the gap is mostly age, not architecture. Older vehicles are cheaper, attract higher-risk drivers, lack modern safety tech. All true. But the Jeep Grand Cherokee (body-on-frame, 0.51) and Jeep Cherokee (unibody, 1.73) invert the pattern entirely, with the unibody killing at 3.4x the BOF rate. No single variable explains everything. But "SUV" containing both a 7.83 and a 0.03 in the same regulatory category means the label communicates nothing about occupant risk.

What to do with this: If you are shopping for an SUV, the category name is not a safety specification. Check the specific model's IIHS rating and NHTSA star rating. Avoid aging body-on-frame SUVs if crash survivability matters: 2000s-era Tahoes, Yukons, Expeditions, and TrailBlazers kill at rates closer to motorcycles than modern crossovers. If you own one, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls.

Limitations: FARS captures only fatal crashes; a low fatality rate may mask high injury rates. VMT estimates use fleet-level calculations, not odometer data, introducing ~15% uncertainty for low-volume models. Body-on-frame vs. unibody classification is simplified. Tracker and Land Cruiser rates are inflated by small surviving fleets. This analysis does not control for driver age, income, geography, or road type.
