Existential Dread

Motorcycles Were the Only Vehicle That Got Deadlier Per Mile in 2023

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
Sunday, November 7, 2027

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. In 2023, every kind of vehicle in America got safer per mile traveled, with passenger cars improving 5.8%, light trucks 8.3%, and the big rigs a full 12.1%. Motorcycles went the other way, up 19.4%, the only vehicle type on the chart moving toward the grave instead of away from it.[1] One of these is not like the others.

+19.4%
Motorcycle occupant fatality rate per 100M VMT, 2022 to 2023. Deaths rose 1.3% while the miles estimate fell 15.1%.

Before you sell the Harley, look at the body count. Motorcyclist deaths went from 6,251 to 6,335 in 2023, an increase of 84 people, or 1.3%.[1] In a year when 40,901 Americans died on the roads, 84 is a rounding error with a funeral attached. The 19.4% rate spike came entirely from the other side of the fraction: the federal estimate of motorcycle travel collapsed 15.1%, from 23.8 billion miles to 20.2 billion.[1] Divide nearly the same number of deaths by far fewer miles, and arithmetic manufactures a crisis.

Now follow the denominator, because this is where it gets properly existential. Registered motorcycles barely moved, from 9.19 million to 9.52 million, which means the average American bike supposedly dropped from 2,587 miles a year to 2,121, a fall of 18% in twelve months.[1] Did riders really change their habits 18% in a single year? Did a fifth of the nation's motorcycling simply evaporate? Or does the Federal Highway Administration's motorcycle travel estimate swing wildly, plus 21% one year and minus 15% the next, because its traffic sensors were built for cars and barely register a bike? Consider that the famous "28 times deadlier than a car" figure, the one this very site has quoted, rides on that same wobbling ruler. In 2022 the identical math said 21.9 to 1.[1] The risk did not change 28% in a year; the ruler did.

Full-strength objection, as required by the laws of dread. Maybe the miles are real, and perhaps the COVID riding boom faded, the casuals parked their bikes, and the riders who remain are the hardcore core: the unlicensed, the unhelmeted, the ones for whom lane-splitting is a personality. Thirty-five percent of riders killed in 2023 were not wearing helmets, and 26% had been drinking.[1] If total riding fell while the surviving riding got riskier, then the 19.4% is not an artifact at all. The published numbers cannot tell you which story is true, and that ambiguity is the actual story.

What this does not prove, stated plainly: it does not prove riding got safer, and it certainly does not prove it got more dangerous. FARS counts the dead; the FHWA estimates the miles with sensors that miss bikes; injury rates by vehicle type live in a separate dataset I did not open. The honest read is that the motorcycle per-mile rate has sat roughly flat in the high twenties while cars, trucks, and big rigs all improved, which is its own quieter horror: everything else keeps getting safer while motorcycles just do not.

So what do you do with any of this? If you ride, the baseline was always the whole argument. Helmet, license, assume invisibility, because 35% unhelmeted is a choice, not a mystery. If you make policy, stop steering motorcycle countermeasure money off year-to-year per-mile rates built on a denominator that swings 20%; fund real exposure data, odometer-based, and trust multi-year death counts until then. And if you quote "28 times deadlier" at dinner parties, know that in 2022 it was 22. The number is true enough, and it is also made of sand.
