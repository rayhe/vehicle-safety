Sobriety Report

Two BlueCruise Crashes, Two Drunk Drivers, Zero Braking

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 12, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, the NTSB's new BlueCruise investigation reads less like a technology failure than a bar tab with a body count. Two 2022 Mustang Mach-Es, both hands-free on the highway, drove into stationary vehicles nine days apart in early 2024.[1] Three people died, and neither the cars nor their drivers braked.

San Antonio, February 24, about 9:50 at night: a Mach-E in BlueCruise mode doing roughly 74 mph down the center lane of I-10 meets a 1999 Honda CR-V stopped in that lane. Its driver, 56, carried a femoral blood ethanol of 0.326 g/dL, four times the Texas limit.[1] The report's phrasing: the CR-V "may have been stopped in the center lane of the highway due to the impairment of its driver." Translation: a man got blackout drunk and parked in a travel lane.

The part the headline writers missed: the Mach-E driver was sober. Police found no intoxication; he told officers he was following his navigation to a charging station and was "definitely paying attention to the road," then declined the NTSB's interview through his attorney.[1] Nobody watched the road, the one job BlueCruise still assigns to the human.

0.326
The Honda driver's blood ethanol, in g/dL; Texas presumes impairment at 0.08.

Nine days later, March 3, 3:20 a.m.: a second Mach-E doing roughly 72 in a 45-mph work zone on I-95 hits a stopped Elantra and Prius, killing both drivers. Her hospital draw three and a half hours later read 0.126 g/dL, putting her at an estimated 0.14 to 0.19 behind the wheel, plus 7 ng/mL of delta-9-THC and a phone held on top of the steering wheel,[1] which the monitoring camera logged as "eyes forward" the whole time.

72 in a 45
The Philadelphia Mach-E's speed, in mph, in a work zone the system allowed.

The machine's autopsy lists five design choices: drivers could disable automatic emergency braking while BlueCruise ran; adaptive cruise didn't have to run concurrently; hands-free was allowed in work zones; cruise tolerance could be set 20 mph over posted; and the driver monitor couldn't tell a phone in the forward line of sight from road attention.[1] No braking or steering, by driver or by system, was recorded before either impact.

Ford learned of the San Antonio crash two weeks later from an online forum post, and of the Philadelphia crash a month later, when the NTSB called.[1] Of 35 driver-assistance crash reports Ford filed with NHTSA from 2021 through 2025, effectively zero were phoned in by the car itself.

The NTSB's counterfactual: at 74 mph, braking at 1g from 146 feet cuts impact speed to about 39 mph, and modeled serious-or-fatal injury probability falls from 76 percent to under 1 percent.[3][5] At the posted 45 in Philadelphia, a compliant system would have had to miss the Elantra entirely. Both crashes were survivable, but neither car even tried.

The NTSB wants cars that phone home when they crash, recorders that log the automation's actions, monitors that can tell a phone from the road, and passive alcohol detection in every new vehicle.[2] Credit where due: the humans failed first and hardest, and no Level 2 system promises to rescue that. But Ford sold a system that lets you switch off the brakes, run 20 over, and drive hands-free through a work zone, and IIHS research cited in the report found these systems don't cut crash rates versus plain automatic braking.[4] Convenience features in a safety costume.

Mach-E owners: verify automatic emergency braking is on before engaging hands-free, check your adaptive-cruise speed-tolerance setting, and skip the system in work zones. Assume it cannot see a stopped car at highway speed; twice now, it couldn't. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls.[7]
