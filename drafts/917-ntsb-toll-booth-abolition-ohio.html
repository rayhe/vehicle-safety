1,099 Near-Misses in Three Weeks. Now the NTSB Says the Toll Booth Has to Go.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 17, 2026

At 5:29 on an August morning, a 2015 Toyota Highlander carrying a driver and three passengers slowed down in the left lane of Interstate 80, swung across the path of a 2019 Mack truck, and aimed itself at a toll booth. The truck could not stop, and the trailer overturned. Two passengers died, two more people were seriously injured, and sulfonic acid spilled across the Ohio Turnpike.[1]

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. Video of that same toll plaza over three weeks showed 1,099 last-minute lane changes just like it, an average of 52 a day, with drivers illegally crossing the painted gore between the high-speed lanes and the booth lanes.[2] The August crash was not a freak event. It was one losing ticket drawn from a raffle the plaza runs 52 times a day.

52 per day
Illegal last-minute lane changes at the Swanton Township toll plaza over a three-week NTSB video review

Here is the geometry, and it deserves your full attention. The eastbound approach to the Swanton Township toll plaza runs two high-speed toll lanes at a 70 mph limit beside two lanes that funnel into manual toll booths, and the only thing separating them at first is paint, which transitions to a concrete wall as the plaza closes in.[1] Booths collect money, but this one was manufacturing a 70-to-near-zero speed differential on an interstate and selling the public 1,099 chances a month to experience it up close.

On September 16, the National Transportation Safety Board finished its homework and turned in a demolition order. Nine new safety recommendations, addressed to the Federal Highway Administration, the Ohio Turnpike and Infrastructure Commission, Springfield Township, and the toll industry's trade association, call for fully electronic tolling and the permanent closure of low-speed lanes at four named Ohio plazas: Westgate, Swanton Township, Newton Falls, and Eastgate.[3] The board says the crash could have been avoided if the turnpike had used electronic tolling and told drivers they could pay missed tolls online. Nobody had told the Highlander driver, and that missing sign is in the official probable cause.

Missing from the press conference: Illinois and Pennsylvania have already gone fully electronic on their stretches of the I-80 turnpike.[2] Ohio is the holdout on the same road. "This crash shows the deadly safety consequences of mixing high-speed electronic tolling with low-speed manual tolling lanes," said NTSB Chairwoman Jennifer Homendy. Translation: your neighboring states deleted the booths and your state kept the raffle.

Fairness requires the defense to get its turn, and the defense is not nothing. The NTSB pinned probable cause on the SUV driver's deceleration and lane change, with fatigue likely dulling the driver's awareness at half past five in the morning.[3] A tired driver can cross a lane line anywhere, booth or no booth. And the deadliest variable inside the cabin may be the one the board has been pleading about since 2014: the two passengers who died were not wearing seat belts, while the belted occupants survived a collision with an 80,000-pound truck.[2] Ohio still lacks primary-enforcement seat-belt coverage for every seating position, which is why the NTSB dusted off its 2014 recommendation and stapled it to the new ones. You can demolish every toll booth in the state and still lose unbelted, exhausted drivers to the interstate.

One more body count, because the morning kept going. From the initial crash a traffic queue formed, and more crashes occurred within it; contemporaneous reporting put that morning's Ohio Turnpike toll at four dead across three separate crashes involving thirteen vehicles.[4] The booth's ledger for August 15 was four lives, not two: somebody's $3 toll.

What to do with this, practically, is to treat every hybrid toll plaza, in Ohio and the shrinking list of holdouts, as a billing event rather than an emergency when you miss your lane. Pay it online, because that is the sign Swanton never posted and not posting it is officially part of the probable cause.[3] Never cross a painted gore at highway speed. And buckle every passenger in every seat, because the board's own data says the difference between the dead and the survivors in this crash was a belt, not a booth.

It was designed to collect money from cars that had stopped. Nobody designed what happens to the cars that must cross 70 mph traffic to reach it. The NTSB just did the math 1,099 times, and demolition is the remedy.
