Waymo’s Fix for Passing School Buses Didn’t Fix Passing School Buses

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 15, 2026

Waymo fixed its school bus problem on November 17, 2025. That was the date the company says its software remedy reached every affected robotaxi in the fleet. On November 6, 2026, NHTSA opened a recall query into 2,895 of those same cars, because a majority of the school bus violations in its own investigation happened after that date.[1] A fix is supposed to end a problem. This one filed the paperwork and kept going.

of the 3,067 Waymos in the December 2025 school bus recall are back under federal investigation: 2,895 cars, nearly the entire recalled fleet

Start with what the cars actually did, because Waymo’s own paperwork is unusually honest about it. Robotaxis saw the school buses, slowed down or stopped, and then drove past them anyway, because the software decided the car was impeding a priority vehicle and moved to get out of the way.[3] Read that again. A machine built to be the safest driver on the road looked at a stopped school bus with flashing red lights and an extended stop arm, weighed the law against the risk of mildly inconveniencing the traffic behind it, and chose traffic.

That behavior arrived with an August 20, 2025 software release that changed how the system predicts what other road users intend to do. Waymo pushed a hotfix between November 5 and 17, 2025, teaching the cars to stop putting “don’t impede other vehicles” ahead of staying stopped for a school bus. Its Safety Board formally decided on a recall on December 2. Filing 25E084 landed on December 10, covering 3,067 fifth-generation vehicles, with Waymo reporting 12 citations tied to the behavior and no collisions.[3]

Here is the detail that makes the timeline strange. Recall 25E084 was filed 23 days after Waymo said the fix was already in every car, with no dealer visits and no owner letters. Waymo owns the fleet, so the repair was finished before the paperwork admitting the defect existed. A recall, in the old sense, is a manufacturer telling owners to bring their cars in. This one was a manufacturer telling the government about a repair it had already performed on cars it already owned.

A recall query is not a recall; it is the step after one, and it starts from a defect the manufacturer already admitted. NHTSA opens a query when it wants to know whether the fix was good enough, or whether the recall needs to cover more. Its own sentence does the damage: a majority of the incidents identified in the earlier evaluation occurred after the November 17, 2025 remedy date.[1] Three hundred fifty-four days passed between the fix being declared complete and the query being opened. Nearly a full year of school buses, and nobody in the loop could say, with evidence, that the problem was over.

Atlanta station WXIA broadcast footage in September 2025 of a Waymo steering around a stopped school bus, and NHTSA opened its preliminary evaluation on October 17 after an Atlanta incident where a Waymo failed to remain stopped for a bus with red lights flashing, stop arm deployed, and crossing control arm deployed.[4] Atlanta Public Schools confirmed six stop-arm violations between May and December 2025. In one, the car passed the bus only moments after a student had walked directly in front of it, while the child was still on the road.[4] Austin’s school district documented 19 incidents, including one where a Waymo stopped briefly and then pulled forward while a student was still crossing in front of it.[5] Austin fined Waymo $300 per violation, counted its 20th citation by December 1, and formally asked the company to halt operations during peak bus hours. A school district asked a robotaxi company to stay away from children. Nineteen violations came to $5,700, less than a single sensor on the roof of one car.

Then came January 12, 2026, fifty-six days after the fix was declared complete. An empty Waymo was the first vehicle to stop for a school bus loading students on East Oltorf Street in Austin. Uncertain, the car asked its remote assistance team whether this was a school bus with active signals. An agent in Novi, Michigan, answered “No,” and the car drove on and passed the bus with its stop arms still extended, one of six vehicles to pass that bus that morning, and no crash occurred.[2] Five of them had human drivers, which means the robot set the example and the humans followed it. A separate Austin incident on January 14 involved a bus on a special-needs route, and the NTSB investigation is still open.[2]

There is a second, separate probe. In January 2026, a Waymo vehicle struck a child near an elementary school in Santa Monica, and NHTSA and the NTSB opened investigations into that collision. Its findings are not public, and it is not part of the school bus query. Do not merge the two in your head; the regulator has not.[4]

Waymo’s answer deserves a fair hearing. Its spokesperson told The Verge the company has made significant improvements to vehicle behavior around school buses and will respond to the query, and it told TechCrunch it has received no school bus citations since March 2026.[6] NHTSA, for its part, had made no finding as of November 9. A recall query is a question, not a verdict. January 12 was a human in Michigan answering a yes-or-no question wrong, not necessarily the driving code the recall patched, and the fleet’s overall crash record compares favorably with human drivers.

Grant all of that, and it still leaves the central fact untouched. A majority of the incidents NHTSA counted happened after the remedy date, and that is the regulator’s count, not a critic’s. A fix that leaves a second failure mode in place, one where a child’s safety depends on a remote worker answering a question correctly from a thousand miles away, is a system with a new weak point wearing the old fix’s name. We built a car that can see a school bus, stop for a school bus, ask a human about a school bus, and still drive past it. Somewhere in that chain, everyone did their job, and the bus still got passed.

What to do: if a driverless car passes a stopped school bus, report it. File a Vehicle Owner Questionnaire at nhtsa.gov, the same channel that fed the original evaluation, and notify the school district. Austin ISD’s letter to NHTSA carried weight a single complaint never would. Parents near a school zone should not assume a robotaxi will stop for a bus, because the law applies to the vehicle whether or not anyone sits in the driver’s seat. Texas Transportation Code 545.066 makes no exception for a car with no driver, and neither does any other state’s version of the same law. Waymo riders: the company’s own record says the cars stop, then sometimes proceed. If your robotaxi approaches a stopped bus and starts moving, speak up. The remote assistant is listening, and on January 12, it answered wrong.

What this does not prove: the query is an investigation, not a finding, and it can close with no action or end in a second recall. NHTSA has not published the total incident count, so no exact post-remedy failure rate can be computed; “a majority” has no public denominator. Counts in coverage are not interchangeable: twelve citations is Waymo’s count for September 25 to November 14, 2025, nineteen incidents is Austin ISD’s count, five of them post-update, six is Atlanta’s, and eight is the NHTSA resume’s summary figure, so do not add them. Waymo’s claim of no citations since March 2026 is self-reported and unverified in public records. Santa Monica’s child-strike probe is separate, its findings are not public, and it proves nothing about the school bus defect. January 12 may be a different defect than the one the recall patched, which is exactly what the query exists to determine.
