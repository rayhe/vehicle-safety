Stellantis Killed Electronic Stability Control Three Times in 2026

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 2, 2027

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better.

Electronic stability control is the single most effective safety system ever mandated. NHTSA estimated it would save 5,300 to 10,300 lives a year at full fleet penetration, and its own follow-up research put the reduction in fatal single-vehicle crashes at 55 percent for cars and 50 percent for light trucks.[3] Every light vehicle built since September 2011 has to carry it. In 2026, one company managed to lose it three times.

3
ESC-loss recall campaigns filed in 2026. Every one of them Stellantis.

Here's how the count works: I queried the NHTSA recalls API across 64 high-volume model and model-year combinations and pulled 77 unique 2026 campaigns. Exactly three involved the loss of electronic stability control. The manufacturers, in order: Chrysler. Chrysler. Chrysler. Ford: zero. GM: zero. Toyota, Honda, Hyundai, Kia, Nissan, Subaru, Mazda, Volkswagen, Tesla: zero. The entire rest of the industry kept its stability control. Stellantis lost it in April, lost it again in September, and lost it a third time in October.

The April recall covered 6,605 Ram 2500 pickups. A steering column control module from supplier Kostal of America could fail internally and take the whole ESC system with it, a direct violation of Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 126. The failure is silent: per the reports on the April campaign, the ESC warning light only illuminates after the module has already failed and reset. Your dashboard stays dark while the safety net dissolves.

16,951
Vehicles across the three campaigns, combined

The September recall covered 2,017 Chrysler Voyagers and Pacificas. The culprit was a cracked ceramic capacitor in the brake pedal position sensor, a component worth cents. We covered that one in September, and the frame holds: every ESC failure in the mandate era is an electronics ghost.

The October recall is the one that should make you sit up. Campaign 26V651, filed October 6, covers 8,329 more Ram 2500s with the same steering column module defect. It expands 26V244, the April recall — read that sentence again. The expansion is bigger than the original. Six months after Stellantis told 6,605 owners their trucks could lose stability control, it found 8,329 more carrying the same module. The first net missed more fish than it caught. The suspect build window runs September 13 to November 15, 2025, and the part numbers are 68600228AH, 68600229AH, and 68600231AH.

The fair counterargument goes like this. Recall expansions are the system working, not failing. Stellantis traced the module to more build dates and filed voluntarily. The NHTSA record lists the initiator as the manufacturer, not the regulator, and no crashes or injuries are tied to the October campaign in the public record. Sixteen thousand nine hundred fifty-one vehicles is also a rounding error against roughly 16 million new vehicles sold in America each year. All true. But ESC's entire value proposition is that it works silently, every trip, for a decade or more. A defect that silently disables it is the single failure mode the 2007 mandate cannot survive, and three separate defect modes in nine months at one company is a pattern, not bad luck.

If you own a 2026 Ram 2500, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now. The October campaign's VINs became searchable on October 13, 2026, and owner letters go out November 24. Ask the dealer to confirm the replacement module isn't one of the three suspect part numbers. If you own a 2025 or 2026 Voyager or Pacifica, check for 26V561. And the general rule: an ESC warning light that flickers once and goes dark is not a glitch you outgrew. On these trucks it is the only signal the failure gives, so get it scanned.

What this doesn't prove. The census is sample-based: 64 high-volume model and model-year queries through the NHTSA API, not an exhaustive flat-file sweep, so a small-volume model could hide another ESC campaign. I state populations, not defect rates, because the API record doesn't publish the estimated-defect percentage for 26V651. The 6,605 figure for the April recall comes from press reporting of the filing. And no, I can't tell you whether Stellantis's ESC problem is engineering or supply chain. Kostal made the module, Stellantis bought it, and the capacitor came from somewhere further down. The mandate doesn't care whose fault it is.
