# Research Notes — #989 "The Missing Immobilizer"

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
Hyundai and Kia shipped ~9 million US cars (2011-2022) without engine immobilizers that Canadian and Mexican buyers got as standard. A December 2025 settlement with 35 state AGs now forces free retrofits and mandates immobilizers on all future US vehicles: the federal safety standard NHTSA never wrote, achieved by attorneys general.

## Original findings (the "novel contribution")
1. **The $55/car arithmetic**: $500M retrofit / 9M vehicles ≈ $55 per vehicle. A factory-fit immobilizer costs less than a retrofit (no dealer labor, no zinc sleeve R&D). The omission saved Hyundai/Kia less than ~$55/car while generating a $500M+ liability plus settlements. Show the math.
2. **Regulation by settlement**: NHTSA has never mandated immobilizers in the US (unlike the EU, which required them since 1998, and Canada). 35 AGs just created a de-facto national immobilizer mandate for the two largest Korean automakers without a single FMVSS rulemaking. Contrast with NHTSA's stalled mandates (congressional breathalyzer deadline blown, #942).
3. **The missing body count**: FARS does not record whether a crash vehicle was stolen. The "14 crashes, 8 fatalities" figure is from 2023 regulators and predates the worst years; there is no official count of people killed by stolen-car crashes. The data gap is the story's honest limit.

## Limitations (dedicated accounting)
- No official count of deaths/injuries in stolen-vehicle crashes exists; FARS has no "stolen" flag. The 14/8 figure is a 2023 regulatory statement and certainly an undercount, but I cannot quantify by how much.
- Theft counts (NICB) measure stolen vehicles, not crashes; most stolen cars are recovered or stripped without a crash.
- The settlement covers 2011-2022 key-start vehicles; push-button-start models already had immobilizers.
- I infer factory-fit cost < retrofit cost from economics, not from a Hyundai/Kia cost disclosure.

## Strongest counterargument
The automakers complied with every FMVSS on the books; no US regulation required immobilizers, and millions of older cars from every brand lack them. The theft wave needed three ingredients: the missing part, viral how-to videos, and willing teen joyriders. Pinning the crashes solely on a boardroom cost decision ignores the social-media accelerant and the drivers who chose to steal and crash the cars. Also: immobilizers are not theft-proof (relay attacks defeat keyless systems), so the "mandate" fixes one vulnerability, not car theft.

## Actionable takeaways (HARD GATE)
- Own a 2011-2022 Hyundai or Kia with a turn-key (non-push-button) ignition? You are almost certainly eligible for the FREE zinc-sleeve ignition protector + software upgrade. Check the settlement site / Hyundai/Kia recall lookup with your VIN.
- One-year clock: you have one year from the date of the companies' notice to get the repair free. Repairs are available early 2026 through early 2027. Do not wait for the letter; check your VIN now.
- Got the earlier software update? You may STILL need the hardware fix; the settlement explicitly covers vehicles previously only eligible for software.
- Park in lit areas, lock up; a steering-wheel club remains a cheap visual deterrent while unpatched cars are still being hit at ~5x the all-makes theft rate.
