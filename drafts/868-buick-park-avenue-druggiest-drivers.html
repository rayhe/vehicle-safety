The Druggiest Drivers in America Drive a Buick

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 10, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, the most drug-positive drivers in America are not in Hellcats or WRXs but in Buicks. Across a decade of FARS toxicology data covering 307 nameplates, the Buick Park Avenue ranks first in drug-positive drivers: 16.6% of its drivers in fatal crashes tested positive for drugs, nearly double the sedan median of 9.1%.[1] Runner-up is also a Buick: the Verano, at 13.1%. The beige sedan was not on anyone's suspect list.

It also tops the alcohol leaderboard at 24.3%, making it the only nameplate to wear both crowns at once. Below the crown, though, the two rankings split apart. A vehicle's alcohol-positive rate and its drug-positive rate correlate at just 0.50, a polite way of saying these are different populations. Ten of the twenty most drug-positive models do not even crack the top forty for alcohol: the Verano, the Suzuki Grand Vitara, the Nissan NV200 delivery van, the Acura RDX. The alcohol leaderboard is dominated by discontinued cheap cars; the drug list includes vehicles still in production. Different leaderboard, different story.

Any non-alcohol drug detected in the toxicology panel counts as drug-positive, including prescription medications and THC metabolites that linger for days or weeks after any impairing effect fades. In 2018, FARS removed its three-drug reporting cap, after which detected drug use among fatally injured drivers jumped from 39.9% to 55.0%. The drivers did not change; the spreadsheet did.[3] Then there is NHTSA's own case-control study, the largest ever on drug crash risk: after adjusting for age, gender, and alcohol, THC-positive drivers showed no increased crash risk at all.[2] Read that again. Even the federal government's best study could not find the risk.

Now consider who drives a Park Avenue. Its last model year was 2005, and Buick spent decades as the brand your grandfather drove to church. Stack an elderly buyer base against a definition that sweeps in prescriptions, and the druggiest car in America starts to look like a census of medicine cabinets, not a recklessness ranking. Give the counterargument full volume: the alcohol number is unambiguous. A 24.3% alcohol-positive rate is the highest in the nation, and alcohol is impairment, not a metabolite. Whatever the drug story is, Park Avenue drivers remain the most impaired drivers in America by the measure that matters. Nearly one in three tested positive for something. That is not a rounding error.

If you drive a 2000s Buick (Park Avenue, LeSabre, Lucerne) or care for someone who does, the safety conversation is a medication review with a doctor or pharmacist, not a breathalyzer. Sedating prescriptions and their interactions are the under-discussed impairment vector on American roads. Next time a headline screams about drug-positive drivers, ask what it actually measured, because presence is not impairment. Toxicology reports tell you what was in the blood; they never tell you why the car crashed.

Sources & References

  1. NHTSA, Fatality Analysis Reporting System (FARS), 2014–2023 per-model toxicology data (307 nameplates; Buick Park Avenue: n=259 drivers, 16.6% drug-positive, 24.3% alcohol-positive, 31.7% any-positive). nhtsa.gov
  2. NHTSA, Drug and Alcohol Crash Risk Study (2015), Virginia Beach case-control study: THC-positive drivers had an unadjusted crash odds ratio of 1.25, falling to 1.05 after demographic adjustment and 1.0 after also controlling for alcohol — no increased population crash risk. nhtsa.gov
  3. Chihuri & Li (2026), “Impact of the enhanced Fatality Analysis Reporting System on drug detection in fatally injured drivers,” Accident Analysis & Prevention, Vol. 226. In 2018 FARS removed its three-drug reporting cap; any non-alcohol drug detection rose from 39.9% to 55.0%. doi.org
  4. NHTSA, Report to Congress: Marijuana-Impaired Driving (July 2017): THC crash-risk estimates of 1–3x, “similar to drivers with BAC levels between .01 to < 0.05,” with wide confidence intervals. nhtsa.gov

Source: NHTSA FARS 2014–2023. Drug-positive indicates detection, not impairment: panels include prescription medications and long-lived THC metabolites, and testing rates vary by state. The prescription-medication interpretation of the Park Avenue result is inference from buyer demographics, not a finding of the toxicology panel. See methodology for caveats.
