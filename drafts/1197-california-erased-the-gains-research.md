# Research: #1197 — Nine States Saved 457 Lives. California Gave Back 338 of Them.

## Story angle
NSC's August 2026 preliminary fatality estimates (published ~Oct 12, 2026) show American roads getting safer at the national level — August deaths down 9% year-over-year, mileage death rate down to 1.17 from 1.41 three Augusts ago — while California, the country's most populous state, moved hard in the opposite direction: +18% year-to-date, +338 deaths, the largest absolute increase of any state. Original arithmetic: the nine best-improving states saved a combined 457 lives YTD; California's 338 erased 74% of that. The state with the strictest traffic-safety reputation is single-handedly eating the nation's progress.

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? Yes — fresh federal-adjacent data (Oct 12), a real paradox (national improvement vs. biggest-state deterioration), and a clean original calculation (74%).
- Novel angle? Yes — nobody has run the "California vs. the nine best states" arithmetic; coverage of NSC monthly estimates is usually national-only.

## Primary sources (4)
1. **NSC Injury Facts, August 2026 preliminary estimates** (injuryfacts.nsc.org/motor-vehicle/overview/preliminary-monthly-estimates/): August 2026 = 3,440 deaths (-9% vs Aug 2025, -12% vs Aug 2024); mileage -0.6% YoY; monthly rate 1.17/100M VMT vs 1.28 (2025), 1.34 (2024), 1.41 (2023). Through-August state changes: CA +18% (+338), PA +11% (+73), DC +93% (+14), RI +42% (+10); HI -34% (-29), IL -17% (-128), NH -20% (-18), CO -15% (-64), KS -15% (-36), OK -15% (-62), ID -13% (-21), TN -12% (-79), UT -12% (-20). 33 states + DC down in August vs Aug 2025; 17 up. Mileage source: FHWA.
2. **NSC H1 2026 analysis via ISHN** (ishn.com, ~Sep 17, 2026): 18,460 deaths H1 (-2% vs revised H1 2025 18,846); mileage +0.8%; H1 rate 1.12 (-3.4% vs 2025, -10.4% vs 2024). 13 states down ≥10%, 10 states + DC up ≥10%.
3. **NHTSA Q1 2026 early estimates via CrashStats** (crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov, pub 813833): 7,770 deaths Q1 (-4.3%); rate 0.99/100M VMT; 2025 full-year early estimate 36,640 (-6.7%).
4. **NHTSA "Speeding Catches Up With You" press release** (nhtsa.gov): 2024 speeding deaths 11,288 (29% of fatalities); 2025 speeding estimate 10,035 (-11%).

## Original calculations
- Nine ≥12%-decline states' combined YTD savings: 29 (HI) + 18 (NH) + 128 (IL) + 64 (CO) + 36 (KS) + 62 (OK) + 21 (ID) + 79 (TN) + 20 (UT) = **457 fewer deaths**.
- California's +338 / 457 = **74%** — California erased roughly three-quarters of the nine best states' combined gains.
- California's +338 vs Illinois' −128 (best single state): CA added **2.6x** what the top saver saved.
- August monthly rate trajectory: 1.41 (2023) → 1.34 (2024) → 1.28 (2025) → 1.17 (2026) = **17.0% improvement in three years**.
- DC +93% = 14 deaths (base-rate illustration: biggest percentage, trivial absolute).

## Limitations (must state)
- NSC figures are **preliminary** and mature upward as deaths are confirmed; YTD comparisons are preliminary-vs-preliminary of comparable maturity.
- NSC uses the NCHS definition (traffic + non-traffic deaths within a year); **not comparable to NHTSA figures** (traffic deaths within 30 days). Do not mix NSC and NHTSA numbers in one arithmetic.
- Small-base percentages mislead: DC's +93% is 14 deaths; treat percentages and absolutes separately.
- No causal attribution available — the data does not say WHY California diverged (VMT growth, enforcement, policy, demographics all unmeasured here).
- State reporters' methods vary; the 2026-vs-2025 comparison assumes comparable reporting maturity.

## Strongest counterargument
California has ~12% of US population and the most vehicle miles traveled; +338 deaths on a large base may partly reflect exposure growth rather than rising danger, and preliminary data often narrows on revision. Illinois' −128 may likewise reflect a mild winter or reporting lag rather than policy success. The honest reading: the divergence is real in the preliminary data, but its causes — and whether it survives revision — are unknown.

## Actionable takeaway
Nationally the trend is genuinely improving (August rate down 17% in three years), so the standard advice compounds: the gains come from slower speeds, belt use, and sober driving. For California drivers specifically: you are in the one large state moving against the national trend, which means the marginal value of those behaviors is highest where you drive. For everyone else: Illinois, Hawaii, and Tennessee show double-digit declines are achievable — the policy question is what they did differently, and the data to answer it is the state-level detail NSC publishes monthly.
