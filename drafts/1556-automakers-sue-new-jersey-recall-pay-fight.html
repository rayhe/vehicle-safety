Four Automakers Sued New Jersey the Same Day 1.2 Million Cars Were Recalled

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

On Friday, November 20, Ford, Honda, Stellantis, and Volkswagen sued New Jersey in federal court in Trenton.[1] Their target was a state law that forces automakers to pay franchised dealers retail rates for warranty and recall repairs. That same day, Honda and Toyota recalled about 1.6 million vehicles over Takata airbags, and Stellantis recalled 82,843 Ram pickups over bad ABS software.[4][5] Every one of those free repairs has to be done by a franchised dealer, which means the four automakers sued the people who do the work on the same day they assigned them 1.2 million more jobs.

New Jersey's law is called the Motor Vehicle Open Recall Notice and Fair Compensation Act, and it has been in effect since April 1.[2] It does three things that matter to anyone who owns a car. First, it requires automakers to reimburse dealers at the average retail labor rate, retail time allowance, and retail parts markup for warranty and recall work, with the rates set from the dealer's own customer-paid repair orders.[3] Second, it pays dealers 1.5 percent of trade-in value per month when a stop-sale or do-not-drive order leaves a car sitting because parts have not arrived.[3] Third, the part nobody is suing over, it puts an open-recall notice in every registration renewal, telling owners the repair is free at an authorized dealer, and requires dealers to check NHTSA for open recalls before selling a used car.[3]

Automakers call the law a blank check. Their complaint says it lets dealers set prices for warranty and recall work while barring manufacturers from avoiding what they call extortionate results, and that it will shift more than $375 million a year to dealers as a windfall, with no obligation to spend any of it on better service or higher pay for technicians.[1] Since the law took effect, each automaker says it has received dozens of bills for hours no technician worked, at prices above what comparable dealers charge for the same work and above what retail customers pay.[1] They also object to paying marked-up prices on parts the dealers got cheap or free, and to being barred from supplying cheaper parts.[1]

Run the numbers before picking a side. $375 million sounds enormous until you set it against New Jersey's $45.4 billion auto retail sector: it is 0.83 percent, less than one cent in every dollar of car retail in the state.[2] That does not make the automakers wrong. $375 million is real money, concentrated on four companies, and a bill for hours nobody worked is fraud no matter who sends it. But the scale matters, because the rhetoric does not match it. Stop-sale arithmetic is just as concrete: at 1.5 percent a month, a dealer holding a $30,000 trade-in under a do-not-drive order collects $450 a month, $2,700 over six months, capped at the car's full value.[3] Automakers call that a blank check. Dealers call it the cost of a car they are legally forbidden to sell, and both descriptions fit the same formula.

What makes this a safety story is the pipeline underneath the price fight. NHTSA's own data says only 50.3 percent of recalled vehicles had been repaired as of February 2026, leaving about 14 million recalled cars on the road with known defects.[6] For cars five to ten years old, completion falls to 44 percent. A November 2026 analysis of NHTSA data found 25.8 million vehicles still unrepaired in recalls at least two years old, and one Honda inflator recall had repaired only 5 percent of its 1,439,841 vehicles.[7] Repairs happen at dealers, performed by technicians, in bays, on the clock. Pay is not a side issue in that system; it is the system.

Fairness demands the automakers' case at full strength, because it has one. A dealer that sets its own reimbursement rate, challengeable only by auditing a sample of its own submitted orders, is a real structural problem. Bills for hours no technician worked are not a rounding error, and a $375 million annual transfer with no strings attached is a windfall by any plain reading. If automakers pass the cost through, New Jersey car buyers pay it in higher prices. Against that, dealers spent decades being paid below cost for warranty work, the audit right exists in the statute, and retail rate is what the market already charges. Both sides are describing the same money, and whoever is right about the price, the repair has to happen, because right now about half of recalled cars never get it.

If you live in New Jersey, read the open-recall notice that now comes with your registration renewal. It links to NHTSA's database and says the repair is free at an authorized dealer. If you are buying a used car in the state, ask the dealer for the NHTSA check in writing; the law requires the check, but paper is what makes a requirement real. And if you own a 2012-2013 Honda or Acura in a hot, humid state, a 2003-2017 Toyota or Lexus on the Takata list, or a 2022-2024 Ram 1500 Classic, your car is in the November recall wave. Do not wait for the letter: Honda's interim letters go out December 18, Toyota's between December 28 and January 11, and Ram VINs are searchable November 26. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today, because the lawsuit is about who pays for the repair, and the repair only happens if you show up.
