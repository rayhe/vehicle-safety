California Passed Four Tougher DUI Laws. It Killed the One Proven to Work.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 27, 2027

According to the toxicology reports — and there are a lot of them — Sacramento had a choice last month. Four bills that make drunk driving more punitive. One bill that makes drunk driving harder to repeat. Governor Newsom signed the four. The one got quietly suffocated in a Senate committee. In 2024, 1,311 Californians died in alcohol-impaired crashes, the second-highest body count in the nation.[1] The legislature looked at that number and chose the murder warning.

To be specific about what died and what lived.

5,700,000
Drunk-driving attempts blocked by ignition interlocks since 2006, per MADD's June 2026 report

The corpse is AB 1830, authored by Assemblymember Cottie Petrie-Norris of Irvine. Simple concept: every first-time DUI offender installs an ignition interlock, the dashboard breathalyzer that refuses to start the car if it smells alcohol. It passed the Assembly 74 to 0. Then it reached the Senate Appropriations Committee, chaired by Sen. Sabrina Cervantes of Riverside, where a late-hour amendment made the interlock optional for first-timers who hadn't hurt anyone. Take the device, or take a license suspension instead. The bill never made it to the governor's desk.[2]

What lived: a warning to DUI defendants who plead down to lesser charges that they could face murder charges if they drive impaired again and kill someone. Felony charges for a third DUI within ten years. More DMV violation points for intoxicated vehicular manslaughter. Longer license revocations for repeat offenders. All punishment, all aimed at people already convicted, none of it aimed at the ignition switch. "We will not stand by as more lives are lost," Newsom said in the signing announcement.[2] Correct, except for the part where he stood by as the prevention device lost.

Now the part where I do the thing I do, which is read the actual studies. The CDC's Community Preventive Services Task Force reviewed 15 studies of ignition interlocks and found that while the devices are installed, re-arrest rates for alcohol-impaired driving fall by a median of 67%.[3] Not a survey and not a vibes-based estimate: thirteen studies, same direction, median two-thirds. MADD's 2026 report adds the scale: interlocks have blocked more than 5.7 million drunk-driving attempts since 2006, including 336,000 in 2025 alone. That is one blocked start every 95 seconds, around the clock, all year.[4] Thirty-four states plus Washington, D.C. already require interlocks for all offenders. California sits in the bottom tier with five other states where a judge simply gets to decide.[4]

And here is the Sacramento subplot, reported straight because I am a toxicology desk and not a gossip column, but it deserves the daylight. Cervantes offered no public explanation for the amendment. Her spokesperson did not return requests for comment. In 2025, Sacramento police cited Cervantes herself on suspicion of DUI after a crash near the Capitol; prosecutors declined to file charges after a blood test showed no drugs or alcohol in her system, and she has since sued the Sacramento Police Department alleging misconduct. Her sister, Riverside Councilmember Clarissa Cervantes, has two DUIs, the most recent in 2023, and entered rehab afterward.[2] Kellie Montalvo, a Corona mother who lost her son to a 2020 hit-and-run by a driver prosecutors said had been drinking and smoking marijuana, happens to be Cervantes' constituent. She asked for an explanation, and she is still asking.

Give the opposition its full hearing, because the counterargument is not frivolous. The San Francisco Public Defender's office, quoted in the bill's legislative analysis, argued that mandating interlocks "contradicts available evidence about reducing vehicular fatalities, and broadly increases (the) likelihood that a person with a first DUI conviction will never be able to reinstate their license." Installation runs $70 to $150, plus $60 to $80 a month in monitoring fees, which lands hardest on low-income drivers who may end up driving unlicensed and uninsured instead. The office said it would "only support evidence-based fatality reduction measures that can be implemented successfully and equitably."[2] That is a real objection. It argues for subsidizing the devices, not for pretending the devices do not work. And note the CDC's own asterisk: re-arrest rates revert to no-interlock levels once the device comes off.[3] An interlock manages a risk. It does not cure the disease behind the wheel.

What this story does not prove: that AB 1830 would have saved a specific number of Californian lives. That is counterfactual modeling, and this desk does not do counterfactuals over coffee. The 1,311 figure is NHTSA's FARS estimate, which imputes BACs where test results were unknown, so treat the last digit as honest uncertainty. And interlocks do nothing about drug-impaired driving, which contributed to 2,271 California crashes in 2024.[5] The claim here is narrower and fully supported: the evidence-based tool was on the table, a 74-0 Assembly vote put it there, and one committee chair took it off.

What you can actually do: if you live in California, know that first-offense interlock installation is currently a judge's call, not a requirement. The state's extended pilot program, now running through 2033, mandates the devices only for repeat offenders and first-timers who cause injury crashes.[2] If you live in one of the 34 all-offender states, you already blow to start after a DUI. And if you want to see where your state lands, MADD's 2026 report grades every state's interlock law. The national average grade is a fail. California helped keep it that way.[4]
