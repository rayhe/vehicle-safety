## Kill test: PASS

**Proposed angle (1-2 sentences):** On October 15, 2026, NHTSA named the 23 vehicles it will crash-test for model year 2027, plus 18 for its brand-new pedestrian protection check and 10 for driver-assistance evaluation. Only three models — the Tesla Model 3, the Volkswagen Atlas, and the Kia Telluride — appear on all three lists, and the list's most interesting name is a truck most shoppers have never seen: the Slate pickup, a startup's first vehicle, getting federal crash testing before most buyers can buy one.

**Challenge — is this surprising after 1,374 articles?** Yes, three ways. (1) Zero prior site coverage of the MY2027 selection list: queue/filename grep 2026-11-03 for "5-star", "ncap", "pedestrian-protection" finds #1246 (the September inaugural pedestrian *results*, a different federal action) and #1156/#863 (NCAP program pieces), but nothing on the October 15 *selection* announcement. (2) The three-list cross-reference is original: nobody has published which models sit on all three lists, and the answer (Model 3, Atlas, Telluride) is not the set a shopper would guess. (3) The honest gap: NHTSA says ratings cover 87% of new vehicles, but selection is not a rating — a model absent from these lists is not unrated, and a model on them has no new stars yet. That distinction is exactly the kind of thing that costs shoppers money when dealers wave old stars at them.

## Verified facts (all sourced, no fabrication)

1. **The announcement.** NHTSA released its MY2027 vehicle selections for the New Car Assessment Program 5-Star Safety Ratings on October 15, 2026. (NHTSA press release, nhtsa.gov.)
2. **The three tracks.** Crash safety: 23 vehicles, frontal + side + rollover. Pedestrian protection: 18 vehicles, under the brand-new pedestrian protection assessment. Driver assistance: 10 vehicles across five systems: automatic emergency braking, pedestrian automatic emergency braking, lane keeping assist, blind spot warning, blind spot intervention. (NHTSA press release; Motoring Chronicle full lists, Oct 22, 2026.)
3. **Crash list (23):** BMW X5; Chevrolet Silverado 1500, Tahoe, Trailblazer; Dodge Charger; Honda Civic Hatchback Type R; Hyundai Elantra, Tucson; Infiniti QX65; Jeep Cherokee; Kia EV3, Niro, Seltos, Telluride; Lexus NX 350h; Nissan Frontier Crew Cab, Pathfinder, Rogue, Rogue e-Power; Slate pickup; Tesla Model 3; Toyota Sienna Hybrid; Volkswagen Atlas. (Motoring Chronicle, from NHTSA release.)
4. **Pedestrian list (18):** Acura MDX; Audi Q5; gas-powered Ford Explorer; Honda HR-V; Hyundai Santa Cruz; Kia EV3, Telluride; Mazda CX-50, CX-90; Mitsubishi Outlander; Nissan Frontier Crew Cab, Sentra; Subaru Outback; Tesla Model 3; Toyota Prius, RAV4 Hybrid, Tundra; Volkswagen Atlas. (Motoring Chronicle.)
5. **Driver-assistance list (10):** BMW X5; Chevrolet Silverado 1500; Hyundai Elantra; Jeep Cherokee; Kia Telluride; Lexus NX 350h; Rivian R2; Tesla Model 3; Toyota Sienna Hybrid; Volkswagen Atlas. (Motoring Chronicle; GM Authority corroborates Silverado as the only GM vehicle on the ADAS list.)
6. **87% coverage claim.** NHTSA says safety ratings cover 87% of all new vehicles. That figure counts ratings carried over from earlier testing of unchanged models, not just these 23 — selection for 2027 testing is not itself a rating, and results post only after testing and quality control. (NHTSA press release; trade-press explainer: selection ≠ rating.)
7. **Pedestrian test mechanics.** Three impacts: child and adult headform strikes on hood/windshield area; upper-leg impacts on the hood's leading edge; lower-limb impacts on the bumper. Outcome is a yellow pass / fail / pending / not-submitted box on the model's NHTSA.gov/ratings page, not stars. September 2026 inaugural round: 102 models from 10 manufacturers met the criteria (covered by site article #1246; this article covers the October selection list, not those results). (Motoring Chronicle; Repairer Driven News, Sept 2026.)
8. **Notable details.** The Silverado 1500 is all-new for 2027; the Trailblazer gets a refresh with new safety features (GM Authority, Oct 28, 2026). The gas-powered Ford Explorer is the only American-branded vehicle on the pedestrian list (GM Authority). NHTSA has set no publication date for the 2027 results. Star ratings appear on new-vehicle window stickers. (Motoring Chronicle; GM Authority.)
9. **FARS context (site dataset, 2014–2023, verified this run from fars_output.js):** Chevrolet Silverado: 9,591 deaths, rate 1.25 per 100M VMT. The 2027 Silverado on the crash list is a new generation, so the FARS figure describes the nameplate's history, not the 2027 truck — say so explicitly.
10. **Quote.** NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison: "For more than 30 years, NHTSA's 5-Star Safety Ratings have helped families choose the vehicle that’s right for them." (NHTSA press release.)

## Original calculations / analysis (verifiable, show work in article)

- **On all three lists (intersection of the three published lists):** Tesla Model 3 (crash ✓, pedestrian ✓, ADAS ✓); Volkswagen Atlas (✓, ✓, ✓); Kia Telluride (✓, ✓, ✓). No other model appears on all three. Kia EV3 and Nissan Frontier Crew Cab appear on crash + pedestrian but not ADAS; BMW X5, Silverado 1500, Elantra, Cherokee, NX 350h, Sienna Hybrid appear on crash + ADAS but not pedestrian.
- **The Slate pickup** appears on the crash list only. A startup's first truck gets the full federal crash battery — the list is not just the usual suspects.
- **Absences (checked against the full 23-name crash list):** no Ford F-150, no Toyota Camry, no Honda Accord, no Toyota Corolla, no Honda CR-V, no Toyota Tacoma. Absence is NOT a scandal and must not be framed as one: a model missing from the 2027 selection may still carry a rating from earlier testing of the same generation. The actionable point is to check the model-year page, not to assume "untested."
- **87% arithmetic, stated honestly:** 23 crash tests cannot by themselves cover 87% of new-vehicle sales; the 87% figure only works if prior-year ratings carry over for unchanged models. The article must say this, because a dealer can otherwise wave a 5-star sticker from a previous generation at a buyer of a redesigned car.

## Story spine (Clara Rollover voice: direct, practical, slightly angry on behalf of consumers)

- Lede: the federal government just told you which 23 cars it will crash next year. Three of them get every test it offers.
- The three lists, rendered concretely: 23 crash, 18 pedestrian, 10 driver-assist; the three triple-list models named.
- The Slate surprise: a startup truck on the federal crash list.
- The pedestrian box is not stars: pass/fail/pending/not-submitted, and a five-star car can still fail the pedestrian check.
- The 87% trap: selection is not a rating; results have no publication date; check the model-year page and the window sticker, and check by trim.
- Actionable: before you buy, search the exact model + year at NHTSA.gov/ratings; if the model is on the 2027 pedestrian list, ask the dealer for its pedestrian status specifically (it will not be on older printed materials); compare ADAS systems by name, not marketing term.
- Closer: the list is useful only if you read it as a schedule, not a scorecard.

## Limitations (for the limitations section)

- These are planned evaluations, not results. No 2027 star, pedestrian, or ADAS outcome exists yet for the listed vehicles, and NHTSA has set no publication date.
- The September pedestrian round (102 models passing) is prior site coverage (#1246) and a different action from this October selection list; do not conflate the two.
- FARS figures (2014–2023) describe past generations, not the 2027 models on these lists; any nameplate history cited must carry that caveat.
- The "absence" observation (no F-150, Camry, Accord on the crash list) is a fact about this selection only; it does not mean those models lack ratings, which may carry over from earlier testing.
- Full-list names come from NHTSA's release as republished by Motoring Chronicle (Oct 22, 2026); the NHTSA press page itself did not render the tables in the copy reviewed this run.

## Strongest counterargument

A testing list is not a safety finding, and writing 500 words about a schedule risks dressing up bureaucracy as news. NHTSA tests a rotating selection every year; most models carry over ratings because they are unchanged, which is efficient, not negligent. The pedestrian test is new and its first 102 passes were already covered (#1246). The honest version of this article's thesis is narrower: the list matters not because it condemns or crowns any car, but because shoppers mistake selection for a score and old stars for new ones — and the three-list overlap plus the Slate inclusion are the only genuinely new facts in it.
