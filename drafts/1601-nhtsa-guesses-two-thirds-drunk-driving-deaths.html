Two-Thirds of America's Drunk-Driving Death Toll Is a Statistical Guess

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

NHTSA's 2024 drunk-driving toll is 11,904 dead, one every 44 minutes, 30 percent of everyone killed on American roads.[2] It is also, mostly, a model. Blood alcohol results existed for just 36 percent of the drivers in fatal crashes last year, which means the BAC of the other 35,537 drivers was estimated by a statistical formula, not measured in a lab.[1] That number is not fake. It is something stranger: a national statistic that America stopped bothering to measure.

64%
Share of drivers in fatal crashes whose blood alcohol level was estimated by a statistical model, never measured in a lab.

Ten years ago the picture was different, and not by a little. In 2015, NHTSA had a real BAC result for 49 percent of drivers in fatal crashes. By 2024 that was 36 percent, a 13-point slide in a single decade.[1] Dead drivers still get tested most of the time, 58 percent in 2024, down from 74 percent in 2015. Surviving drivers, meanwhile, barely get tested at all, just 18 percent. Four out of five people who walk away from a fatal crash never blow into anything, never give blood, never enter the record as anything but a modeled estimate. Death gets a toxicology screen; survival gets the benefit of the doubt.

Filling the gap is a statistical method called multiple imputation, and NHTSA is admirably open about how it works. The model weighs crash factors like time of day and day of week, vehicle and person factors like age and restraint use, and, in the fact sheet's own words, most important, the subjective assessment of the investigating police officer as to whether alcohol was involved.[1] Read that again. The single most important input to the federal drunk-driving death toll is a cop's judgment at the scene, formalized into a number and published as if it came from a lab. It usually points the right way, though that word is doing a lot of work in this sentence.

Cross a state line and the whole thing gets stranger. Vermont tests 98 percent of its dead drivers for alcohol, the highest rate in the country, and reports the worst drunk-driving share in America: 41 percent of its traffic deaths involved an impaired driver.[1] Mississippi tests 9 percent of its dead drivers and reports 23 percent, comfortably below average. Montana tests 86 percent of all drivers in fatal crashes; Mississippi tests 8 percent. South Carolina's alcohol death rate per mile is 0.67, more than four times Massachusetts at 0.16. Are South Carolinians four times drunker than Bay Staters? Maybe. Or maybe the ranking measures which states actually look. Vermont's 41 percent might be the most honest number in the country, and Mississippi's 23 percent the least measured. NHTSA warns, in the same fact sheet, that great caution should be exercised comparing states, because testing rates may affect the accuracy and reliability of the estimates. The agency is telling you the leaderboard is rigged by diligence, and almost nobody reads that far.

The decade table makes the point harder to ignore. Vermont's alcohol share of traffic deaths jumped from 26 percent in 2015 to 41 percent in 2024, the biggest move in the country, while South Carolina went 31 to 40, Ohio 28 to 37, Kansas 23 to 35, Oklahoma 26 to 35, and California 27 to 34. Utah, the only state in America with a .05 limit, went 16 to 26. Alaska went the other way, 34 to 19, the biggest improvement anywhere.[1] Some of those moves are real changes in drinking and driving. Some of them are changes in who gets tested and how often. The fact sheet does not let you tell which is which, and that is the story: a decade of movement on a chart where the measuring instrument itself was degrading.

Now the counterargument, at full strength, because it deserves it. Multiple imputation is not a guess in the barroom sense. It is a peer-reviewed statistical method, validated against known cases, and NHTSA discloses it in every fact sheet instead of burying it.[3] The national trend is probably real: alcohol deaths fell 3.9 percent in 2024, right in line with the 4.3 percent fall in deaths overall, and a broken instrument can still track a direction. Vermont's 41 percent could be genuinely heavy drinking, not just thorough testing; the state also leads the country in impaired drivers per fatal crash at 36 percent, and testing more finds drunks, it does not invent them. All fair. None of it changes the fact that the precision implied by 11,904 is false precision. A number with four significant figures, built on 64 percent modeled inputs, is a confidence trick the data plays on its own readers.

What this does not prove matters just as much. These are 2024 Annual Report File numbers, not the finalized file, so expect small revisions. An alcohol-impaired-driving fatality means a driver at .08 or above was involved in the crash, not that alcohol caused it. The testing paradox is a pattern across 50 states, not proof that any single state's number is wrong; Mississippi's true rate is unknowable from FARS alone, and Vermont's rise could be real. Utah's 2015 baseline predates its .05 law, which took effect at the end of 2018, so the 16-to-26 move is not evidence the law failed. And the 11,904 total fell from 12,382 in 2023, a real decline by any measure.[2]

What to do about it is boring, which is why it hasn't happened. Test every driver in a fatal crash, dead or alive. Montana tests 86 percent of drivers in fatal crashes and Vermont tests 98 percent of the dead ones, so this is not a technical problem; it is a policy choice most states have not made. If you read a state drunk-driving ranking, look up Table 5 of DOT HS 813 813 first and check how much of that state's number was measured and how much was modeled. A ranking without a testing rate next to it is a rumor with a decimal point. And if you survive a fatal crash, know this: there is an 82 percent chance nobody will ever test your blood alcohol, and your crash will enter the national record as a probability, not a fact. The system trusts survivors; the data would rather it didn't.
