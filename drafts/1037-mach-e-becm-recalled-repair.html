NHTSA Filed Two Recalls of Repairs One Number Apart. Ford’s Version Is a Battery Gauge That Lies.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 1, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens when the part that measures your battery has no idea what’s in it. A Battery Energy Control Module, the BECM, is the pack’s accountant. It reads cell voltages, estimates state of charge, and tells the dashboard what to print. Ford’s replacement BECMs shipped as service parts were calibrated to 100 percent state of charge instead of the pack’s actual state, which means 86 Mustang Mach-Es may display a charge reading that has no relationship to the charge in the battery. Forget estimates: the gauge is a fixed answer to a changing question.

26V582 / 26V583
Two consecutive NHTSA campaign numbers, filed days apart, both tracing the defect to the repair channel instead of the factory

The failure mode splits two ways, and both are ugly. If the actual state of charge sits below what the gauge claims, the car can hit unexpected power limitation and lose forward propulsion. That is the kind of surprise you schedule for a merge lane. Separately, the bad calibration can push the pack into a severe high-voltage condition, which Ford’s own filing says may cause a fire, with the risk rising after repeated DC fast charging.[1] The affected cars are 2023–2025 Mach-Es with lithium iron phosphate packs, and the defective modules are two service part numbers: PZ98-10B687-K and PZ98-14C197-K.[2]

None of this came from the factory. Every affected car drove into a shop once already, warranty repair or over-the-counter parts sale, and drove out with a module carrying the wrong calibration.[3] That is the structural point. Recall systems are built around VINs and factory build records, but this defect traveled through the service-parts supply chain. A module bought over the counter and installed at an independent shop leaves no dealer-service trail, and an owner Ford has never met is not going to get an owner letter. We have now seen this vector twice in one month: Daimler’s 26V583, filed days later, recalled 2,404 trucks because dealers installed the wrong brake modulator valves during earlier recall repairs, a story this site covered as #916.[4] Campaigns 26V582 and 26V583 sit one number apart. The factory got the parts right, and the fix channel still managed to get it wrong.

Ford put the affected VINs on NHTSA.gov on September 9. Interim owner letters go out around October 26. No software fix is expected until December.[1] That is three months of knowing about a battery-fire risk with nothing to install, and the interim letter is, per the filing, mostly a promise of a future letter. If your Mach-E has a replacement BECM, the gauge is the diagnostic: a charge reading that sits at 100 percent and refuses to move is not a miracle, it is the defect. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now, call Ford at 1-866-436-7332 and cite 26S68, and go easy on the DC fast chargers until the update lands.

In fairness: eighty-six cars, zero reported fires, zero injuries, and the fire scenario is a conditional tail risk after repeated fast charging. Ford caught this through internal review and disclosed it. Ranked honestly: lying gauge first, power loss second, fire a distant third. What makes it matter is the vector, not the volume. Service parts are the least scrutinized link in the recall chain, and nothing external audited this one before Ford did. Nobody audited it at all.
