One Loose Bolt Put Fourteen Rivian R2s Under Recall. The Robot Missed It.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 8, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: the most advanced vehicle Rivian has ever built can be defeated by a single bolt. Fourteen 2027 R2s rolled out of the Normal, Illinois factory with a high-voltage battery fastener that was never tightened properly, and Rivian believes every one of the fourteen carries the defect. When the connection lets go, the drive unit loses power, and there is no warning first: no sputter, no shudder, no dashboard tap on the shoulder, just silence where the torque used to be.[1]

100%
Rivian's estimated share of the 14-vehicle recall population carrying the defect (NHTSA 26V625)

On paper it is NHTSA campaign 26V625, Rivian number FSAM-1888: fourteen R2s built between May 18 and August 27, 2026, each carrying an M6x1.0x24.5 fastener, part number SC00036202-D, on the high-voltage battery circuit, torqued to something less than the specification.[2] Dealers will inspect the connection, repair or replace damaged components, and apply a properly torqued fastener, free of charge. Owner letters mail November 28, and VINs become searchable at nhtsa.gov/recalls the same day,[1] and for fourteen trucks the entire remedy is a torque wrench.

Except the bolt is not the story; the story is the machine that was supposed to catch the bolt. The story is the machine that was supposed to catch the bolt. Rivian's factory runs an automated torque station whose entire reason for existing is verifying that fasteners meet their torque values. An under-torqued fastener passed through it and kept going. What caught it was a person: a manufacturing operator who spotted the loose fastener on the factory floor on September 17 and escalated it to quality.[2] Rivian reprogrammed the station to reject fasteners that do not meet the required value, which is a polite way of saying they taught the machine to do the thing it was already supposed to do. The federal filing contains no explanation of how the station passed the bolt in the first place. Somewhere in Normal, Illinois, there is a robot whose one job was torque, and it failed at torque, and the paperwork just moves on.

This is the R2's second recall, and the pair of them is the whole EV-startup condition in miniature. The first, last month, was a rearview camera software glitch fixed with an over-the-air update: no human hands, no service visit, patched from the cloud while owners slept.[2] This one needs a wrench and an appointment. Code can be fixed from Normal; a bolt cannot. Rivian told TechCrunch that all fourteen affected vehicles are already at service centers, which sits oddly beside a filing that still schedules owner notification letters for late November.[6] If every truck is already in the shop, the letters are theater. If the letters are real, somebody might be driving on a silent fault for two more months. Both claims sit in the filing, and it resolves neither.

What to do with this: if you took delivery of a 2027 R2 built between May and August 2026, do not wait for the letter. Call Rivian service at 1-888-748-4261, cite FSAM-1888 or NHTSA 26V625, and ask whether your VIN is in the population.[1] If any EV loses motive power without warning, the move is coast, not panic: signal, get fully off the roadway, then stop. There is no engine sputter to warn you, because there is no engine. And for the reservation holders watching from the sidelines, file this under the oldest rule in car buying: the first model year of a new platform carries the highest recall density in the industry. The 2028s will have the cleaner record, because early adoption has a price, and this time the price was a bolt.

Honest objections, because the universe insists on them. Fourteen trucks, zero complaints, zero incidents, zero injuries. A line worker caught it, Rivian decided on a voluntary recall within a week, and the remedy is a torque wrench applied free of charge.[5] This is manufacturing QA working as designed: the human layer caught what the automated layer missed, which is precisely why you keep the human layer. The filing does not say how far under-torqued the fastener was or what the per-mile failure probability is, so do not picture fourteen fireballs. And this is a suspect fastener lot on early-build vehicles, not a design flaw in the R2 platform; do not generalize it to every R2 on the road. Fair, but the next time someone tells you the robots have the factory floor handled, remember the torque station in Normal, and the person with eyes who did its job for it.
