Nissan Fixed Its Back Seat With a Belt Revision. Audi, Toyota, and Chevy Didn’t.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. IIHS released its latest round of updated moderate overlap crash tests on October 31, and five small crossovers went through a barrier at 40 mph with a dummy the size of a 12-year-old belted in behind the driver. Only two earned Good, the Nissan Kicks and the Volkswagen Jetta, while Audi’s redesigned Q3 scored Poor, and so did Chevy’s Trailblazer, and Toyota’s redesigned RAV4 split the difference, Acceptable as a hybrid and Marginal as a plug-in.[1]

How much higher the Chevy Trailblazer’s real-world fatality rate runs than the Toyota RAV4’s (2.83 vs. 0.19 deaths per 100M VMT) — FARS 2014–2023

Nissan’s result is the one that matters, because Nissan failed this exact test first. When the current Kicks debuted for 2025, it scored Poor. Nissan went back and changed the rear seat belts and load limiters on cars built after June 2026, then asked IIHS to test again, and Poor became Good. Four months, a belt revision, and the cheapest car in the test now holds a 2026 Top Safety Pick award.[2] That is what makes the other results hard to excuse. Nobody needed a new platform or a new structure, because a belt and a load limiter did it.

What actually failed in the other cars was geometry, not metal. In the Q3, the shoulder belt slid up toward the rear dummy’s neck while the lap belt slipped off the pelvis onto the soft abdomen, a combination that turns a survivable crash into an organ injury. Toyota’s RAV4 failed in the same place: shoulder belt toward the neck on the hybrid, lap belt onto the abdomen on the plug-in. Both cars earned Good in every other IIHS category, small overlap, side impact, headlights, crash prevention. Their structures are fine, but their back-seat belts are not.[1]

Cross-referencing these five nameplates against a decade of FARS data complicates the story in a useful way. Chevy’s Trailblazer pairs its Poor lab result with a real-world fatality rate of 2.83 deaths per 100M VMT across 2,473 deaths, the worst of this group by a wide margin. VW’s Jetta, which earned Good, sits at 1.71 across 1,375 deaths. Nissan’s Kicks sits at 0.69 across 339 deaths. And Toyota’s RAV4, the car that just stumbled in the lab, carries a real-world rate of 0.19 across 914 deaths, one of the lowest in the entire FARS dataset.[3] One test measures one failure mode for one dummy size. A decade of FARS measures everything: structure, avoidance tech, who buys the car, how it gets driven. Both measurements are true, and they point in different directions for the RAV4.

Honest caveats, because the cross-tab deserves them. FARS rates cover all model years from 2014 through 2023, so the Trailblazer number mixes the old generation with the current redesign, and the Audi Q3 does not appear in the repo dataset at all, so no rate is claimed for it. Rates use VMT estimates rather than odometer readings. And the IIHS test is one crash mode, not a whole-vehicle verdict. What to do with all of it: if you are shopping a 2026 or 2027 Kicks, check the build date, because only cars built after June 2026 carry the revised belts and the Good rating. If kids ride in your back seat, check the moderate overlap rating at iihs.org/ratings before you sign anything, since a Good there is the gatekeeper for every IIHS safety award. NHTSA’s rear seat-belt reminder rule reaches rear seats on September 1, 2027, and the agency estimates it will save about 50 lives a year.[4] Until the chime arrives, the belt geometry is doing the work, and in three of these five cars it is doing it badly.
