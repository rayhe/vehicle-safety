E-One Recalled 301 Fire Trucks Over a Loose Wire That Can Start a Fire. It's the Third Recall in Three Months.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
August 27, 2027

On October 6, E-One reported its third fire-truck recall in 83 days.[1] This one covers 301 Typhoon N, Cyclone N, and Cyclone II trucks whose positive power supply may be loose, overheating the wiring harness and busbar.[1] NHTSA's stated consequence: an increased risk of fire. Read that again. The fire department's fire truck has a fire risk, because of a loose wire.

519
fire trucks across E-One's three 2026 recalls

The first recall, filed July 15, covered 159 trucks whose drive/pump transfer case could disengage, killing power to the rear wheels.[2] A fire truck that can't move is a very expensive traffic cone. The second, filed August 19, covered 59 trucks with a hydraulic valve that could go inoperative, freezing the outriggers in place.[3] Outriggers stabilize the aerial ladder. Without them, the ladder stays down and the crew's tallest tool is a tall ladder. Now the third recall completes the set: not propulsion, not stabilization, but the electrical system itself, and the failure mode is fire.

Three recalls, three months, three orthogonal systems, one quarter. That is the part worth sitting with for a moment. E-One didn't have one bad component; it had three different ways for a fire truck to stop being a fire truck, on overlapping model families, inside a single quarter. Of the three, the electrical defect is the most instructive, because the failure is the oldest in the trade: a loose power connection. High resistance at the joint turns current into heat, heat cooks the busbar, and eventually something built to carry power starts a fire instead. Fixing it takes a new wiring harness and busbar, real hardware provided free of charge, with owner letters going out October 20.[1] Two of the three recalls were fixed with software; this one needed new metal.

Full strength to the other side: recalls are the safety system working. E-One self-reported all three campaigns, each with a free remedy and owner notification, which is exactly what the law wants. Fire apparatus are low-volume, custom-built machines; 519 potentially affected units across three campaigns is a small slice of the fleet, and the public records show no crashes or injuries tied to any of them. A cynical read says this is a manufacturer being diligent. A less cynical read says diligence and a bad quarter can both be true.

What the data doesn't show: NHTSA's public records don't list injuries, crashes, or warranty claims for any of the three campaigns, so don't invent a body count. Those unit counts are E-One's estimates of potentially affected vehicles, not confirmed failures, and while the same model families appear in all three recalls, the populations differ by component — the number of individual trucks caught in more than one campaign is unknown. If your department runs a 2024–2026 Typhoon N, a 2025–2026 Cyclone N, or a 2026 Cyclone II, check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now instead of waiting for the October 20 letter. Have the shop thermal-scan the main positive power connection at the next preventive maintenance check; a loose busbar shows up as a hot spot long before it shows up as a fire. And confirm the transfer-case software update and the outrigger valve swap are actually in the truck, not just in the file.
