## Angle (1–2 sentences)
NHTSA's headline 2024 drunk-driving death toll (11,904) is built on BAC test results that exist for only 36% of drivers in fatal crashes. Testing has collapsed over the decade, survivors are almost never tested, and the state rankings reward the states that test least.

## Key numbers (all from DOT HS 813 813 unless noted)
- 2024: 11,904 alcohol-impaired-driving fatalities = 30% of all 39,254 traffic deaths. 11,367 impaired drivers = 20% of 55,620 drivers in fatal crashes.
- **Known BAC results: only 20,083 of 55,620 drivers (36%) in 2024, down from 49% in 2015.** That means 35,537 drivers' BACs — 64% — are estimated by NHTSA's multiple-imputation model, not measured.
- Dead drivers with known BAC: 58% (14,355 of 24,636) in 2024, down from 74% in 2015.
- **Surviving drivers with known BAC: 18%.** Four out of five drivers who walk away from a fatal crash are never BAC-tested.
- State known-BAC range (all drivers): **8% Mississippi to 86% Montana**.
- State known-BAC range (driver fatalities): **9% Mississippi to 98% Vermont**. Puerto Rico 99%.
- State alcohol-impaired fatality rates per 100M VMT: **0.16 Massachusetts (lowest) to 0.67 South Carolina (highest)**; national 0.36. 4.2x spread.
- Highest alcohol share of traffic deaths: **Vermont 41%**, then South Carolina and Texas 40% each.
- Impaired-driver share range: 14% Alaska to 36% Vermont.
- Table 10 decade moves (2015 → 2024, share of deaths): Vermont 26%→41% (+15 pts, worst in nation); South Carolina 31%→40%; Ohio 28%→37%; Kansas 23%→35%; Oklahoma 26%→35%; California 27%→34%; Utah 16%→26% (the only .05-limit state, up 10 pts); Alaska 34%→19% (biggest improvement); Mississippi 25%→23% — while testing 8% of drivers.
- The imputation model leans on crash factors, vehicle/person factors, and "most important, the subjective assessment of the investigating police officer as to whether alcohol was involved" (DOT HS 813 813, p. 2).
- NHTSA's own caution: "Great caution should be exercised in comparing the levels of alcohol involvement among States," because testing rates "may affect the accuracy and reliability of the estimates."
- 2024 data is FARS Annual Report File (ARF), subject to revision in the Final File. 2023's toll was revised 12,429 (ARF) → 12,382 (Final).

## Original contribution
Cross-tabulation nobody ran: state testing coverage vs. reported alcohol share. Vermont tests 98% of its dead drivers and reports the worst share in America (41%). Mississippi tests 9% of its dead drivers and reports a below-average 23%. The ranking confounds honesty with danger — the states that look safest may simply be the states that don't look. Second finding: the testing collapse itself (49%→36% overall, 74%→58% dead drivers) means the national trend line NHTSA publishes each year is increasingly a model output, not a measurement.

## Strongest counterargument
The imputation model is not a guess in the colloquial sense: it is a peer-reviewed statistical method (DOT HS 808 816), validated against known cases, and NHTSA discloses it openly in every fact sheet. The national decline (12,382→11,904, −3.9%) is consistent with the overall fatality decline (−4.3%), so the trend is probably real even if the level is modeled. And Vermont's 41% could reflect genuinely heavier drinking, not just better testing — the state also has the highest impaired-driver share (36%) and a rural, high-mileage driving mix. Testing more does not manufacture drunk drivers; it finds them.

## Limitations
- 2024 figures are ARF, not Final File; expect small revisions.
- State comparisons are confounded by demographics, rurality, vehicle mix (motorcycles have the highest alcohol involvement), and enforcement — NHTSA says so explicitly.
- "Alcohol-impaired-driving fatality" means a driver at BAC ≥ .08 was involved; it does not prove alcohol caused the crash.
- The testing-rate paradox is a correlation across 50 states, not proof that any single state's number is wrong; Mississippi's true rate is unknowable from FARS alone.
- Utah's .05 law: share rose 16%→26% over the decade, but the law took effect Dec 30, 2018, and the 2015 baseline predates it; do not claim the law failed — #826 covered the law's effect on heavy drinkers separately.

## Actionable takeaways
- If you are killed in a crash, your BAC will probably be tested (58%). If you survive one, it almost certainly won't be (18%) — and that asymmetry is why the numbers are modeled.
- State rankings are not comparable without the testing rate next to them. Before citing a state's drunk-driving rank, check Table 5 of DOT HS 813 813 for how much of it was actually measured.
- The fix is boring and cheap: test every driver in a fatal crash, dead or alive. Montana (86%) and Vermont (98% of fatalities) prove it is possible.
