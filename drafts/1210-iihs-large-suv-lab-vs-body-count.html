IIHS Crowned Two Electric SUVs the Safest. The Real World Has Never Measured Them.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 18, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better.

IIHS expanded large-SUV testing for 2026 from three vehicles to nine, which is the first time the lab has taken the segment seriously, and out of nine only five earned any award at all. The two Top Safety Pick+ honors went to the Rivian R1S and the Volvo EX90, both fully electric. Here is the part the press releases skip: neither vehicle has a single entry in a decade of FARS fatality data. Too new. The safest rating the lab gives out, in the biggest vehicle class it tests, currently belongs to two vehicles the real world has never measured.[1]

So I cross-tabulated the 2026 lab ratings against FARS 2014-2023 death rates for the nameplates with enough history to measure, and the table is doing a lot of work:

Start at the bottom of that table: the Tahoe earned no award with a Poor moderate-overlap score, at a real-world rate of 2.49. Same story for the Expedition, no award and Marginal small-overlap scores, at 2.31. Missing from the nine entirely is the Yukon, at 2.55, the highest rate of the group. When the lab and the graveyard agree this completely, believe both.

Now read the middle, because that's where the award system breaks: the Sequoia got the same "no award" as the Tahoe, at a real-world rate of 0.83. That is a threefold spread hiding inside one label, which means the label is doing roughly none of the work a shopper needs it to do. A Marginal moderate-overlap score and unrated headlights kept the Sequoia off the podium while actual bodies piled up three times slower than in the Tahoe. The binary award says these two trucks are equivalent, but the bodies say otherwise.

Meanwhile the quiet winner is the Armada: Top Safety Pick plus the lowest measured real-world rate in the group at 0.55, less than a quarter of the Tahoe's. Nobody is writing press releases about the Armada, but the numbers don't care.

As for the electric winners, the R1S just earned its third TSP+ under progressively tougher criteria, a genuine engineering achievement, credit where due, and the EX90 swept Good ratings across the crash tests it completed. But an award is a prediction about the future made from laboratory collisions, and predictions need calibration data. Right now the calibration data for both vehicles is exactly zero fatal crashes, which is the best possible number and also the least informative one.

For shoppers, the data-backed pick is the Armada: award winner, lowest body count. If you're cross-shopping the Tahoe or Expedition, know that no award and a 2.3-plus death rate is the worst combination on this table. Contrarian play: the Sequoia, no award, but a real-world rate that embarrasses trucks the lab rated the same. And if you're buying the R1S or EX90 on safety, you're buying the lab's prediction, which has been right before but has never been tested by a decade of American roads, so check back in 2030.

What this doesn't prove: FARS 2014-2023 measures older generations; the 2026 IIHS ratings cover redesigned models, so this is a nameplate comparison, not a model-year one. Sequoia (136 deaths) and Armada (182) have small samples with wide uncertainty. FARS rates aren't adjusted for driver demographics, geography, or fleet use. Several IIHS cells for Tahoe/Expedition show "not tested," so their no-award status partly reflects incomplete testing. And the R1S/EX90 have no FARS record at all, so the "prediction vs measurement" framing cuts both ways.
