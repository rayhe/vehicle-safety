Kia’s Child-Sensing Airbag Can’t Tell a Kid From a Seat. 21,290 EV9s Are Recalled.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 8, 2026

Your car’s front passenger seat has one job it can never get wrong: knowing the difference between a 180-pound adult and a 40-pound child. In 21,290 Kia EV9s, the sensor doing that job can be silently broken by adjusting the seat.[1] Kia is recalling every one of them.

This is not a bad batch or a bad month. All US-built 2025 EV9s and certain 2026s, built at Kia’s Georgia plant between May 2024 and May 2026, are in the recall,[4] which means the defect lived on the assembly line for nearly two years before anyone caught it.

6%
Kia’s estimate of how many of the 21,290 recalled EV9s actually have the broken tube. The other 94% still need a dealer inspection.

The culprit is a rubber tube routed wrong. Under the front passenger cushion sits the occupant detection system, a sensor mat and bladder tube that weigh whoever is in the seat and tell the airbag whether to fire, and during assembly that tube got routed badly. Raise the seat to its highest position and the tube pinches; do it enough times and it breaks, leaving the system unable to tell a child from an empty seat while the airbag stays armed.[3]

Read that again, because the failure mode is what should scare you: an everyday action, adjusting your seat height, is what destroys a federally mandated child-protection sensor. Not a crash, not a flood, just the seat controls.

That 6% figure is what gets me, because six percent of 21,290 is roughly 1,277 vehicles, and Kia can’t tell which ones they are without looking. So all 21,290 owners get the letter. Owner notifications start mailing October 20, but the VINs have been searchable on NHTSA’s site since September 4, so you do not have to wait for the envelope.[1]

Here is what to watch for. An airbag warning light that won’t quit, or a seatbelt chime nagging you about a passenger who isn’t there, means the sensor is already confused, and confused is the dangerous state: a confused sensor can decide the seat holds an adult when it holds your kid.

Now the part where I give Kia some credit, because the record matters: Kia reported this itself, with no federal investigation and no injuries forcing its hand. The recall initiator on the federal filing is “MFR,” which is bureaucrat for the company walked in on its own.[1] That almost never happens, and it deserves to be said out loud.

To be fair about exposure: children 13 and younger are supposed to ride in the back seat anyway, and Kia’s own guidance says never install a child restraint in the front passenger seat.[6] If you follow the rules you already know, this defect cannot touch your kid. The real-world risk concentrates among the families who put kids up front, or who never hear about a recall on a car they bought last year.

What we don’t know matters too, starting with how Kia arrived at 6%, since the methodology behind the estimate isn’t public. Kia has not reported any injuries tied to this defect, so the harm here is potential, not realized. And the remedy is a human at a dealership re-routing a tube, which is the same species of assembly step that caused this in the first place.

Here is your checklist, and it starts with the obvious one: if you own a 2025 or 2026 EV9, punch your VIN into nhtsa.gov/recalls today. Korean-built cars are not affected, and the VIN check settles it in seconds. Keep kids in the back seat, where they belong regardless of any recall. And if your dashboard is already nagging you about a phantom passenger, skip the letter and call the dealer now, because the repair is free: Kia’s recall line is 1-800-333-4542, and the federal campaign number is 26V545.[1]
