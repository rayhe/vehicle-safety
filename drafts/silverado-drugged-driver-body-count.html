The Druggiest Car in America Is a Pickup Truck

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 17, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, all 490,736 of them, the druggiest vehicle in America is not some forgotten Oldsmobile. It is the Chevrolet Silverado, whose drivers tested positive for drugs in 2,060 fatal crashes, more than seven times the combined drug-positive driver count of the ten vehicles with the highest drug-positive rates on the road.[1]

2,060
Drug-positive drivers in fatal crashes involving the Chevrolet Silverado, FARS 2014–2023 — versus 292 combined across the ten highest drug-rate vehicles

Look at the rate leaderboard and you see a different sport entirely. The Oldsmobile Alero sits at number one with an 18.4% drug-positive rate, a stat this very desk once printed with real enthusiasm. It represents twenty-six drivers, twenty-six against a dataset of nearly half a million. The Alero’s crown is small-sample theater, a participation trophy in a league where the actual teams play in front of fourteen thousand fans.[1]

Count the bodies instead and the leaderboard is the sales chart. Silverado (2,060), F-150 (1,696), Camry (1,160), Accord (1,146), Civic (1,114), then the Altima, Sierra, Corolla, RAM and Malibu. Those ten mainstream models account for 26.3% of every drug-positive dead driver in the dataset. More than one in four drugged drivers who died died in a car you see at every stoplight.[1]

What you are watching is a batting-average-versus-hits mix-up. Rate measures cohort risk: the Alero’s 18.4% says something real about who ends up behind the wheel of a $3,000 sedan, and about the $110,000 BMW M5 sitting third on the same list, while the body count lives where the sales are. Drugged driving is not a beater-car subculture and it is not a German-sedan country club. It is the Camry in your rearview mirror.

To be fair, the Silverado’s count is huge partly because America buys roughly 600,000 of them a year, so volume is sales-driven while rate is cohort-driven, and both lists are true. Any outlet that only ever prints one of them is running a racket. And the toxicology caveat applies double here: drug-positive means detectable, not impaired. THC lingers for days or weeks; testing rates swing from nearly universal to near zero depending on the state. The Government Accountability Office says plainly that available crash drug data cannot reliably prove impairment.[4] The honest reading is that rates identify risky cohorts while counts identify where the casualties concentrate, and the mistake is publishing only one. The share of drivers killed who tested positive for drugs jumped from 28% in 2006 to 44% in 2016, which is exactly why this distinction matters.[3]

So what do you actually do with this? Stop treating drugged driving as somebody else’s problem in a vehicle you would never buy. If one in four drug-positive fatal drivers died in a top-ten mainstream model, the threat is already in your lane, and the interventions worth funding are the ones that move the body count: fleet screening policies for work-truck drivers, and roadside drug testing, which still does not exist in validated form.[4] Prescription, over-the-counter, weed, it does not matter. If you are impaired, stay off the road. Morgue data does not care what kind of high it was.[2]
