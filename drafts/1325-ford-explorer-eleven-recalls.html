The 2026 Ford Explorer Has Been Recalled 11 Times. Four of Them Are the Engine.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 29, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, because the first result looked like a typo, which it was not. Across 77 model-and-model-year queries of the NHTSA recalls API this week, 94 distinct 2026 campaigns surfaced — and the 2026 Ford Explorer appears in 11 of them, more than any other single model-year in the sample.[1]

11
NHTSA campaigns touching the 2026 Explorer — most of any 2026 model in a 77-query census

Four of the eleven are internal engine failures, filed between March 31 and August 25. Read that list slowly: piston circlips installed wrong at the factory (26V205), an unspecified engine failure causing loss of drive power (26V343), connecting rod bearings that seize in the 2.3L (26V532), and piston domes manufactured incorrectly (26V548).[1] Four different defects and four different ways for the engine to quit, all inside five months on one model year.

4
Separate internal-engine recalls on the 2026 Explorer in five months

And the engine is only the opening act. The windshield may detach in a crash because it was never bonded properly (26V024). Headlights that bend the wrong way in curves (26V121). Second-row seat belt anchor bolts may be loose (26V159). The HVAC can die and take the defroster with it (26V202). The second-row seats can unlatch on their own (26V451), the driver’s seat can recline itself during a remote start (26V470), and the passenger airbag’s vent may not open (26V581).[1] Eleven campaigns: practically a punch card.

For context, the Explorer is America’s best-selling three-row SUV — Ford moved 222,706 of them in 2025, and Ford’s own sales releases still call it the segment leader.[2][3] Ford also issued 153 recalls in 2025, the most by any automaker in a single year ever, its fifth straight year atop that particular leaderboard.[4] When your best seller is also your most recalled nameplate, that is not a coincidence the marketing department gets to ignore.

Now the counterargument, at full strength, because it deserves it. In November 2024, NHTSA hit Ford with a $165 million civil penalty for dragging its feet on rear-camera recalls, and the consent order forced the company to re-review three years of past campaigns and expand or refile the narrow ones.[5] Ford says it doubled its safety engineering headcount. On that reading, eleven Explorer campaigns might be the most heavily surveilled quality system in the industry finally doing its job — catching misinstalled circlips that a less-scrutinized manufacturer would have shipped in silence. Campaign counts cannot distinguish “worst quality” from “best detection.”

All of that is fair. But no consent order manufactures a seized connecting rod bearing, and no re-review invents four distinct internal engine defects that all reached paying customers within five months. Detection explains the count, but it does not explain the engines.

What to do: if you own a 2026 Explorer, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today — eleven campaigns is not a typo, and the four engine ones (26V205, 26V343, 26V532, 26V548) are the ones to clear first, because sudden loss of drive power puts you in the crash instead of merely under-protected during it.[6] Shopping a used one in a few years? Engine campaigns follow the VIN forever; a truck with all four remedies documented is a materially different vehicle than one with them open. And the honest caveat: this census covered 77 high-volume model-year combinations, not every 2026 vehicle sold, and campaign counts are not defect rates — they reward diligent reporting exactly as much as they punish bad manufacturing.
