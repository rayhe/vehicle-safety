Her Airbag Was a Counterfeit. A Jury Says Her Life Was Worth $603 Million.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 21, 2026

On June 12, 2026, a jury in Broward County, Florida, awarded $603 million to the family of Destiny Marie Byassee.[1] She was 22 years old, a mother of two, and she died in a minor crash in a 2020 Chevrolet Malibu when the airbag in her steering wheel exploded instead of inflating. She was wearing her seat belt. A metal fragment severed her throat, and she bled to death in a collision she should have walked away from.[1]

$603M
Broward County jury award to the family of Destiny Byassee, 22: $243 million compensatory, $360 million punitive

Byassee bought the Malibu used, and she never knew what was behind its steering wheel. In September 2022, the car crashed hard enough as an Enterprise rental to deploy its driver airbag and fire its seat-belt pretensioner, and her family's lawsuit says it should have been declared a total loss, issued a salvage title, and removed from service.[2] Instead, Enterprise sent it to Manheim Auctions, Manheim sold it to DriveTime, DriveTime hired a shop called Jumbo Automotive to repair it, and Jumbo allegedly installed a counterfeit airbag built by a Chinese company called DTN. Five companies touched that car. Nobody asked what was behind the steering wheel.

While it was in the shop, the lawsuit alleges, somebody also cut the wires to the deployed seat-belt pretensioner instead of replacing it, which means the car Byassee bought had two of its safety systems disabled, and neither one announced itself.[2] DriveTime sold it to her anyway.

DTN stands for Jilin Province Detiannuo Safety Technology Co., a Chinese manufacturer whose inflators are marked DTN60DB. NHTSA has linked those inflators to 11 deaths in 14 crashes, all in collisions the agency calls otherwise survivable, and on April 29, 2026, it issued its first motor-vehicle equipment ban in more than twenty years against them.[6] Every inflator known to have been in a crash has ruptured, sending metal fragments into drivers' chests, necks, eyes, and faces. Byassee's was one of the earliest: her crash came on June 11, 2023, more than two years before NHTSA opened its investigation.[7]

DTN retained a lawyer, filed a response denying the allegations, and then failed to attend its own trial.[1] Jurors heard one side, and they answered with $243 million in compensatory damages and $360 million in punitive damages. Morgan & Morgan, the firm representing Byassee's family, says it believes DTN has collectible assets and intends to pursue them to the fullest. DTN says it does not do business in the United States, that its products are prohibited from sale here, and that the inflators may be counterfeits of its own products.[2]

Fairness demands the other side at full strength, because this verdict proves less than its number suggests. DTN did not attend, so the jury heard one side of a case about parts the defendant says it never made and never sold here. If the inflators really are counterfeits of DTN's products, then the jury held the wrong company liable for someone else's crime, and the $603 million is a message aimed at an empty chair. Nor have the four American companies in the chain, Enterprise, Manheim, DriveTime, and Jumbo, been found liable in any reporting so far, and they are the ones with addresses and assets a court can reach. Against that, two facts hold: DTN filed a written denial and then chose not to defend it, and NHTSA's position is that it does not matter who made the inflators, because every one that has been in a crash has ruptured.[7]

FARS puts the Malibu's ordinariness in numbers: 3,465 deaths across a decade, a rate of 2.03 per 100 million miles, on a fleet of nearly 1.5 million cars.[5] Nothing about the nameplate predicts what happened to Byassee, and that is the point: an ordinary sedan, an ordinary crash, and a safety device that turned lethal because of what a repair shop put behind the steering wheel two owners earlier. Morgan & Morgan has filed three wrongful-death suits over these inflators in three states and was investigating at least three more fatality cases as of June.[4]

If you bought a used car that was in a crash where an airbag deployed, have the airbag inspected by a franchised dealer. A VIN recall lookup cannot detect a counterfeit replacement part, because these inflators were never factory equipment and are tied to no VIN. Look for the pattern NHTSA describes: a salvage or rebuilt title, a fresh steering wheel, and a repair done somewhere other than a dealership. And if a shop offers you a cheap replacement airbag, or your car comes back from a repair with a pretensioner warning light on, stop. A deployed pretensioner must be replaced, never bypassed. Cut wires are a tell that the repair was not a repair.
