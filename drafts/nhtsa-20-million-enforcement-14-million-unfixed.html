The Gap

NHTSA Has $20 Million to Catch Bad Drivers. It Has $0 to Fix 14 Million Recalled Cars.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
August 7, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, NHTSA has decided that dangerous drivers deserve a $20 million enforcement technology program and dangerous vehicles deserve a PDF nobody downloads.

14,000,000
Recalled vehicles still on U.S. roads with known, unrepaired safety defects

On August 3, NHTSA announced its Innovative Traffic Safety Enforcement program: $20 million for local police departments to deploy predictive analytics, high-visibility enforcement campaigns, and "next-generation enforcement models" targeting impaired driving, speeding, distraction, and unbuckled seatbelts.[1] Administrator Jonathan Morrison called it a chance to "stop risky driving behaviors before they lead to tragedy on our roadways." The grant applications opened immediately.

Three months earlier, NHTSA published its own 2025 Annual Recalls Report. It documented 670 recall campaigns covering more than 28 million vehicles. Software and electronics alone triggered 119 campaigns affecting 8.19 million cars. Powertrain defects added another 87 campaigns and 7.99 million.[2] The recall completion rate as of February 2026 stood at 50.3%. Half. Fourteen million vehicles with documented, manufacturer-acknowledged defects were still rolling around without repairs.[3]

The enforcement program to address 14 million unfixed vehicles: nothing. No grant. No predictive analytics. No "next-generation" anything. Federal law prohibits selling a new car with an open recall. It says nothing about used cars. A dealer can hand you the keys to a vehicle with a known fire risk, a fractured steering component, or an airbag that deploys shrapnel, and the transaction is perfectly legal.

Twelve years into the largest recall in automotive history, 4.8 million vehicles still carry Takata airbag inflators that can turn a fender-bender into a fragmentation wound.[4] NHTSA tracks these numbers quarterly. It has no mechanism to compel a single owner to visit a dealership. For recalls affecting more than a million vehicles, the completion rate drops to 49%. For large SUVs, it falls to 33%.[5] A third.

The logic goes like this: bad drivers kill 36,640 people a year, so fund the programs that catch them. Behavioral factors like speeding and impairment show up in roughly two-thirds of fatal crashes. Fair enough. But NHTSA's own FARS data shows the Silverado (9,591 deaths in the dataset), the F-150 (9,194), the Accord (7,102), and the Explorer (3,797) at the top of the body count.[6] These same models cycle through recall campaigns every year. When a Silverado with an unfixed power steering defect meets a speeding driver, both failure modes collide. The $20 million targets only one of them.

The reasonable counterargument: behavioral factors cause roughly two-thirds of fatal crashes, and enforcement grants target behaviors. Recall completion is a manufacturer responsibility, not a policing problem. Different budget lines, different mechanisms. All true. But NHTSA is the agency tasked with both, and the contrast in urgency is the point. When police need new tools to catch drunk drivers, there is a press release, an acronym, and a grant portal. When 14 million cars need to get to a dealer for a free repair, there is a quarterly completion rate table buried in a PDF.

NHTSA did not announce what the cost-per-life-saved projection is for the ITSE program. It did not compare that figure to requiring used car dealers to disclose open recalls at point of sale, funding mobile recall repair units, or blocking registration renewal for vehicles with critical safety recalls. Each proposal has surfaced in Congressional testimony over the past decade. None has become law.

What you should do: Enter your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today. If you are buying a used vehicle, request proof that all open recalls have been completed. No federal law requires the seller to provide this, but Carfax and the NHTSA VIN lookup are free. If your vehicle has an unfixed recall marked "park outside," NHTSA means it. Park outside.
