Speeding Killed 11,288 People Last Year. The Drivers Were Drunk, Unlicensed, and Unbelted Too.

Dale Impactor III • Toxicology Desk Chief
November 9, 2026

Somebody died speeding in America about once every 47 minutes last year. NHTSA's Speeding: 2024 Data fact sheet counts 11,288 people killed in speeding-related crashes in 2024, 29 percent of the entire national traffic toll, and the number that should stop you cold is not the total. It is the company the speeder keeps.[1]

37%
Of speeding drivers in fatal crashes were alcohol-impaired (BAC .08 or higher), more than double the 17% rate for drivers who were not speeding. (NHTSA, DOT HS 813 823)

Speeding drivers in fatal crashes were drunk 2.2 times as often as non-speeders. They were unlicensed 1.9 times as often: 27 percent had no valid license at the time of the crash, against 14 percent for everyone else. And half of them were unbelted, 50 percent against 21 percent, which means the person doing 90 in a 55 was also the person who decided the seat belt was optional. Stack the three and you get the profile that actually kills people. It is not a driver who misjudged a curve. It is a driver who ignored every rule in the book at once, and the speeding ticket is just the one a cop can write fastest.[1]

Speeding drivers in fatal crashes were also repeaters, more likely than non-speeders to carry previously recorded crashes, license suspensions or revocations, and speeding or DWI convictions in the five years before the crash that killed someone. NHTSA's own words, not mine. One in four speeding drivers was at more than twice the legal alcohol limit, BAC .15 or higher, against one in nine non-speeders. Among speeding drivers aged 25 to 34, the highest of any age group, 43 percent were alcohol-impaired. Speeding does not travel alone. It travels with a record.[1]

Now the trend, because the headline number actually fell. Speeding deaths dropped 5 percent in 2024, from 11,913 to 11,288, and that is real progress in the same direction as the overall toll. But zoom out one notch and the celebration curdles. In 2019, speeding killed 9,592 people. Then the roads emptied in 2020 and the speeding deaths jumped to 11,428, then 12,498 in 2021, a 30 percent surge in two years. By 2024 the toll had eased to 11,288, still 18 percent above 2019. Total traffic deaths over the same span rose only 8 percent. Speeding grew more than twice as fast as the overall toll since 2019, and its share of the dead climbed from 26 percent to 29 percent. Whatever the empty highways taught drivers in 2020, they kept the lesson.[1][2]

Young men own the worst of it. Among male drivers aged 15 to 20 in fatal crashes, 39 percent were speeding, the highest rate of any age and sex group in the fact sheet. For young women the same age it was 20 percent, also the highest for their sex. Overall, 21 percent of male drivers in fatal crashes were speeding against 12 percent of female drivers. Motorcyclists were the worst vehicle type by the same measure: 37 percent of riders in fatal crashes were speeding, against 22 percent of passenger car drivers, 15 percent of light truck drivers, and 8 percent of large truck drivers. One in nine riders in a fatal crash was both speeding and drunk. One in ten was speeding and unhelmeted.[1]

Where it happens will surprise anyone who pictures a speeder on an interstate. Only 13 percent of speeding-related fatalities happened on interstate highways. Eighty-seven percent happened on ordinary roads, non-interstate urban streets accounting for 5,775 deaths and non-interstate rural roads for 3,985. Speeding kills least on the fastest roads in the country, because the margin on a two-lane road is thinner and the consequences arrive sooner. It also kills at night, on weekends, and in work zones: 23 percent of drivers in fatal crashes during nighttime weekend hours were speeding, against 15 percent on weekday days, and speeding was a factor in 34 percent of fatal crashes in construction and maintenance zones, against 28 percent outside them.[1]

November is the right month to read the surface-condition table. On dry roads, 18 percent of drivers in fatal crashes were speeding; on wet roads, 23 percent; on snow or slush, 35 percent; and on ice or frost, 38 percent. Read that last number twice, because "speeding" on ice does not mean 90 in a 55. It means too fast for conditions, and the posted limit is a dry-road number. Drive the limit on black ice and you are speeding by the fact sheet's own definition, and the crash report will say so.[1]

States disagree with each other by almost five to one. In Vermont, 54 percent of all traffic deaths were speeding-related; in Florida, 11 percent. Alaska sat at 50 percent, Montana at 43, North Carolina and Hawaii at 42. Texas had the most speeding deaths in absolute terms, 1,496, or 13 percent of the national speeding toll in a single state. And the belt gap follows the speeder everywhere: among speeding passenger-vehicle drivers, Idaho's unrestrained share hit 77 percent, while Nevada's was 30 percent. In every state, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico, speeding drivers were drunk more often than non-speeding drivers, with no exceptions anywhere in the country.[1]

Fairness requires the other side, stated at full strength, because this whole edifice rests on a cop's checkbox. "Speeding-related" is not a measurement. It is a judgment made at a crash scene, often after the fact, and it is applied unevenly: a drunk, unlicensed, unbelted driver who dies in a wreck is far more likely to get "speeding" written on the report than a sober commuter in the same ditch, because the officer already has a theory of the crash. Some of the compound-offender pattern may be selection bias wearing a lab coat. On ice and snow, "too fast for conditions" turns ordinary winter driving into a speeding statistic, so the 38 percent may say as much about physics as about recklessness. If the coding is biased, 11,288 is a soft number and the ratios are softer. None of that makes the toll disappear. It makes the toll harder to count, which is a different problem, and a worse one.[1]

So what do you do with a number like this? If you are the parent of a 15-to-20-year-old boy, know that he is in the single highest-risk speeding group on the road, and the conversation is not "don't speed" in the abstract. It is Friday night, it is midnight to 3 a.m., and it is the first ice of the season. If you drive in winter, cut the posted limit in half on ice; the fact sheet's own definition says the limit is not the safe speed when the road is frozen. Slow down in work zones, where a third of fatal crashes involved speeding. And if you are buying a used car for a teenager, remember the profile: the speeding driver's car is usually also the unbelted, unlicensed, drunk driver's car. Horsepower is not the intervention. The belt habit, the license status, and the keys on a Friday night are.[1]
