**Ship target:** 2026-10-07

## The story in one line
Congress ordered NHTSA in 2021 to mandate passive drunk-driving detection in every new car, with a final rule due Nov 15, 2024. It is now Sep 7, 2026: 661 days past deadline, zero rules issued, and NHTSA's own March 2026 report to Congress admits no commercial technology meets the passive standard.

## Kill test
Genuinely newsworthy: a statutory deadline 21+ months in the past, a fresh (March 2026) NHTSA report openly admitting the tech isn't ready, an active docket (modified June 9, 2026), and real 2026 congressional repeal attempts. Novel angle: nobody has published the delay-window death arithmetic, and the honest audit that the IIHS "9,409 lives" figure assumes instant 100% fleet penetration (a fantasy). Axle's "I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again" frame fits perfectly.

## Verified facts (with sources)

1. **The law:** Section 24220 of the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (enacted Nov 15, 2021) directs NHTSA to issue a final rule prescribing an FMVSS requiring "advanced drunk and impaired driving prevention technology" in new passenger vehicles "not later than 3 years after the date of enactment" — deadline Nov 15, 2024. (NHTSA Report to Congress, https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/2023-07/Report-to-Congress-Advanced-Impaired-Driving-Prevention-Technology_07-17-23.pdf)

2. **Deadline missed.** "NHTSA missed its own November 2024 rule-making deadline." (CBT News, https://www.cbtnews.com/controversial-kill-switch-mandate-lives-on/). The stateofsurveillance.org tracker notes the September 2027 enforcement date in the law is a "no later than" date keyed to the FMVSS effective date plus three years — with no effective date, "the clock has not started." (http://stateofsurveillance.org/news/nhtsa-section-24220-impaired-driving-prevention-rulemaking-status-2026/)

3. **Docket activity:** NHTSA-2022-0079, "Advanced Impaired Driving Technology," RIN 2127-AM50, disposition Pending, last modified June 9, 2026. ANPRM published Jan 5, 2024 (89 FR 830); 18,367 comments submitted — "one of the most-commented NHTSA dockets of the last decade." Dec 15, 2025: separate Paperwork Reduction Act notice (90 FR 58079) on developing an impairment-detection *telltale* (dashboard indicator) — procedural, not the rule. (stateofsurveillance.org)

4. **NHTSA's own tech assessment (Feb/March 2026 Report to Congress):** "neither report found commercially available technology that detects driver alcohol impairment accurately and passively." "To date, there are no in-vehicle technologies in production that can measure BAC or BrAC at or above 0.08 g/dL passively." Current systems "have yet to demonstrate the precision, speed, and reliability needed to meet the IIJA mandate or the Safety Act." DADSS test vehicles exist in a few states (VA, MD pilots; Schneider fleet trial) but are not production. (https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/2026-03/Report-to-Congress-Advanced-Impaired-Driving-Prevention-Technology.pdf)

5. **The false-positive problem:** "Currently, detection technology around the legal limit continues to have an error rate that would be unacceptably high" — NHTSA, quoted in Carscoops March 2026. (https://www.carscoops.com/2026/03/nhtsa-impaired-driving-detection-tech/)

6. **DADSS's broken promise:** DADSS projected the ambient-air system "as an option" in new vehicles as early as 2025 (technical.ly, https://technical.ly/?p=42360). 2025 came and went; NHTSA's 2026 report says nothing commercial qualifies.

7. **IIHS savings estimate (Farmer 2020):** alcohol-detection systems that work perfectly in all vehicles, restricting BAC below 0.08, "could prevent more than 9,000 deaths a year" — 9,409/year based on 2015–2018 FARS data. Also: interlocks only for drivers with a conviction in the past 5 years would avert a maximum of 837 deaths/year; only for commercial/government/rental vehicles, 348/year. (Consumer Reports, https://www.consumerreports.org/car-safety/alcohol-detection-systems-in-cars-could-prevent-many-deaths/)

8. **Congressional repeal attempts:** Rep. Thomas Massie's defunding amendment failed 164–268 in January 2026 (57 Republicans against). Rep. Roy introduced an amendment to repeal the mandate. A House bill to repeal outright remains in committee. Mandate stands. (reason.com, https://reason.com/2026/04/29/all-new-cars-could-have-mandatory-surveillance-tech-unless-congress-stops-this-mandate/)

9. **2024 alcohol death baseline:** 11,904 alcohol-impaired driving deaths in 2024 (NHTSA DOT HS 813 815, June 2026 — used in our #817). Total 2024 deaths 39,254, rate 1.19/100M VMT (NHTSA Quick Facts 2024, https://crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov/Api/Public/ViewPublication/813809).

## Novel contribution (the numbers I ran)

- **Delay window:** Nov 15, 2024 → Sep 7, 2026 = 661 days (365 + 296).
- **Deaths in the delay window:** 11,904 ÷ 366 days (2024 leap year) = 32.5/day × 661 days = **21,499 ≈ 21,500** alcohol-impaired deaths since the statutory deadline.
- **IIHS ceiling over the same window:** 9,409/yr × 661/366 = **≈17,000** — the theoretical maximum if the fantasy scenario (perfect tech, 100% of vehicles, instantly) had held.
- **The honest audit:** even if NHTSA had met the deadline, the law gives automakers ~3 years after the final rule, so no production car would carry the system before ~late 2027. The delay's real cost is a shifted compliance horizon, not deaths caused by the delay itself — I state this plainly (it is the counterargument to my own headline math).
- **Comment-to-rule ratio:** 18,367 public comments → 0 final rules. One of the most-commented dockets of the decade produced a Paperwork Reduction Act notice about a dashboard telltale.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
The delay is not (only) bureaucratic foot-dragging: the technology genuinely cannot do what the law requires. A passive, accurate, maintenance-free alcohol sensor that never strands a sober driver does not exist commercially, and NHTSA's mandate under the Safety Act requires standards to be practicable. Rushing a rule the tech can't meet would strand sober drivers at the grocery store, which is arguably worse than the status quo. Also, the IIHS 9,409 figure assumes a fantasy (instant 100% penetration); with real fleet turnover (~1–2% of the 290M-vehicle fleet replaced per year... keep qualitative, avoid uncited fleet number), the realistic first-decade yield is far smaller. And Congress wrote the extension mechanism into the statute — the "missed deadline" narrative ignores that the law anticipates exactly this.

## Limitations to state explicitly
- Delay-window death count projects 2024 rates forward; 2025–2026 actuals not yet final.
- IIHS 9,409 is based on 2015–2018 crash data and assumes perfect tech in 100% of vehicles — a fantasy ceiling, not a forecast.
- Cannot attribute any specific death to the delay; the counterfactual (rule on time → lives saved by now) fails on the 3-year compliance lag.
- DADSS timelines and pilot mileages come from program announcements, not independent audits.

## Actionable takeaways
- The mandate is alive; repeal efforts failed. When the NPRM eventually opens, it will be the highest-leverage comment opportunity in vehicle safety — 18,367 comments on the ANPRM show the public is watching.
- What works today: ignition interlocks for convicted offenders exist now, and all-offender interlock laws are the proven tool — IIHS: 837 deaths/yr max from that scenario alone, available without waiting for a sensor breakthrough.
- Fleet operators: Driven to Protect pilots (VA, MD, Schneider) are the only real-world passive systems running; the tech is test-fleet only, not showroom-ready.
