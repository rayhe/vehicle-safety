Toyota Recalled 62,000 Tundras to Find 300 Bad Camshafts

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 14, 2026

Numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. Toyota announced on November 13, 2026 that it is recalling about 62,000 non-hybrid Tundras because an engine camshaft can snap while driving, cutting motive power at highway speed. Read the next sentence in Toyota's own release and the story changes shape: only about 300 of those trucks actually carry a suspect camshaft. Sixty-two thousand trucks go to the dealer so mechanics can find 300 parts.[1]

300
Suspect camshafts hiding in a 62,000-truck recall, one supplier lot, one cylinder bank

Toyota's words, verbatim: the trucks "could be equipped with an engine camshaft that was manufactured incorrectly, which could snap during driving." A leaked dealer notice adds the details Toyota left out of the press release. Every suspect camshaft sits on cylinder bank one. All of them came from one production lot from one supplier, a supplier Toyota has not named. Dealers inspect the camshaft and replace it free if it may come from the affected lot. Owners get letters by mid-January 2027, but the VIN lookup works today.[1][2]

Do the arithmetic and the recall looks less like a dragnet and more like a confession. Three hundred divided by 62,000 is 0.48 percent. Dealers must inspect roughly 207 trucks to find one bad camshaft. That ratio is the whole story in miniature: Toyota can trace the defect to a single lot, but it cannot trace the lot to individual trucks without opening 62,000 engine bays. Precision at the supplier, guesswork at the dealership. Somewhere between the factory floor and the service bay, the paperwork that would have named 300 trucks got lost, and 61,700 innocent owners pay for the gap with an afternoon at the dealer.[1]

Four engine recalls on the same engine, and you should count them again, because the table is the indictment. Toyota replaced the 5.7-liter i-FORCE V8, an engine with a reputation for outliving its owners, with the V35A twin-turbo V6 in 2022. Three bearing-debris waves later, totaling roughly 270,000 vehicles, the fourth recall arrives for a different part with a different failure on a different set of trucks. Add the camshaft wave and the V35A recall total reaches about 332,000 Tundras. Some engines in the second bearing wave were built after Toyota claimed to have fixed the manufacturing process. Now the process has produced a camshaft that snaps.[4][5]

Then there is the hole in the model-year list, and holes are where the truth leaks out. Camshaft recall covers 2022, 2023, 2025, and 2026, skipping 2024 entirely while hybrids are out. Sequoias with the same 3.4-liter twin-turbo V6 are out. Meanwhile the May 2026 bearing recall covered only 2024 non-hybrid trucks. Two engine recalls, zero overlap in model years, same engine family, same factory logic. If you own a 2024 Tundra, you are not in this recall. You were in the last one.[2][3]

Fairness requires the other side, stated at full strength, because 62,000 sounds alarming and the truth is quieter. Toyota estimates only about 300 trucks have the bad part, and no failures have been publicly confirmed. Toyota has not said what surfaced the defect, or whether any owner has yet had a camshaft snap. A camshaft that has survived three or four years of driving without breaking is probably not going to break tomorrow. For any individual owner, the risk is tiny. What the fourth engine recall says is not that your truck will break. It says Toyota's V35A quality control has now failed four different ways, and the company that built its name on engines that never broke is recalling engines that break in ways it keeps discovering one lot at a time.

What the data does not prove: no NHTSA campaign number or Part 573 filing was public as of November 13, so the 62,000 and 300 figures are Toyota's estimates and may change. No confirmed camshaft failures, crashes, or injuries have been disclosed; the snap-at-speed failure mode is Toyota's stated risk, not an observed event count. Toyota has not named the supplier or identified the production lot publicly, so owners cannot self-screen. Only a dealer inspection settles it. FARS carries no Tundra-specific fatality-rate entry in this site's dataset, so no crash-outcome cross-tab is possible for this model.

What to do with all of this. Own a 2022, 2023, 2025, or 2026 non-hybrid Tundra? Run the VIN at Toyota.com/recall or nhtsa.gov/recalls now. Inspection is free, and a snapped camshaft at highway speed means a loss of drive power with no warning. Own a 2024 non-hybrid Tundra? You are not in this recall, but check the May 2026 bearing-debris recall if you have not already. Own a hybrid Tundra or a Sequoia? Also not in this recall: same engine, different lot, different recall. Owner letters go out by mid-January 2027, but the VIN lookup works today. Do not wait for the letter.
