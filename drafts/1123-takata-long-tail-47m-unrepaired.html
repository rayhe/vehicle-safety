The Takata Recall Is Being Finished by the Junkyard, Not by You

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better. Carfax's September 2026 tracker counts 4.7 million American vehicles still carrying unrepaired Takata airbags, eleven years into the largest auto recall in U.S. history.[1] Since the recall's ten-year mark in 2024, when Carfax counted more than 6 million, the pool has shrunk about 1.3 million. That sounds like progress until you act like an actuary. The fleet is 10 to 25 years old, Honda's own review of fourteen campaigns shows completion decaying to roughly 33 percent by the ninth and tenth year of ownership,[2] and nobody believes the owners of 2004 Accords are suddenly showing up at dealerships. Much of that 1.3 million didn't get repaired. It got scrapped.

650,000
vehicles per year leaving the unrepaired pool: most of them to the crusher, not the dealer

Do the division: 1.3 million over two years is 650,000 a year, which zeroes the pool around 2033 if the line stays straight. It won't. Removal rates slow as the easy cars disappear, and scrappage does the easy part first, leaving the hardest stratum: aging cars that change hands through private sales and corner lots, where nobody checks a VIN and the owner is, in Carfax's words, harder to locate.[1] The remaining 4.7 million are not a random sample. They sit where compliance is lowest and risk is highest.

50%
rupture rate of "Alpha" inflators in 2001–2003 Honda and Acura models: a coin flip that sprays metal shards

Meanwhile Honda has opened a second front in the junkyard, buying back more than 535,000 recalled inflators from salvage yards through an intermediary, because dead Takatas kept crawling back onto living cars.[2] In 2024 BMW recalled nearly 395,000 3 Series over sport steering wheels with unapproved Takata modules installed by owners; on September 24 eBay banned all airbag listings after a federal inquiry into counterfeits.[1] This is the part that reads like a zombie movie: you kill the part, and it shows up in somebody else's steering wheel.

Worst of all, the residual pool contains the Alpha inflators: driver airbags in certain 2001-2003 Honda and Acura models rupturing at up to 50 percent, per Honda's fact sheet citing NHTSA and Takata.[2] Fifty percent. A coin flip that sprays metal shards into a cabin. NHTSA has confirmed 28 U.S. deaths and 400-plus injuries, warned in February that even minor crashes can detonate them, and says the explosion risk grows as the airbag ages.[3] These are the oldest, most degraded inflators in the most neglected cars. If that describes a car in your driveway, stop driving it. Honda and Stellantis will tow it free or send a technician to you.[1]

Run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls twice a year; NHTSA says so. Buying a used 2001-2016 Honda or Acura from a private seller? Demand proof the recall is closed before money changes hands. Caveats, stated straight: Carfax's counts are registration-based estimates, not a VIN census; my 650,000-per-year figure blends repairs with scrappage and the split is unknowable from public data; NHTSA's death count is confirmed-only and may undercount; inflator counts and vehicle counts are different denominators. Even after the caveats, the shape of the curve holds. The recall's long tail is being amputated, not healed.
