The Safest Cars in America Are Mostly Just the Newest Ones

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 16, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they did not get better. By the figures on this site, the Tesla Model Y is the safest vehicle in America, with a death rate of 0.03 per 100 million miles, about eighteen times lower than the median car. One problem: the Model Y did not exist for most of the decade that number covers.[1]

2.5×
How much the Model Y’s published death rate is understated. It gets a ten-year denominator for four years of driving.

Every death rate on this site comes from one formula. FARS deaths from 2014 through 2023, divided by estimated fleet times class-average annual miles times ten years. Ten years, every model, no exceptions. Our processing script hard-codes the window: total miles equal registered vehicles times annual miles times the number of FARS years. A car that launched in 2020 contributes four years of deaths and gets divided by ten years of driving. Its rate comes out two and a half times lower than it should.[1]

Seven of the fifteen lowest-rate models on the leaderboard launched in 2017 or later. Correct the calendar and the top of the list reshuffles:

Model Y deliveries began in March 2020, the Telluride arrived as a 2020 model, and the Seltos and Bronco are 2021 launches, so their published rates are flattered by a factor of two and a half to three and a third.[2][3][4]

None of this means the leaderboard is fiction. Correct the math and the newcomers still land between 0.07 and 0.17, three to seven times below the 0.54 median across all 337 models. New cars genuinely are safer. Automatic emergency braking cuts rear-end crashes roughly in half, according to IIHS, and the newest systems outperform the first generation.[5] Two cars at the top needed no correction at all. The Porsche Macan posts 0.03 on nine years of exposure, seven deaths total. The Subaru Crosstrek posts 0.08 on a full decade. Some low rates are earned, not borrowed from the calendar.

One number should have tipped us off years ago. The Ram 3500 posts a death rate of exactly zero: zero deaths across 440,000 trucks. Heavy-duty pickups probably fall outside how FARS codes passenger vehicles, but a zero on a leaderboard is a confession, not a result. And the correction itself is approximate, since launch years count in calendar years, fleets grew over the window, and the fleet estimates underneath every rate are binned guesses we have already written about. FARS also counts fatal crashes only, so a car can be gentle on fatalities and brutal on injuries, and this table would never know.

What to do: ignore any safest-car list built on per-mile rates unless it says how it handled exposure. IIHS publishes driver death rates per million registered vehicle years, a denominator that actually respects the calendar. Cross-check Top Safety Pick+ status before you cross-check a rate. And if a model has only existed for three years, treat its death rate the way you would treat a restaurant review written on opening night. Technically data. Mostly hope.
