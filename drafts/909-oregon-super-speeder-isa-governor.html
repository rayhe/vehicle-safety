Suspended Licenses Don't Stop Speeders. Oregon Wants to Govern the Car Instead.

By Vin Wreckage • Existential Dread Columnist
September 16, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: license suspension is America's favorite punishment for dangerous drivers, and NCHRP research cited by advocates says roughly 75% of suspended drivers keep driving anyway.[1] It's a paperwork penalty for a physics problem. Oregon has decided to stop filing paperwork and start governing the accelerator instead.

On September 8, 2026, Representative Dacia Grayber joined Families for Safe Streets, Oregon Walks, and 1,000 Friends of Oregon to present the "Stop Super Speeders" bill concept to the state Senate Judiciary Committee.[2] Headed for the 2027 session, the bill would require the worst speeders to install Intelligent Speed Assistance, a device that talks to the accelerator and refuses to let the car exceed the limit: not a fine, not a lecture, but a governor bolted into the machine.

Before Oregon made the argument, Ocean Parkway in Brooklyn had already made the case. One driver: nearly 100 traffic violations in two years, 18 of them for speeding in school zones, a suspended license, and a car doing more than double the posted limit when she killed a mother and two children.[3] The system didn't fail to find her; it failed to touch the pedal.

We have, in fact, already run this experiment: it's called the ignition interlock, and it's now required for repeat drunk drivers in all 50 states.[4] The regulatory theory is identical: when the driver cannot be trusted, govern the car. Nobody claims interlocks ended drunk driving, and nobody pretends suspending a drunk driver's license worked either. Families for Safe Streets is running the MADD playbook step for step: start with the worst offenders, prove the device, expand. And the theory holds for exactly the same reason alcohol interlocks work: a device does not care about your intentions.

The numbers make the case uncomfortable: Oregon lost 587 people to crashes in 2023, with advocates attributing 32% to speeding,[2] and a New York City municipal fleet pilot found ISA cut speeding events by 64%.[5] The physics is merciless: kinetic energy scales with the square of velocity, so holding a super speeder to 70 instead of 100 more than halves the energy of the crash that would otherwise happen; 100 mph versus 70 mph is 2.04 times the kinetic energy, same car, same road, half the physics.

Now the honest objections, at full strength, because the device camp would rather you not dwell here. Cost comes first: about $150 to install, $150 a month to keep running, paid by the driver.[2] Washington, DC and Virginia subsidize drivers who can't pay; Oregon's concept says nothing about subsidies yet, which makes this a regressive fee wearing a safety costume. Tamper resistance is a statutory requirement and a real-world unknown: Rhode Island's bill mandates tamper-resistant, circumvention-reporting devices, but nobody has defeat-rate data at scale.[6] Active ISA allows temporary override for merging, so a determined driver can still speed in bursts. And Virginia's own history is the warning shot: Governor Youngkin signed the nation's first state active-ISA law but stripped the mandatory-device provision for 100-plus-mph tickets before signing, meaning the exact trigger aimed at the worst offenders was the first thing negotiated away.[3]

Then there's the objection nobody says out loud: we already accept governing the car for alcohol. A breathalyzer that decides whether your engine starts is one of the most intrusive things a government can bolt into a private vehicle, and we normalized it in all 50 states without a national uprising. Nobody built a romantic identity around sipping a beer before the ignition, but speed still feels like freedom, and everyone has an opinion about the left lane. Your freedom to do 100 ends where someone else's mother begins. Apparently that sentence needed a device to be said out loud.

Here's what to do with this: if you live in Virginia, know that active ISA is already a judge's-discretion alternative to suspension or jail, in effect since July 1, 2026.[5] In DC, the STEER Act has run since September 2025, and the District pays for drivers who can't afford the device. If your state is one of the 16 with active ISA legislation in 2026, read the bill text before the hearing, not after the crash. And if you're shopping for a car, know that the EU has required speed-assist warnings on all new cars since 2024, and the hardware is shipping here too, so ask your dealer what it does. Either way, the governor is coming; the only question is whether it arrives as a warning beep or a mandate.
