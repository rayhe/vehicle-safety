The Gap

1,103 Cyclists Died in 2024. Most of Them Died in the Dark.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

Federal data says cyclist deaths fell 6% in 2024, and that is technically true. 1,103 riders died, down from 1,173 the year before. What the headline leaves out is that 2023 was the worst year for cyclists since the government started counting in 1975, which makes 2024 the third-worst year ever recorded.[1][2] A 6% decline from the record is not a recovery; it is a slightly less catastrophic year.

Look at when they died: more cyclists were killed in the dark (52%) than in daylight (44%).[1] Cyclists are the only group on American roads who die more often at night than by day, and the deadliest window is 6 to 9 p.m., the ride home, when the light is gone and the traffic is not. NHTSA's own advice for the problem is a front light, a red rear reflector, and bright clothing. That is the entire federal plan for a 52% problem.

Now look at what hit them: of the 1,035 cyclists killed in single-vehicle crashes, light trucks struck 552. Passenger cars struck 282.[1] SUVs accounted for 292 of the light-truck kills, pickups 220. Do the division and light trucks killed nearly twice as many riders as cars, and 84% of the dead were hit by the front of the vehicle. This is an engineering story before it is a behavior story. A modern pickup's hood sits at a grown man's chest height with a blind zone stretching yards ahead of the bumper, and the thing it cannot see at 7 p.m. is a rider in dark clothes on an unlit road. Every one of those front-end strikes is a sensor and sightline failure wearing a crash report.

Sixty percent of the dead were killed away from intersections, mid-block, where there is no signal, no crosswalk, and usually no bike lane. Eighty-one percent died in urban areas, and Florida alone buried 208 riders, 19% of the national total, while Louisiana's death rate per capita ran triple the national average.[1] Tucson, Arizona, posted the worst big-city rate in the country at 1.44 deaths per 100,000 people, 4.5 times the national figure. Seven major cities, Dallas and Seattle among them, reported zero cyclist deaths in 2024, which proves the toll is a local design choice rather than a law of nature.

Who dies is just as skewed: eighty-seven percent were men, and the average age of a killed rider was 48. Riders 65 and older accounted for 237 deaths, more than one in five.[1] This is not the spandex stereotype. It is a middle-aged man riding home from work, or a retiree on an evening ride, meeting a vehicle whose front end was designed around a crash test that never included him.

Alcohol shows up in a third of fatal cyclist crashes, counting the driver, the rider, or both.[1] Twenty-one percent of the killed riders had alcohol in their system. Helmets matter too: IIHS found 68% of the cyclists killed in 2024 were not wearing one.[2] Those are real numbers, and they are also the numbers everyone reaches for first because they blame the dead, but they explain a minority of the toll. No amount of helmet wearing changes the fact that a pickup's blind zone starts at the bumper and the rider's protection ends at his skull.

One more number cuts against the good-news headline. Injuries rose 6% in 2024, to an estimated 52,887, while deaths fell 6%.[1] Injuries per death climbed from 42.6 to 47.9 in a single year. Crashes did not get rarer; they got slightly less lethal, or slightly better reported. Pick whichever explanation you like; neither one is a safety program working.

The counterargument, at full strength: the light-truck finding is partly a fleet artifact, since light trucks are now the majority of vehicles sold, so they strike more of everything, not just cyclists. Exposure is unmeasured, nobody knows how many miles Americans cycled in 2024, so the death rate per mile cannot be computed and a 6% decline against unknown mileage proves nothing about per-mile risk. Evening is also when the most people ride, which inflates the darkness share. And 2023 may have been a post-pandemic spike unwinding rather than a trend. All fair. None of it erases the shape of the data: a 2-to-1 kill ratio for light trucks over cars, a dark-over-daylight majority that appears in no other road-user group, and a death toll 73% above the 2010 low while total traffic deaths rose only 19% over the same span.[2]

What this does not prove: the fact sheet cannot say whether the dead riders were in bike lanes, because it does not record that. It cannot apportion blame between driver and rider in individual crashes. The 2024 figures come from the FARS Annual Report File and are subject to revision when the Final File lands. NHTSA counts 1,103 deaths and IIHS counts 1,075; the 28-death gap is a boundary difference, NHTSA has counted motorized-bicycle riders as pedalcyclists since 2022 and IIHS does not. And the injury figures are survey estimates with sampling error, not counts.

What This Means for You

If you ride, the cheapest life insurance in cycling is a front light and a flashing red rear light, used every ride after 5 p.m., plus reflective gear. Two-thirds of killed riders wore no helmet, so wear one. If you drive a pickup or SUV, understand that your blind zone is measured in yards and your automatic emergency braking was probably never tested against a cyclist in the dark; slow down at dusk and check twice before turning across a bike lane. If you are shopping for a family vehicle, ask the dealer for the pedestrian and cyclist crash-prevention rating, not just the star count. And if your city has a bike-lane plan sitting in a drawer, this data is the argument for building it: 60% of the dead were killed mid-block, exactly where a separated lane would have put concrete between them and the truck.
