## Story in one line
The Toyota Avalon — discontinued after 2022, replaced by the Crown — kills at 1.33 deaths per 100M miles, 34% slower than its little brother the Camry (2.03), even though Avalon drivers in fatal crashes test impaired at 21.7% against the Camry's 19.2%. The bigger Toyota, driven by drunker drivers, is the safer Toyota. Toyota killed the safer car.

## Key verified facts (FARS 2014–2023)
- **Avalon body count:** 469 deaths, rate **1.33** deaths per 100M VMT, fleet 306,250, crashes 686, lethality 0.684 deaths per fatal-crash involvement. Rank by rate: 271st of 337 (i.e. 67th-highest); rank by deaths: far down the table (small fleet), which is the point — the rate is what matters and the rate beats the Camry by a third.
- **The Camry comparison (the piece's spine):** same maker, same Kentucky plant lineage, stretched version of the same platform, Avalon the bigger car. Camry: 6,328 deaths, rate 2.03, fleet 2,712,500, lethality 0.593. Avalon/Camry rate ratio 0.66x — the Avalon kills 34% slower per mile. The Avalon's fleet is one-ninth the Camry's, so the raw count (469 vs 6,328) understates the Avalon's record; the rate is the honest comparator.

  Avalon impairment (21.7%) is the second-highest in the table, behind only the Impreza (21.9%), and its alcohol-positive share (16.2%) is the highest of the Toyotas in the set. Yet its rate (1.33) sits in the bottom third of the table. Impairment spans 17.6–21.9% while rates span 0.40–3.07 — the same 4.3-point impairment band against a 7.7x rate spread, now with the Avalon proving the spread runs the other way too. The drivers are worse; the car is better. The gap is the vehicle, not the driver.
- **The Lexus ES twin:** the ES shares the Avalon's extended platform (since 2013) and rates 1.14 with 402 deaths — the two big-Toyota-platform sedans cluster at the safe end of the table while the Camry sits mid-pack. Platform, not badge: the ES costs more and rates about the same as the Avalon.
- **Lethality note (disclosed, not over-claimed):** the Avalon's lethality (0.684 deaths per fatal crash) is HIGHER than the Camry's (0.593). When an Avalon is in a fatal crash, more people die per crash — consistent with an older buyer profile carrying more passengers or older occupants, who die more easily. The Avalon's advantage is entirely in crash frequency per mile (fewer fatal crashes), not in survivability once a fatal crash happens. The article must say this plainly: the Avalon keeps you out of the file, but it does not make the file gentler.
- **Model-year trend (FARS_MODEL_YEAR; model-year sums total 454 of 469):** 1990s 76; 2000s 268; 2010s 104; 2020s 6. Peak single model year: 2006, 39 deaths. Recent years: 2015: 13; 2016: 13; 2018: 7; 2019: 6; 2020: 6 (2017 records zero deaths in the file).
- **Exposure-year adjustment (original computation):** dividing each recent model year's deaths by its years on the road inside the 2014–2023 window: 2015: 1.4/yr; 2016: 1.6/yr; 2018: 1.2/yr; 2019: 1.2/yr; 2020: 1.5/yr — the 2015–2020 cohort runs ~1.2–1.6 per exposure-year. The 2005–2008 cohort: 2005: 2.3/yr; 2006: 3.9/yr; 2007: 2.1/yr; 2008: 3.2/yr — ~2.1–3.9 per exposure-year. The newer Avalon kills at roughly half the annual rate of the 2000s car. Method note: assumes roughly uniform annual sales per model year and full-year exposure; treat as directional. The 2019–2022 fifth generation (TNGA-K platform, Toyota Safety Sense 2.5 standard) is the safest Avalon ever built, and it is the one Toyota stopped selling.
- **The lab record (2022, verified):** the final Avalon earned IIHS Top Safety Pick with Good in every crashworthiness test and Superior front crash prevention; NHTSA rated it 5 stars overall (4 frontal / 5 side / 4 rollover). Unlike the Camry story, the lab and the road agree here: the Avalon is good in both. The 2026 Camry holds the higher award (Top Safety Pick+, #1551) while the Avalon holds the lower one (Top Safety Pick) — and the Avalon still kills slower on the road. The award gap runs opposite to the death-rate gap.
- **The discontinuation:** Toyota ended US Avalon production after model year 2022 (production April 2018–August 2022 for the fifth generation) and replaced it with the Crown crossover sedan for 2023. A used 2019–2022 Avalon now sells for roughly $23,000–$36,000 (J.D. Power, via TopSpeed). The safest big Toyota sedan is a used-car buy now.

## What this does NOT prove (limitations)
- FARS rates use estimated fleet and estimated VMT, not odometer readings; ±15% uncertainty for low-volume models. The Avalon's 306,250 fleet is small — its 1.33 rate carries wider uncertainty than the Camry's 2.03, though the gap (34%) exceeds the uncertainty band.
- Impairment percentages come from drivers in fatal crashes with toxicology results; testing rates vary by state and year. The inversion shows impairment cannot explain the Avalon/Camry gap; it does not show impairment is irrelevant.
- The weight explanation is a mechanism, not a proof: the Avalon is ~120–265 lb heavier than the Camry, but buyer age, trip mix (more highway miles), and equipment (standard Toyota Safety Sense 2.5 on the fifth generation) are confounded with weight. The article must present weight as the leading explanation, not the proven one.
- The model-year exposure adjustment assumes uniform sales and full-year exposure per model year; it is approximate, and 2018–2020 counts are small (6–7 deaths each). Treat the halving as directional.
- The lethality reading (older occupants, more passengers) is a plausible interpretation of 0.684 vs 0.593, not a finding — FARS in this file does not break out occupant age or passenger counts per model.
- The 2022 Avalon's TSP and 5-star ratings apply to the 2019–2022 fifth generation. Older Avalons carry older ratings and older structures; the FARS rate blends all generations.
- Model-year sums total 454 of 469 deaths; 15 deaths fall in model years the file records no deaths for.

## Strongest counterargument
The Avalon's advantage may be entirely demographic: Avalon buyers are older, wealthier, drive fewer miles at night, and take more highway trips — all of which lower fatality rates independent of the car. The impairment inversion cuts against a pure-demographics story (Avalon drivers test MORE impaired, not less), but impairment is only one demographic proxy, and age itself is unmeasured in this file. The honest statement is that the Avalon is safer per mile for whoever drives it, and the file cannot fully separate the car from its buyer. The used-car guidance stands either way: the same buyer, in an Avalon instead of a Camry, is in the safer car.

## Actionable insights
- Shopping a used full-size sedan: a 2019–2022 Avalon (TNGA-K, Toyota Safety Sense 2.5 standard, IIHS Top Safety Pick) kills at roughly half the annual rate of a 2000s Avalon and 34% slower per mile than the Camry. Budget $23,000–$36,000.
- Check the headlights: only Limited, Touring, and Hybrid Limited trims carry the Good-rated LED projectors; XLE/XSE trims carry Poor-rated LED reflectors, which is what kept the Avalon from Top Safety Pick+.
- If you own an older Avalon (2005–2008): it kills at roughly double the annual rate of the newer car. The upgrade that matters is the 2019+ redesign, not a newer Camry.
- Check any VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before buying; the Avalon's record is clean on recalls relative to its platform twin, but verify the specific car.
