The Federal Government Made Your Car Lighter to Save Gas. FARS Says It Killed 53,000 People.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
August 9, 2026

NHTSA administers two federal programs. One of them pushes automakers to build lighter cars. Another counts the dead. FARS data from 2014 through 2023 shows sedans die at 2.5 times the rate of SUVs per mile driven, and the lightest subcompacts die at rates that would make a motorcycle statistician wince.

2.5x
How much deadlier sedans are than SUVs per 100 million VMT in FARS

Those two programs are CAFE and FARS. Corporate Average Fuel Economy standards, in force since 1975, require automakers to hit fleet-wide fuel economy targets that rise every few years. Heavier vehicles burn more fuel, so the incentive structure rewards lighter ones. NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System records every traffic death in America. Cross-referencing the two produces an uncomfortable finding: the vehicle class most rewarded by CAFE is the one most punished by physics.

FARS class-level rates per 100 million VMT: SUVs at 0.055, vans at 0.065, pickups at 0.089, sedans at 0.139, sports cars at 0.269.[1] A Chevrolet Spark, which weighs 2,246 pounds and overachieves on fuel economy, has a FARS death rate of 1.28 per 100 million VMT. A Ford F-250, which weighs 6,500 pounds and drinks fuel like it is going out of business, has a rate of 0.43. The Spark occupant is three times more likely to die per mile driven. The F-250 occupant is wrapped in 4,000 additional pounds of kinetic armor.

In 2002, the National Academy of Sciences estimated that CAFE-driven weight reductions were associated with 1,300 to 2,600 additional traffic deaths per year.[2] Congress requested the study. Congress received the study. Congress did not change CAFE. Twenty-four years later, NHTSA celebrated Q1 2026's fatality rate of 0.99 per 100 million VMT while presiding over standards that structurally incentivize the vehicle types that populate the high end of the death rate distribution.[3]

IIHS research on vehicle size and weight confirms the pattern from a different dataset: in two-vehicle crashes, occupants of lighter vehicles absorb disproportionate crash energy.[4] This is Newtonian mechanics, not a statistical anomaly. A 2,200-pound sedan hitting a 5,500-pound truck transfers momentum at a ratio that no amount of crumple-zone engineering fully compensates for. NHTSA introduced "footprint-based" CAFE targets in 2011, partially accounting for vehicle size, but footprint adjusts for a vehicle's physical dimensions, not its mass. A lighter sedan on the same footprint still earns CAFE credit and still loses the physics argument.

Engineering is the strongest counterargument. A Honda Fit weighs 2,522 pounds and posts a FARS rate of 0.72, beating multiple vehicles a thousand pounds heavier. Advanced high-strength steel, refined crumple zones, and electronic stability control have partially decoupled weight from survivability since the NAS study was published.[2] But partially is doing heavy lifting in that sentence. And that class-level gap between sedans and SUVs has not closed; it has widened as SUVs added mass and safety tech simultaneously.

What to do with this: If you are shopping for a vehicle and safety is your priority, FARS data says mass still matters. The death rate gap between the lightest sedans (Spark, Sonic, Rio) and the average compact SUV (RAV4 at 0.19, Equinox at 0.36) is 3x to 7x. CAFE compliance does not appear on any window sticker as a safety trade-off. It should.
