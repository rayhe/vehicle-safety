# Research: Waymo's School Bus Fix Failed. NHTSA Just Said So in Writing.

**Slug:** 1461-waymo-school-bus-fix-failed-rq26006 | **Journalist:** Mia Crumplezone | **Kicker:** Investigation

## News peg

NHTSA opened recall query **RQ26006** on November 6, 2026, covering 2,895 Waymo vehicles running the 5th-generation automated driving system. The reason, quoted from the ODI resume: "Of the total reported incidents identified during PE25013, a majority of the incidents occurred after the November 17, 2025 remedy date." The fix was declared complete. The violations kept happening anyway. Reuters reported it November 9, 2026; TechCrunch the same day.

## Self-Critique Gate

**Proposal:** Waymo's school-bus software fix did not stop its robotaxis from passing stopped school buses, and NHTSA has now opened a formal recall query to ask why. The timeline arithmetic is the story: 354 days between "fix complete" and the regulator saying the fix may not have worked.

**Challenge:** Haven't we covered Waymo already? Yes, three times: the six-recalls piece (June 2026) mentioned the school-bus recall in one line and called it the "least well-documented of the six"; the freeway-problem piece and the 170-million-miles piece covered other failures. But RQ26006 is a NEW event (Nov 6, 2026) with a NEW angle: not the original defect, but the failure of the remedy itself. A recall query is the regulator's formal way of saying "your fix didn't fix it." Nobody on this site has covered a failed remedy before. The F-150 piece (#1460) covered a stalled remedy; this one covers a remedy that was declared done and then kept failing. Different failure mode, different story.

**Verdict:** Proceed.

## Verified facts (3+ primary sources)

### Source 1: NHTSA ODI Resume, Recall Query RQ26006 (primary)
- Opened November 6, 2026, by NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation.
- Population: 2,895 vehicles, Waymo 5th-generation automated driving system.
- Purpose: "to assess the adequacy of the remedy for Recall 25E084 and to identify the cause(s) of the conditions leading to the post-remedy reports."
- Key sentence: "Of the total reported incidents identified during PE25013, a majority of the incidents occurred after the November 17, 2025 remedy date."
- NHTSA closed the earlier preliminary evaluation (PE25013) the same day and opened the query instead.
- The resume lists 8 incident reports in its summary; crash and injury counts marked confidential. Waymo's own incident count is marked confidential. The resume does NOT publish a total incident count.
- A recall query is an investigation into a prior remedy, not a new recall or a finding of defect. It can close without further action.

### Source 2: TechCrunch, November 9, 2026 ("Waymo robotaxis kept illegally passing school buses after software fix, feds say")
- ODI opened its initial investigation in October 2025 after footage of a Waymo vehicle maneuvering around a stopped school bus in Atlanta, with the stop sign extended and lights flashing, while unloading children.
- Waymo said the software update was completed for all affected vehicles on November 17, 2025.
- NHTSA: "a majority of the incidents occurred after the software update."
- Waymo and Austin ISD had not responded to TechCrunch's comment request at publication time.

### Source 3: Reuters, November 9, 2026 (via secondary coverage)
- NHTSA opened the recall query November 6; confirmed Monday November 9.
- Covers "nearly 3,000" company-owned self-driving vehicles.
- Waymo says it improved bus behavior and has had no citations since March 2026.

### Source 4: NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts, DOT HS 813 819 (July 2026), FARS 2015-2024 — school-transportation-related crashes (primary, stakes data)
- 962 fatal school-transportation-related crashes, 1,069 people killed, 2015-2024: an average of 107 fatalities per year.
- 204 school-age children died; 77 were pedestrians, 78 occupants of other vehicles, 39 occupants of school transportation vehicles.
- 55% of school-age pedestrians killed were 5 to 10 years old.
- 162 pedestrians killed in total; 117 were struck by school transportation vehicles.
- 70% of all fatalities were occupants of other vehicles; only 11% were bus occupants. The danger is outside the bus, not inside it.

### Source 5: National Association of State Directors of Pupil Transportation Services (NASDPTS) 2025 survey
- 13th annual survey, released July 22, 2025: 114,239 drivers across 36 states and Washington, D.C. logged 67,258 illegal passes in a single day.

## Timeline (verified)
- **October 2025:** NHTSA opens preliminary evaluation PE25013 after Atlanta footage of a Waymo passing a stopped, unloading school bus.
- **November 5-17, 2025:** Waymo installs software update across the fleet; says complete November 17, 2025.
- **November 20, 2025:** Austin ISD has counted 19 illegal school bus passes by Waymo vehicles.
- **December 10, 2025:** Waymo files voluntary recall 25E084 (3,067 vehicles).
- **January 12, 2026:** NTSB documents an Austin incident: a Waymo vehicle initially stopped for a school bus, then proceeded while stop arms were still extended. Six vehicles passed the bus. No crash. NTSB opens its own school bus inquiry in 2026.
- **March 2026:** Waymo says it has received no citations for passing school buses since this month.
- **November 6, 2026:** NHTSA opens recall query RQ26006 (2,895 vehicles). 354 days after the fix was declared complete.
- **November 9, 2026:** Reuters and TechCrunch report the query publicly.

## Original contribution (novel analysis)
1. **The 354-day gap.** Waymo declared the remedy complete November 17, 2025. NHTSA opened its query November 6, 2026. That is 354 days, nearly a full year, during which the regulator's own count says most violations happened after the fix. The remedy was not a fix that failed fast; it was a fix that failed quietly for a year.
2. **The shrinking population.** Recall 25E084 covered 3,067 vehicles. The query covers 2,895. That is 172 fewer vehicles, about 5.6% of the recall population, no longer in scope. The filings do not say why. Fleet turnover is the likely explanation, but the regulator has not said so, and the difference means the query population is not the recall population.
3. **The paperwork order.** The recall (filed December 10, 2025) ratified a software push that Waymo said was already complete on November 17, 2025. The recall was paperwork after the fact. And the software push, per NHTSA's own count, did not stop the behavior. Two layers of assurance, both post-hoc, both now in question.
4. **The denominator problem.** NHTSA publishes 8 incident reports and says "a majority" came after the fix, but it does not publish the total number of encounters between Waymo vehicles and stopped school buses. Without a denominator, nobody can compute a failure rate. Eight reports could be eight failures out of ten encounters or eight out of ten thousand. The regulator's filing proves the fix did not eliminate the behavior; it cannot prove how often the behavior still occurs.

## What this does NOT prove (limitations)
- No child has been hurt in any of these incidents, per the filings. No crash is reported in the NHTSA resume summary.
- A recall query is not a finding of defect and not a new recall. It can close without further action.
- Waymo says it has had no citations since March 2026, which suggests the behavior may have stopped months before the query opened. The query looks backward at post-remedy reports; it does not establish the behavior is ongoing.
- The total incident count is confidential. "A majority" is the only proportion NHTSA published.
- The January 12 NTSB incident involved six vehicles passing one bus; whether those count as six incidents or one event in NHTSA's tally is not stated.

## Strongest counterargument
Waymo's defense is straightforward and partly supported by its own record: the company identified the problem, pushed a fleet-wide software update within weeks, filed a voluntary recall, and says it has received no citations since March 2026. By that account the system worked: defect found, fix deployed, behavior corrected, and the November 2026 query is a regulator doing due diligence on a closed chapter rather than catching a live failure. The counterargument has real weight on the citation point. But it does not answer NHTSA's central sentence: a majority of the incidents the agency identified happened after the fix was declared complete. A fix that works eventually is not the same as a fix that worked when declared. The query exists because the regulator, not the company, gets to decide when the chapter is closed.

## Actionable takeaways
- **Parents in Waymo cities (Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin, Atlanta):** the stop arm and flashing red lights are the law, and a robotaxi is not exempt from it. If a driverless car passes your child's bus, report it to the school district and to NHTSA at nhtsa.gov (file a complaint, reference recall query RQ26006).
- **School districts:** Austin ISD's 19 counted passes and 8 citations are why this query exists. Districts that log bus-pass violations create the paper trail regulators act on. If your district does not log them, the violations do not exist in the federal record.
- **Waymo riders:** nothing in the filings suggests a current, ongoing failure. But the episode is a reminder that a software recall is only as good as its verification, and verification here took 354 days and a second federal probe.
