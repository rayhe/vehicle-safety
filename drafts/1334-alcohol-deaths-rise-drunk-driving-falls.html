America Beat Drunk Driving. It Lost to Alcohol by a Factor of Fifteen.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 30, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, this country just recorded its third straight annual decline in drunk-driving deaths. NHTSA's fact sheet for 2024, published this June, puts alcohol-impaired crash fatalities at 11,904, down 3.9% from 12,382 in 2023.[1] Somewhere a press release is being framed. Meanwhile the CDC's ledger for the same molecule reads like a different sport: about 178,000 Americans a year die from excessive alcohol use, up roughly 29% from 2016 and 2017.[5] Fifteen dead on the slow ledger for every one dead on the fast one. Same ethanol. Different paperwork.

11,904
People killed in alcohol-impaired driving crashes in 2024 (NHTSA DOT HS 813 815); roughly one every 44 minutes

New York just gave us the cleanest decade-long split. A state-by-state analysis from Lantana Recovery, reported by the Staten Island Advance this month, found the state's alcohol-impaired road death rate fell 3.12% between 2015 and 2024, from 1.55 to 1.50 per 100,000 residents, the 15th-largest decline in the country.[2] Over the identical ten years, New York's age-adjusted rate of alcohol-induced deaths, the deaths where alcohol is the underlying cause on the certificate, rose 18.46%, from 6.5 to 7.7 per 100,000. That ranked the state 43rd for improvement. Same state. Same decade. Same molecule. Fifteenth place on the road, forty-third in the hospital.

~178,000
Annual US deaths from excessive alcohol use (CDC, 2020-2021), about twenty every hour, up 29% from 2016-2017

It is tempting to call this a paradox, and it is not one. Drunk driving is the rare alcohol harm that comes with a victim, a location, an investigating officer, and a statute. That makes it governable. We spent decades assembling the machinery: the 0.08 standard, administrative license revocation, checkpoints, ignition interlocks, the designated-driver social contract. And the machinery visibly works. In 1982, 48% of traffic deaths involved alcohol-impaired crashes; in 2024 it was 30%.[1] Thirty states plus DC recorded declines in 2024 alone; only nineteen saw increases.[3] Put a number you can test in a field sobriety exam and society will grind it down over forty years. That part of the story is a legitimate public health victory. This desk acknowledges victories. We just also keep score.

Here is the score. Of the CDC's 178,000, about 117,000 are chronic conditions built up over years: liver disease, several cancers, heart disease, alcohol use disorder itself. About 61,000 are acute: crashes, poisonings, alcohol-involved overdoses, suicide.[5] The road deaths we celebrate declining are the most governable slice of the smallest slice. And the chronic ledger is where the gender gap is opening fastest. Deaths from excessive alcohol use rose 35% among girls and women between the CDC's two measurement periods, against 27% among boys and men, with breast and colorectal cancer among the leading causes.[5] Breast and colorectal cancer. Show me the ignition interlock for that.

The access data explain why the chronic curve refuses to bend. A JAMA Network Open study from January, covering 3,153 counties over roughly 22,000 county-years, found that the share of counties with at least one treatment facility offering the three FDA-approved medications for alcohol use disorder rose from 34% in 2017 to 44% in 2021, and then stopped. Through 2023 the line stayed flat.[4] The drugs are old, naltrexone has been oral-approved since 1994, any clinician with a DEA number can prescribe it. The bottleneck is geography. The facilities cluster in metro counties with large populations and low uninsured shares; rural counties, small counties, and poor counties were significantly less likely to have any facility offering the medications at all.[4] New York's alcohol-induced death rate is a statewide average, and averages of that kind hide their own geography: an hour to the nearest naltrexone prescriber in the North Country, a corner store at the end of every block.

Even the overdose success story, which is real, absorbs the alcohol numbers into someone else's ledger. New York's overdose deaths fell for a third consecutive year, down 44.9% since 2022, with opioid-related deaths down 51.9%, and the Governor's office rightly took the win.[2] What the announcement did not mention is that the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism estimates alcohol played a role in about 16% of 2022 opioid overdose deaths. When a person dies with fentanyl and alcohol both in the system, the death is counted as an overdose, investigated as an overdose, and reported as progress when the count drops. The drinking that made the overdose more likely gets filed in a different drawer.

Note the instrumentation asymmetry, because this is the part that should bother you. New York publishes a real-time overdose death dashboard. It publishes no equivalent for alcohol mortality. Overdose fatalities arrive through medical examiners and get counted within weeks, which is what makes them available for press releases and budget cycles. Alcohol mortality arrives through vital statistics, which move at the speed of death-certificate completion and annual tabulation.[2] The thing a state can count quickly is the thing a state can respond to quickly. We built the breathalyzer, the field test, the FARS database, the real-time dashboard, and an entire federal agency around the alcohol death that produces a defendant. The alcohol death that produces a hepatology chart got death certificates and silence.

None of this diminishes the 11,904 number's decline. Fewer people dying at the hands of a drunk driver is good news, full stop, and the policy stack that produced it is one of the genuine infrastructure successes of the last forty years. But notice what the success proves: when alcohol harm is observable, testable, and attached to a statute, society moves. Naltrexone has been prescribable for decades. The decision to treat alcohol as the epidemic it is, at the speed of the dashboards we built for everything else, has not yet been made. One person dies in an alcohol-impaired crash every 44 minutes. Twenty die of alcohol, period, every hour. We celebrated the first number because we built the machine to see it. The second number is larger, uglier, and mostly unseen, and it is growing.
