Toyota Recalled 105,794 Land Cruisers Over a Fuel Connector That Can Come Apart. The Fix Is a Clamp.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 24, 2027

The Land Cruiser is the SUV you buy so you never have to think about the vehicle. Toyota just recalled 105,794 of them because a fuel tube connector near the gas tank can lift off and disconnect, letting fuel leak in a way that can start a fire.[1] Those figures are official. The smirk is mine.

105,794
2024-2027 Land Cruiser Hybrids recalled: every model year of the returned US nameplate

Here is the mechanism, because it is almost elegant in its simplicity. A resin fuel tube connects to a metal fuel tube near the fuel tank. The connector linking them can, in Toyota's phrasing, "lift and disconnect." Fuel escapes. And here is the wrinkle the hybrid system adds: the fuel keeps leaking until the engine in the hybrid system shuts off. One connector, failing at one joint, produces a stall and a fire risk simultaneously. A single part gets you coming and going.

Now the timeline, which is where it gets properly interesting. Toyota started receiving field reports of fuel leaks in mid-July 2024. Investigators tore into more than 600 vehicles, running vibration and pressure tests. By early October of this year, the internal tally stood at 23 Toyota Field Technical Reports and 64 warranty claims in the United States.[2] The recall came on October 14, 2026. That is roughly 27 months from first report to recall, or about one signal every nine days, processed through a two-year engineering investigation before Toyota decided the connector had to go.

39.7%
Share of the recall that is model year 2025: 41,955 of 105,794 vehicles

The 2025 model year carries the bulk of it. Of the four affected years, 2025 accounts for 41,955 vehicles, nearly two in every five recalled, followed by 25,041 for 2024, 21,274 for 2026, and 17,524 for 2027.[2] There is a historical rhyme here worth noting: the last Land Cruiser fuel-system recall of any scale was in 1991, when roughly 38,000 trucks were recalled for leaking fuel tanks.[3] This one is about 2.8 times that size, and it covers the entire modern fleet. Every Land Cruiser sold in America since the nameplate returned in spring 2024 is on the list.

And the remedy? A clamp. Dealers inspect the connection and install a supplemental clamp so the connector cannot pull apart, replacing the resin fuel tube where the inspection calls for it.[1] Free of charge, which is decent of them. There is something beautifully honest about a $55,000 expedition vehicle being fixed with the same class of hardware that holds your washing machine hose to the wall. The legend says nothing ever breaks, though the parts bin disagrees.

What you should actually do: If you own a 2024 through 2027 Land Cruiser Hybrid, check your VIN at Toyota.com/recall or nhtsa.gov/recalls right now; do not wait for the letter. Toyota says owner notifications go out by mid-December 2026, and the federal Part 573 report had not even been posted in NHTSA's public database as of mid-October.[4] The dealer visit is free, the defect is a fire hazard, and there is no prize for being the last person on your block to get the clamp.

Limitations

Several key facts are still missing from the public record. Federal Part 573 filings normally specify the defect rate estimate, the affected production date range, and the root cause; this one had not appeared in NHTSA's database as of October 15, so the actual failure rate across the 105,794 vehicles is unknown. Toyota declined to say whether any fires or injuries have occurred. Nobody has named the resin tube's supplier, and whether the connector lift-off involves thermal cycling, vibration, or a tooling tolerance issue remains undisclosed. Until the Part 573 filing is public, the full causal story is Toyota's word against a connector.

Strongest counterargument

The 27-month investigation reads as diligence, not delay. Toyota physically inspected more than 600 vehicles and ran vibration and pressure tests before issuing the recall, which is the opposite of the rush-to-recall pattern that produces defective remedies. The recall landed before any publicly documented fire, suggesting the internal threshold worked as designed. And a clamp is a legitimate engineering fix for a connector walk-off problem; simplicity of remedy says nothing about severity of defect. The system found a fuel leak, studied it for two years, and recalled a hundred thousand trucks before the worst happened. That is, in fairness, the way this is supposed to work.
