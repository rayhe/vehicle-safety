The .15 Club: 68% of Drunk-Driving Deaths Involve Drivers at Nearly Twice the Legal Limit

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 18, 2026

According to the toxicology reports, and there are 13,500 of them, the drunk driver who kills someone on an American road is not a guy who had two beers with dinner and guessed wrong. In 2024, 68% of alcohol-impaired-driving fatalities involved a driver with a blood alcohol concentration of .15 or higher, nearly double the .08 legal limit.[1] That is not the margin of error. That is the whole sport.

68%
Share of 2024 alcohol-impaired fatalities involving a driver at BAC .15 or higher

Run the numbers the way NHTSA did. Of the 13,500 drivers who had any alcohol in their systems in fatal crashes last year, 84% were at or above .08, and 56%, a full 7,547 people, were at or above .15.[1] The crash-risk curve explains why the distribution clusters there: a driver at .08 is about 4 times more likely to crash than a sober driver, while a driver at .15 is at least 12 times more likely.[6] Now look at the 11,904 dead: 8,097 of them died in crashes where at least one driver blew .15 or worse. The remainder, every alcohol death involving a driver under .15, totals 3,807. Do the division. The .15 club outnumbers the rest more than two to one.

Holiday weekends make it uglier. During the 2024 Labor Day window, 6 p.m. August 30 through 5:59 a.m. September 3, 505 people died in traffic crashes, 170 of them, 34%, in collisions involving at least one alcohol-impaired driver.[3] Of the alcohol-impaired drivers who died that weekend, 77% had BACs of .15 or higher. Among drivers aged 18 to 34 who died, 40% had been drinking at or above the legal threshold.[3] The National Safety Council puts the average Labor Day death toll 10% above comparable non-holiday weekends, and counted 39,254 crash deaths in all of 2024, more than 107 a day, with the 25 deadliest days clustered on summer and fall holiday weekends.[3]

These are not first-timers. Alcohol-impaired drivers in fatal crashes were three times as likely to have a prior DWI conviction on their five-year record as drivers with no alcohol, 6% versus 2%.[1] The .15 club is a membership with dues paid in advance. And here is the part the enforcement campaigns keep tripping over: in June, this desk reported that 2,028 people died in 2024 in crashes where a driver's BAC sat between .01 and .07, below the limit in 49 states.[5] That number is real and it is policy-relevant. But the .15 club is roughly four times its size. Utah's drop to a .05 limit in 2018 cut fatal crashes 19.8% by shifting the entire distribution, not by aiming at the hardcore.[5] Both tails matter. The volume lives far above the line, while the cheap policy lever sits below it.

So what do you do with a crisis concentrated in people who blow nearly twice the limit? Start with the countermeasure that actually bites: ignition interlocks. NHTSA strongly supports expanding interlocks as proven technology that keeps drunk drivers from getting behind the wheel, yet only a minority of states require them for first offenders.[6] A .15 reading is not a judgment call, it is a habit documented in blood. If you host a gathering, NHTSA's guidance is blunt: do not let a drinking guest drive, make sure every guest has a sober ride home before the first pour, and call 911 with the vehicle description, plate, road, and direction if you see one of these drivers on the road.[3] A DUI costs the average driver about $10,000 in fees, fines, and lost work. That figure is doing a lot of heavy lifting in the campaign ads, because the safety argument alone apparently was not enough.[3]

What this did not prove: FARS captures fatal crashes only, so high-BAC drivers may be overrepresented relative to how often they are actually on the road. NHTSA imputes BACs when test results are missing, which means the 56% figure above .15 includes estimated values. The 77% Labor Day figure is a holiday window, not the year, and almost certainly overstates the annual average of 68%. Comparing Utah's fatal-crash decline to national policy is suggestive, not causal: states differ in enforcement, demographics, and weather in ways a single before-and-after cannot isolate.
