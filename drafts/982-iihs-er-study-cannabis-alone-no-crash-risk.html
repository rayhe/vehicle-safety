IIHS Paid for a Study. It Says Weed Doesn't Crash Cars. Alcohol Does.

Dale Impactor III • Toxicology Desk
September 24, 2026

Read the funder line first. Nine researchers from Oregon Health & Science University, the University of Colorado, UC Davis, and Portland State drew blood and ran breathalyzers on drivers at emergency departments in Denver, Portland, and Sacramento. That funding came from the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety. IIHS funded a peer-reviewed study that found cannabis alone does not raise your odds of crashing a car. Against a sober baseline, the odds ratio was 0.47, with a confidence interval of 0.21 to 1.03: not statistically significant. In plain English: indistinguishable from zero effect, with the point estimate pointing the wrong way from what you'd expect, which should embarrass every per se THC statute still on the books.

4.6× – 5.2×
Crash odds with alcohol alone, vs. 1.0 (no effect) with cannabis alone

Alcohol is the one that survives every adjustment. Alcohol alone multiplied crash odds by 4.57 in one model and 5.15 in the other, both with p-values under 0.001. Combined use of alcohol and cannabis landed at 2.58 to 3.30. So the dose-response curve runs backward from what the culture war assumed: the thing that smells like a felony is inert, the thing sold in supermarkets is a fivefold multiplier, and mixing them is the actual trap. Among infrequent cannabis users who combined with alcohol, the odds ratio hit 8.11.

Here is the part that should make every policymaker reach for antacids. In a published journal, the authors wrote that "high self-reported acute cannabis use was associated with lower odds" of a crash — read that twice: more weed, fewer crashes, dose-response inverted. Stoned drivers self-throttle, and the mechanism is boring: they drive slower, leave bigger gaps, and decline to overtake. Booze does the opposite, dismantling judgment and confidence calibration at the same time.

Toxicology has known this forever, but the paper adds the mechanism that kills per se THC limits. A prior simulator study the authors cite found daily cannabis users carried five times the blood THC of occasional users, yet only the occasional users showed measurable driving-skill decrement. Tolerance decouples the blood number from the impairment. A 2019 study put it directly: drivers at the legal THC limit of two to five nanograms per milliliter were statistically no more likely to crash than sober drivers. On nanogram cutoffs, the authors' verdict was "complex from a scientific and legal perspective," which is journal-speak for "these numbers are fiction." Congressional Research Service reached the same conclusion in 2019. Meanwhile a House appropriations report is still demanding an "objective standard to measure marijuana impairment" for roadside use. THC lingers in blood for days in regular users; it cannot timestamp last use. You cannot build a breathalyzer for a molecule that tells you nothing about now, and the chemistry will not provide one.

Which brings up the genuinely strange part: IIHS also funds the ecological work showing legalization raised injury crash rates 5.8% and fatal crash rates 4.1% across legal states. Both studies can be true, and they reconcile through exactly one channel: the combination. If cannabis alone carries an odds ratio near 1.0, then the legalization-linked crash bump has to ride on something else, and the paper hands you the suspect with its own data — legalization does not make cannabis more dangerous, it makes the drink-then-smoke combo more common. Fourteen percent of the confirmed marijuana-involved crash drivers had a child passenger, per IIHS's summaries. Risk migrates from the driver's skill to the passenger's seat. Sit with that.

Fairness demands the limits up front, and this paper has real ones. Only drivers who consented were enrolled, which means drivers who just crashed while at fault — the people with the most reason to refuse a blood draw — are missing from the numerator, and self-reported use is almost certainly understated. Three urban ERs in three cannabis-legal states make up the sample; rural drivers and prohibition states are absent. And the case-crossover design compares each driver's crash day to their own non-crash days, which controls for the person but not the calendar: Friday night versus Tuesday morning sneaks risk into the comparison that the substance didn't cause. That 8.11 combined-use odds ratio for infrequent users carries a confidence interval of 1.64 to 40.02, which is a scream of a point estimate sitting on a whisper of data. Small sample. Big confidence interval. Take the direction, not the digit.

What survives all of that: presence is not impairment, the blood number is not the danger, and the combo is. NHTSA's own trauma-center study found 54% of seriously injured drivers had drugs or alcohol in their system and nearly 20% had two or more substances, with polysubstance magnifying each drug's effect. In 2024, alcohol-impaired driving killed 10,832 people, thirty-three a day, roughly thirty percent of all traffic deaths. Cannabis alone does not do this; cannabis plus alcohol might. And the legal line drawn in nanograms per milliliter measures chemistry, not risk. This desk has said it for a decade. Now it's in print, paid for by the insurance industry, which had every reason to find the opposite.

What this means for you: if you use cannabis and drink, the combination is the multiplier, eightfold for infrequent users in this study. Never calibrate safety to a legal THC nanogram limit; that number cannot tell you whether you are impaired. If you are behind the wheel after mixing, you are the datapoint the study is warning about. And check your passengers: the study's cruelest detail is that the people most exposed were children who made no choice at all.
