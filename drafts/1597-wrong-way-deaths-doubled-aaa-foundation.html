Body Count

Wrong-Way Driving Nearly Doubled in a Decade. The Odds Say the Driver Is Drunk, Old, Alone, and Lost.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

Somebody is coming at you in your lane, headlights first, at highway speed, and you have about four seconds to figure out what to do about it. That somebody is more common than they used to be. Fatal wrong-way crashes on divided highways rose from 278 in 2014 to 520 in 2023, and the deaths in them from 373 to 699, nearly double in a decade.[1] Over the same ten years, total American traffic deaths rose 25%. Wrong-way deaths rose 87%. Whatever else got safer, the wrong way got worse, and it got worse faster than everything else.

Higher odds of being the wrong-way driver with a BAC above .12, versus a sober driver. Six in ten wrong-way drivers were past .12.

Credit the AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety with the accounting, because this is their count, not mine. Their research brief, published in March 2026 and built from NHTSA’s Fatality Analysis Reporting System, is the first national update since their 2021 study, and it counted 4,164 fatal wrong-way crashes on divided highways between 2014 and 2023, killing 5,730 people.[1] Divided highways only, Interstates and freeways and their ramps, so the real national wrong-way toll is higher than this number. What the brief adds beyond the body count is a profile, built the hard way: the researchers compared every wrong-way driver against two different groups of right-way drivers in fatal crashes and calculated the odds of being the one going the wrong direction.[1]

Alcohol is not one factor among many. It is the factor. Nearly 70% of wrong-way drivers had a blood alcohol concentration above .08, the legal limit in every state except Utah. A driver at .08 to .12 had roughly 19 times the odds of being the wrong-way driver as a sober one. Past .12, the odds jump to 83 times.[1] Six in ten wrong-way drivers were past .12, which is not tipsy, not buzzed, not “had a couple,” it is the kind of drunk where the road signs stop making sense and the entrance ramp looks exactly like an exit ramp. Only one in five wrong-way drivers had a BAC of zero.

Age runs second, and it runs in a direction that should embarrass everyone who assumed the problem was young men. Drivers between 20 and 39 were more than half of all wrong-way drivers, and their odds were nothing special. Drivers over 80 were 6.5% of wrong-way drivers, and their odds of being the wrong-way driver were 20 times higher than drivers in their fifties.[1] Seventy-somethings ran about six times the odds. Sixty-somethings about twice. Youth supplies the volume. Old age supplies the rate, and the rate is not close.

After alcohol and age, the profile fills in like a police sketch. Eighty-six percent of wrong-way drivers were alone in the car, and having even one passenger cut the odds of being the wrong-way driver by roughly two-thirds.[1] Four out of five of these crashes happened in darkness, with odds more than doubled at night and doubled again at dawn and dusk. Rural divided highways carried 1.8 times the odds of urban ones. Fourteen percent of wrong-way drivers were driving on expired, revoked, or suspended licenses, and another 8% had no license at all. Nearly half the cars were more than ten years old; past twenty years, the odds doubled. Drivers more than 250 miles from home ran 1.8 times the odds in one of the two models the researchers ran, though not the other, so file that one under suggestive rather than settled.[1] Stolen cars were a rounding error in count, 0.6% of wrong-way drivers, and a horror show in odds, 36 to 70 times higher, with a confidence interval wide enough to drive a truck through, which is fitting, because trucks are the other half of this story.

Because here is the part nobody says out loud about a wrong-way crash: the person who dies is usually not the person who caused it. Large trucks and buses were 1.5% of wrong-way drivers and roughly a fifth of the right-way drivers in these crashes.[1] Read that again. In a wrong-way crash, the truck coming the right way, driven by a professional, sober, licensed, doing everything right, is the one that gets hit head-on at combined highway speed. Wrong-way driving is not a private mistake. It is a mistake that kills strangers, and the strangers are disproportionately the people paid to drive for a living.

Geography concentrates the toll the way it concentrates everything in this country. Texas alone accounted for 894 of the 5,730 deaths, 15.6% of the national total from one state.[1] California added 532, Florida 406, and the three together make up nearly a third of every wrong-way death in America. In share terms, the leaders are smaller states with long dark highways: Washington, where 9.2% of divided-highway deaths were wrong-way, and South Dakota at 8.9%.[1]

Fourteen years ago, the National Transportation Safety Board investigated wrong-way driving and named the same two risks at the top of its list: alcohol impairment and older drivers.[4] Fourteen years later, the same two risks top the new list, the annual toll has nearly doubled, and the countermeasures the brief recommends are mostly the ones that were recommended then: ignition interlocks, in-vehicle detection, bigger and lower signs, blank-out signs that light up when a wrong-way car is detected, directional rumble strips, and detection networks that alert traffic centers and highway patrol in real time.[1] Fourteen states run those integrated detection systems now, and several report that more drivers self-correct when the system catches them early.[1] Massachusetts, which logged more than a thousand wrong-way events on its detection trailers in two years, passed a law in July 2026 requiring a statewide wrong-way strategy, because its data showed most drivers who enter the wrong way turn around if something tells them in time.[6]

Tonight is Thanksgiving night, which is worth saying plainly, because the profile in this study is the Thanksgiving night profile: drunk, dark, alone, far from home, on a divided highway. Nothing in the brief breaks out Thanksgiving specifically, so do not take this as a holiday statistic. Take it as a calendar coincidence with teeth. If you are driving tonight, the cheapest safety device in the dataset is a passenger, and the most expensive decision is the drink. If you see headlights where headlights should not be, slow down, move right, and call 911, because the wrong-way driver is hugging what they think is their right, which is your left. If you realize you are the wrong-way driver, pull to the shoulder, stop, hazards on, call 911, and do not try to whip a U-turn across highway lanes at speed. And if someone in your family is over 80 and still driving divided highways at night in unfamiliar places, that is the specific trip to take off their calendar first, not the driving itself, just that trip, because the odds ratio at 80-plus is twenty, and twenty is not a rounding error.[1]

Fairness requires the other side, and it is not small. Wrong-way deaths are about 1.7% of the annual American traffic toll, 699 deaths in 2023 against 40,901 total deaths the same year.[2] Speeding kills roughly 11,000 people a year. Alcohol impairment kills roughly 12,000. Every dollar spent on wrong-way detection trailers and blank-out signs is a dollar not spent on interlocks, speed management, or pedestrian infrastructure that would save more lives per dollar. Doubling is real, but it starts from a small base, 373 deaths, and the profile is a composite: almost no real driver carries every risk factor at once, so the 83-times and 20-times odds ratios describe extreme slices, not the typical case. What this study does not prove matters too. It covers divided highways only, so 5,730 is a floor. It did not examine drug impairment at all, for data-quality reasons the authors state plainly. Some BAC values were imputed by a model, and the confidence intervals at the extremes are wide enough to be honest about. And the far-from-home effect only cleared significance in one of the two models. All of that is true. It is also true that 5,730 people died in ten years because someone drove the wrong way down a road built to keep them from doing exactly that, and the number went up while the country, on the whole, got safer. A problem can be small and getting worse at the same time. This one is both.
