## Scores

### 1. General Editor — 9.0/10
Lede lands the paradox in two sentences and the record-month hook is genuinely fresh news from a release dated October 12. Structure is clean: record, decomposition, rate inversion, fatalities, counterargument, action. Deduction: 865 words of prose runs long against the 300-500 word guide, though it matches the last several shipped articles (1439: 820 words), so this is house style drift, not a defect in this draft alone. The two fatal-case narratives are tight and do not pad.

### 2. Voice Coach — 8.9/10
Axle's voice is unmistakable: "I ran the numbers on the full release" and the parking-lot-log line could not be swapped onto Rex or Mia without sounding wrong. Rhythm variance 1141.1 is the strongest in recent memory; sentence lengths swing from fragments to 60-word builds. No banned phrases, no AI tells. Deduction: "Give the finding its proper discount before believing it" is a house formula that appears in nearly every recent article; it works, but it is starting to read as a template seam rather than a voice choice.

### 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.2/10
Fairness is the article's spine. The counterargument is stated at full strength (hard braking creates conflicts, geofenced fair-weather miles, Waymo's own characterization of its own crashes). Both fatal cases are described factually with no names, no gore, and the explicit flag that these are company accounts, not findings of fault. No victim-blaming: the motorcyclist and the pedestrian are described as struck by other vehicles, which is what the reports say. The piece refuses to let the reader conclude "robots are perfect" and refuses to let them conclude "robots are dangerous" either. That restraint is the ethics.

### 4. Social/Shareability — 8.8/10
Headline is a share in itself: "Half the Cars in It Were Parked" is the kind of line that survives being screenshotted without context. Pull stat 50.4% is the single most quotable number in the file. The 1.3% at 45+ mph and the 3-deaths-in-five-years figures are second and third hooks. Deduction: the rate-inversion paragraph (57 to under 6 per million miles) is the strongest argument in the piece but is buried in the third paragraph; a social reader who stops after the pull stat still gets the point, which is fine, but the per-mile number deserves earlier placement.

### 5. Legal Accuracy — 8.8/10
SGO description is accurate (third amendment, June 16, 2025, property-damage threshold, duplicate elimination, five-day vs monthly reporting). "Company accounts, not findings of fault" is stated twice and the disclaimer repeats it. No legal claims are overreached; no fault is assigned anywhere. Deduction: the IIHS 68%/71% finding is cited through Political.org (ref-3) rather than to IIHS directly. The underlying IIHS study is real and the numbers are as reported, but a direct IIHS link would be stronger. Acceptable for a secondary citation, noted for the record.

### 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Original contribution is real: the stopped/parked decomposition (50.4% / 51.0% at 0 mph / 75.8% under 10 mph / 1.3% at 45+) is a cut of the September 2026 release nobody on this site has published, and the count-vs-rate inversion is quantified (LA count up 3.2x while the rate fell 8.5x). Methodology is transparent: every percentage carries its denominator. Limitations are explicit, including the 144-vs-145 March-count wobble between sources, the June 2025 rule-change contamination, and the Waymo-is-82.8%-of-the-file caveat. The "highest monthly total since July 2021" claim is sourced to ConsumerShield's full-series analysis, not to the Reyes window alone. Deduction: the speed-ladder data would be stronger presented as a small table rather than embedded in a 60-word sentence, but the numbers are all checkable as written.

### 7. Data Presentation — 8.9/10
Pull stat carries the thesis. Percentages are paired with raw counts throughout (775 of 1,538; 20 of 1,538; 29 of 1,538), which is exactly how a data piece should behave. The monthly series and quarterly counts give the reader the trend, not just the peak. Deduction: no visual beyond the hero; a compact speed-distribution table would have made the parking-lot-log paragraph scannable. The prose compensates, but a table would have been better.
