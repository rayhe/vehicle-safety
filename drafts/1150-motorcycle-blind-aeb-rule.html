By The Numbers

The Federal Fix for Work-Truck Crashes Covers 2.5% of the Deaths

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 4, 2027

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. On October 8, IIHS published its first-ever crash-avoidance ratings for commercial vehicles, eleven vans and Class 3 pickups tested against cars, semitrailers, pedestrians, and a motorcycle target that wrecked nearly every scorecard in the room.[1] Crashes involving medium- or heavy-duty vehicles or light vans killed 6,215 people in 2024, a full 16% of all U.S. roadway deaths, and that is the denominator. Every proposed fix deserves to be measured against it.

2.5%
Share of the 6,215 annual heavy-vehicle-involved deaths that NHTSA's proposed heavy-truck AEB rule would save (155 lives)[2]

The headline number inside NHTSA's proposal, first floated in 2023 and still not finalized three years later, is 155 lives saved per year. Do the division: 155 / 6,215 = 2.495%. Also in the proposal: 19,118 of the roughly 60,000 annual rear-end crashes in which a heavy vehicle is the striking vehicle would be prevented, 31.9% of that specific crash mode, with about $2 billion a year shaved off the damage bill.[2] Both numbers are real. One of them tells you how much of the problem the rule actually touches.

Which brings us to the scorecards, where not one of the four Class 3 pickups, machines weighing up to 14,000 pounds, earned better than a Marginal vehicle-to-vehicle rating; the Silverado 3500HD, Sierra 3500, and F-350 all rated Poor. Ford's F-350 only avoided the car target at 31 mph; in one 43-mph run it didn't reduce speed at all.[3] Meanwhile the two vans that aced pedestrian avoidance were both electrics, the Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 and the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400, and GM already discontinued the BrightDrop.[4] So the best pedestrian-avoidance van in the commercial segment is a vehicle you cannot buy anymore. Funny.

But the finding that kept me up all night was the motorcycle target. "Several systems did not issue warnings or slow at all in most of the motorcycle trials," IIHS said. Consider the Rivian: it avoided the car target in four of six runs, issued timely warnings in every car run and all three semitrailer runs, then recorded zero speed reduction and zero timely warnings in all six motorcycle runs.[5] Perfect vision for a sedan. Perfect vision for a sedan, total blindness for a motorcycle. A sensor suite that sees a pedestrian perfectly and a motorcycle not at all tells you exactly which road users were in the training data.

Now the strongest case against my headline, stated properly: judging a rear-end-crash rule by the entire heavy-vehicle death toll is like judging a flu shot by all-cause mortality. Still, the rule was built for striking rear-end crashes, and 31.9% of those is a genuinely meaningful slice of preventable carnage. Everything else in that 97.5% includes crashes no brake system could fix: a light vehicle running a red into a truck's flank, a single-vehicle run-off-road at midnight, an oncoming-lane crossover. And IIHS president David Harkey's own point cuts both ways: at least one vehicle earned a Good in each test, so the technology is achievable, not theoretical.[1] Both of those are fair, but neither changes the denominator.

So what do you do with all of this? If you ride a motorcycle or drive a small car: assume the 12,000-pound box truck beside you has driver assistance tuned for sedans and semitrailers, not for you. Defensive lane positioning near commercial vehicles is not paranoia; it is calibrated to the test data. If you buy fleet vehicles: the Sprinter 2500 was the only vehicle rated Good for vehicle-to-vehicle prevention, and for pedestrian-dense routes the electric vans lead. And if your fleet still runs Chevrolet Express or GMC Savana 2500s, know that they ship without automatic emergency braking at all, forward collision warning is optional, and both earned Poor in both IIHS tests.[1] Demand the checkbox.

What this doesn't prove: IIHS's ratings measure controlled test scenarios at 31, 37, and 43 mph, not real-world crash outcomes, and results are trim-specific (the F-350 tested was a DRW Lariat). The 155-lives estimate is NHTSA's modeled projection at full fleet penetration, which takes decades. The 6,215 figure and the 155 estimate use different vehicle-class denominators, so 2.5% is a cross-tabulation of two public numbers, not an agency statistic. The Express and Savana are legacy platforms; their successors may differ.
