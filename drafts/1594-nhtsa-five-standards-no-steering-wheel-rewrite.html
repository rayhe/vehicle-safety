Existential Dread

NHTSA Is Rewriting Five Safety Standards for Cars With No Drivers. It Still Hasn’t Written the One for Driving.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

Federal safety standards were written for a person sitting behind a steering wheel. Many of them say “driver” outright. Others assume someone who can see through the windshield, read the gear indicator, reach the hazard switch, or press a brake pedal. A car with no steering wheel and no pedals runs into those assumptions in dozens of places, so NHTSA spent 2026 deleting them, one rule at a time. Five proposals, six standards, and not one of them is a final rule yet.[1]

160
Total public comments filed across all five proposals to rewrite the federal safety rulebook for driverless cars, per regulations.gov counts checked November 26, 2026.

Start with the small ones, because the small ones are where the absurdity lives. FMVSS 102 governs the gear display, those four letters P R N D, and the March proposal would exempt driverless cars from having one at all, on the grounds that a display “designed to aid a person driving the vehicle” serves no purpose when no person is driving, and eleven people commented.[1] FMVSS 103 and 104 cover windshield defrosters, wipers, and washers, and the same day’s proposal would exempt driverless cars from both standards entirely, because both exist, in NHTSA’s words, to give “the driver visibility through the vehicle windshield,” and the proposal drew fourteen comments. Nobody asked how the robot keeps its own cameras clear, because the proposal covers windshields, not sensors.[2]

FMVSS 110 covers the tire placard, that sticker on the driver’s door jamb listing pressures and load limits, and the April proposal would let a car with no driver’s side put the sticker on the left side instead, and the entire public response was eight comments. For scale, the Honda Auto Start/Stop probe NHTSA opened on November 13 drew 701 complaints, more than four times the combined comment total of all five rewrites.[10]

Then the stakes rise. FMVSS 135, the brake standard, would apply its foot-operated service brake and hand-or-foot parking brake requirements only to vehicles with manual controls. Stopping distances do not change, and a car with no pedals still has to stop as well as any other car. But read the footnote. NHTSA writes that it “is taking no position at this time as to how a passenger should be able to direct an ADS-operated vehicle to stop, or how the ADS should respond to such direction.” Sixty-one comments, and not one of them can buy you a stop button, because no rule requires one. There is no federal requirement for a passenger to be able to stop the car they are riding in, or to reach a remote operator, or to get out in an emergency.[4]

FMVSS 108, the lighting standard, is the newest and strangest of the five. Published October 26, it would drop a headlamp test voltage, delete rules about how headlamps stack vertically, and limit the requirements for manually operated turn signals, hazard flashers, and beam switching to cars that have manual controls. It would also strike the word “driver” from the definition of the hazard warning signal operating unit, which today defines that unit as “a driver-controlled device” actuated “by a single driver action.” Sixty-six comments, and the comment period now runs to December 10 after a November 16 extension, though secondary coverage published before the extension still cites the original November 25 deadline.[5]

One thing in the lighting proposal is an addition rather than a deletion, and it deserves a closer look. NHTSA would allow optional cyan ADS marker lamps, steady-burning, blue-green, mounted at least 15 inches off the ground, never combined with another lamp, so other road users can tell a car is driving itself. Basis is SAE J3134, and the agency cites a survey of 4,400 drivers in which over 80 percent said they wanted a marker lamp on automated vehicles. Mercedes-Benz ran the on-road validation: five cars, two years in California, turquoise lights approved in California and Nevada in December 2023. Five cars is the entire evidence base for a national placement standard covering passenger cars, trucks, and buses. And the lamps are optional, so a driverless car that omits them is fully compliant, which means the signal teaches the public a rule with built-in exceptions: cyan means robot, but no cyan means nothing at all.[5]

Meanwhile, two companies are already running cars with no controls on public roads under two different legal theories, and the contrast is the real story of 2026. Zoox asked permission in public. NHTSA granted a temporary exemption on July 31, good through July 31, 2028, capped at 2,500 vehicles in any 12-month period, covering portions of eight standards, under operating conditions the agency can change at will. Tesla took the other path. It certified the Cybercab itself, started charging for rides in Austin on September 3, and NHTSA opened Audit Query AQ26002 the same day to examine how far that certification rests on Tesla deciding certain standards simply do not apply. Tesla’s sworn answers arrived October 30 with a request to keep all of them secret, and the public docket now holds a one-page letter whose only text is “The entirety of this file is confidential business information.”[11] One company waited eleven months for a leash while the other waited zero days and sealed the homework.[6][11]

Count the gaps, because the gaps are the article: none of the five rewrites is final, so the current text of all six standards is still the law. No federal standard governs how well the automated system drives, and NHTSA has only said the work is under way. Nothing requires a passenger stop control. Cyan lamps are optional. Four standards Zoox needed exemptions from, rear visibility, interior impact protection, glazing, and occupant crash protection, have not even been proposed for rewriting. Half the rulebook for a car with no driver is still written for a car with one, and the half that matters most, how the car drives, was never in the rulebook at all. All of this is happening on roads where NHTSA estimates 16,750 people died in the first half of 2026,[7] in the same month the agency opened a recall query into whether Waymo’s fix stopped its robotaxis passing stopped school buses.[12]

Fairness demands the other side, stated at full strength. Self-certification is not a loophole, it is how every car on American roads is approved, and NHTSA’s post-market enforcement is the check. A gear display nobody reads and a wiper for a windshield nobody looks through are regulatory theater, and keeping them would be the unserious option. Standardizing the lamp color before mandating the lamp is how lighting regulation normally works, and the five-car validation is small because the eligible fleet is small. And the missing driving standard is a real gap, but writing a bad one fast would be worse than writing a good one slowly. All true, and all of it leaves the same fact standing: the rulebook is being sensibly pruned, but the pruning is outrunning the one rule that would actually govern the driving, and the two companies on the road today got there under opposite levels of scrutiny.

What this story does not prove: no final rule exists for any of the five proposals, so none of this is law yet, and NHTSA may change or drop any provision. Comment counts are a snapshot as of November 26, 2026, and they move as comments post. The “taking no position” footnote and the deregulatory framing come from secondary reads of the Federal Register documents, while the abstracts, citations, dockets, and comment counts for all five NPRMs and the Zoox exemption were verified directly against the Federal Register API this run. The Mercedes validation sample and the 80-percent survey figure are as cited in the FMVSS 108 proposal via secondary summaries, not independently verified. Waymo and Cybercab details are supported by the press sources in refs 11–12 and prior coverage on this site, and were not re-verified from NHTSA documents this run.

Here is what to do with all of this. If you have a view on robot cars glowing cyan, file a comment before December 10, 2026 at regulations.gov under Docket NHTSA-2026-1519, the last open window of the five. If you see cyan marker lamps on the road, the car is driving itself. If you see no cyan, assume nothing, because omission is compliant. And if you ride in a car with no steering wheel, know before you get in that no federal rule requires it to give you a way to stop it, reach a human, or get out. That is not a detail, it is the whole story in one sentence, and it is the sentence the rulebook has not written yet.[5]
