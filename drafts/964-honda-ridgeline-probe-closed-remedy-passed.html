NHTSA Spent 20 Months Investigating Whether Honda's Recall Worked. It Did.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 22, 2026
270×
Drop in the camera-failure complaint rate after Honda's harness remedy (directional estimate)

It started with fourteen complaints. Fourteen owners of 2017 through 2019 Honda Ridgelines told the federal government that their backup cameras had failed after Honda's recall repair, and NHTSA opened a formal engineering analysis covering 129,092 trucks.[1][3] Today, twenty months later, the investigation closed without further action, and the recall fix worked.

Back in November 2022, Honda recalled 117,445 Ridgelines under campaign 22V867000. The problem was almost insultingly mechanical: opening and closing the tailgate flexed the rear-view camera's wire harness until it fatigued and snapped, because the protective corrugated tubing was too short to guard against bending wear and the zip ties holding it in place were tightened too loosely.[2][4] Dealers replaced the harness with a longer-tubed, tighter-tied version, free of charge. Honda had logged 3,437 warranty claims for dead cameras between December 2017 and October 2022, with no injuries or deaths attached.[5]

Then the fix itself went under investigation. On June 26, 2024, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Recall Query RQ24-011, and the resume lists the trigger in plain language: "Prompted By: Recall Remedy Review."[2] It was not investigating Honda's trucks; it was investigating Honda's repair. When Honda acknowledged fourteen post-remedy failures, the query was upgraded to a full engineering analysis in February 2025.[3] NHTSA's Vehicle Research and Test Center surveyed owners, inspected selected trucks, and ran durability testing on the replacement harness.[1]

Twenty months later, the verdict: nothing. Testing found no camera failures caused by wire-harness damage, and the part is not expected to fail within a reasonable vehicle lifespan. Most of the incoming reports turned out to be other failure modes entirely, unlinked to the harness question.[1] Step back and the math is almost funny. Before the recall, 3,437 warranty claims on 117,445 trucks meant roughly 2.9 percent of owners saw this failure. After the remedy, fourteen reports on 129,092 trucks is about 0.011 percent. That is a 270-fold drop in the complaint rate. (Different reporting channels, so treat the ratio as directional, not gospel.) Forty-six months elapsed between the recall and today's all-clear.

Here is the part that ruins the joke: this is the system working. Fourteen post-remedy failures on a back-over-prevention camera is a legitimate reason to spend federal money, because the camera's entire job is keeping a 4,500-pound truck from reversing over a child. NHTSA followed the evidence, ran the tests, and closed the file when the data said the harness holds, which vindicates the repair. It does not fix the trucks whose cameras died from those other, unlinked failure modes. Their owners got a two-year investigation and a shrug.

If you drive a 2017 through 2019 Ridgeline, run your VIN through nhtsa.gov/recalls and look for 22V867000, Honda's 6RW. Never had the harness swapped: the replacement is free. Had it done and the camera still died? File a complaint at nhtsa.gov, because that is literally the mechanism here: fourteen reports opened a twenty-month federal investigation. A completed recall is not the end of the story. Sometimes it is just the part where the government starts grading the homework.
