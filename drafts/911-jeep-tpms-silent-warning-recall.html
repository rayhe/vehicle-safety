Stellantis Recalled 201,976 Jeeps Whose Tire-Pressure Warnings May Never Come. The Watchdog Can't Bark.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 16, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Twenty-six years ago, Congress decided your car should warn you when its tires are going flat. Last week, Stellantis recalled 201,976 Jeeps because the federally mandated tire-pressure monitoring system may never warn anyone at all. The guardian is asleep at the wheel, and the wheel is its own. FCA campaign 54D covers the 2024-2026 Grand Cherokee, Grand Cherokee L and 4XE, the Wagoneer and Grand Wagoneer families, and the 2026 Cherokee: a software error in the radio frequency hub can keep the system from detecting low tire pressure, or from lighting the warning lamp even if it does.[1][2]

Stellantis TPMS recalls in 13 months, with unrelated root causes and the identical failure signature: a silent warning.

Rewind to 1998, when strips of rubber started peeling off Firestone Wilderness AT tires on Ford Explorers, and the SUVs yawed, rolled, and killed their occupants at highway speed. By the time NHTSA pried the data loose in 2000, 271 people were dead and more than 700 injured. Congress held hearings, looked appropriately horrified, and passed the TREAD Act that October, ordering tire-pressure monitoring into every new passenger vehicle at about $48 a car, with GAO projecting 124 lives saved per year.[5] This site already noted the first crack in that edifice: FMVSS 138 only requires the light at 25 percent below placard pressure, within 20 minutes, and the tire industry itself said publicly this year that the mandate triggers too late to prevent damage. Now the recall upgrades laggard to mute: the watchdog doesn't just arrive late anymore, it may never leave the house.

And this is the second time in thirteen months Stellantis has recalled a tire-pressure monitoring system that couldn't monitor tire pressure. In September 2025 it pulled roughly 79,000 Jeep Wranglers because a remote-start antenna cable was getting pinched under the left rear seatbelt retractor bracket during assembly, taking the TPMS down with it; supplier Harada Industry of America, investigation running October 2024 to August 2025.[4] A pinched cable and a software bug are mechanically unrelated. Same automaker, same safety system, same silence, one year apart. Two completely different ways to build the same nothing. To be fair, FCA opened the current investigation itself in August 2025 after its own engineers found the TPMS problems, which is how the system is supposed to work: credit where due, then take it back, because the recall letters don't start until September 29, a full thirteen months after the engineers started asking questions.[3]

Now the case for the defense, at full strength, because it is stronger than it looks. TPMS was always the weakest federal safety mandate, and this site said so first: the horseshoe light legally stays dark until you're 25 percent under placard, by which point your sidewalls are already cooking and your wet stopping distance has grown by double digits. A ten-dollar gauge and your own eyes have always been the real system. Nobody has crashed or died in any record tied to this defect. Stellantis found the bug in its own engineering shop, filed the recall voluntarily, and the fix is a free thirty-minute software flash at the dealer. The warning light may be mute, but the bureaucracy spoke, and that is not nothing in an industry where companies have historically needed federal crowbars to admit anything.

What we do not know is nearly as important. "Potentially affected" means every Jeep carrying the suspect radio frequency hub software, not the number whose TPMS is actually deaf, and Stellantis has not said what that real number is. No crash or injury count is attached to the defect in any source we reviewed, so the marginal risk of a silent TPMS versus a working one cannot be quantified from public data; it depends entirely on how many owners check their pressures manually, which is the kind of behavior data nobody collects until a deposition. The two Stellantis TPMS recalls share an outcome, not an engineering lineage, and this does not prove Stellantis builds worse tire-pressure systems than anyone else; it proves they have been caught twice.

So here is what to actually do if you drive a 2024-2026 Grand Cherokee, Wagoneer, or Grand Wagoneer in any flavor, or a 2026 Cherokee: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls right now instead of waiting for the letter that may not arrive until October 21. Buy the gauge, and check cold pressures monthly against the placard on your driver door jamb, because the horseshoe was a lagging indicator even when it worked. And if your cluster shows a "service tire pressure system" message, a malfunction lamp, or dashes where pressure values should be, get to a dealer: the flash is free and takes about half an hour. The dashboard light was never the sensor; you were always the sensor.[2][3]
