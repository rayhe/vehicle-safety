Ford Redesigned the Expedition for 2007. Its Death Rate Fell 85% Overnight.

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Editor
November 8, 2026

Ten model years of Ford Expeditions killed 1,284 people in the crashes NHTSA counted between 2014 and 2023.[1] Fifteen model years after that killed 228. Nothing about the badge changed, and nothing about the engine changed. What changed was model year 2007, and the death rate fell off a cliff so steep it looks like a data error, but it is not. Adjusted for how long each model year was actually on the road, the death rate dropped 85 percent in a single redesign.

85%
Exposure-adjusted drop in deaths per model year, pre-2007 Expeditions vs. 2007-and-newer (FARS 2014-2023)

Start with the raw counts, because they are the bluntest version of the story. Model year 2004 is the worst Expedition ever built, by this measure: 198 deaths. Model year 2006, the last of the old design, recorded 106. Then 2007 arrived with 29, and no Expedition built after that year ever topped 29 again. Worst year of the new design is still safer than the best year of the old one, and the old design’s best year, 2002 with 88 deaths, is triple the new design’s worst.

Ford did not redesign the truck from scratch. Its 5.4-liter Triton V8 carried over untouched, same 300 horsepower, same torque. Underneath, though, nearly everything that keeps people alive changed at once: a frame 10 percent stiffer with driveshafts routed through the rails, a lower center of gravity, bigger brakes, a six-speed automatic, seat-mounted side airbags, and side curtain airbags covering all three rows.[3][4] Above all of it, AdvanceTrac with Roll Stability Control became standard equipment, a gyroscopic sensor watching yaw and roll angles and braking individual wheels before a tall SUV becomes a rolling one.[5]

That last item does most of the explaining. IIHS research puts the fatal single-vehicle crash reduction from electronic stability control at roughly half, and the benefit concentrates exactly where big SUVs are weakest: rollovers.[2] A three-ton truck with a high center of gravity is a rollover waiting for a bad second, and stability control buys back that second. Ford’s own launch material called the system class-exclusive. FARS suggests it was also, quietly, life-saving at a scale no advertising campaign ever claimed.

Give the objection its full weight, because the numbers invite it. Older Expeditions are scarcer on the road, and a 1997 truck still driving in 2023 was probably not the best-maintained example of its breed. Newer model years had fewer years inside the FARS window to accumulate deaths, and both effects inflate the gap. But attrition is a slope, and this is a step: the drop lands exactly on model year 2007 and stays flat for fifteen years, including recent years with nearly full exposure. A slow fade does not produce a cliff.

What you can do with this. Shopping a used full-size SUV? A 2007-or-newer Expedition is a different safety proposition than a 2006, same nameplate, same engine, same badge on the tailgate. Check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls either way.[6] And at startup, watch the AdvanceTrac light: it should illuminate, then go out. If it never lights, the system that made the cliff is not watching anymore.
