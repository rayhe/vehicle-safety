VW Knew About ID.4 Battery Fires for Nearly Two Years Before Recalling a Single Car

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 10, 2026

Before you plug in your ID.4 tonight, read this. Volkswagen's first known ID.4 battery fire happened in January 2024, while the car was charging. VW sent investigators, confirmed the fire started inside the high-voltage battery, then kept selling and servicing the cars as if nothing had happened. The first recall arrived in December 2025. 683 days passed between the fire VW investigated and the recall VW issued.

683 days
From the first known ID.4 battery fire to the first recall

Three more battery fires followed between July and December 2024, according to the federal class action filed September 4 in New Jersey federal court. The complaint in Chen v Volkswagen Aktiengesellschaft alleges the 2023-2025 ID.4s carry high-voltage batteries with misaligned electrodes that can spark thermal events and fires.[1] These are allegations, not findings, but the recall record is not disputed.

NHTSA's database tells the rest of the story. Recall one landed in December 2025 and covered 629 cars; recall two followed on January 21, 2026 with 670; recall three arrived the same day with 43,881 cars, the battery-overheating campaign VW coded 93EA. In March, the problem went global: roughly 100,000 VW electric vehicles worldwide, per Germany's motor vehicle authority, bringing the American total to 45,180 ID.4s across three campaigns.[2][3]

And here is the part that should make every ID.4 owner furious. While the recalls were rolling out, VW's interim guidance told owners to cap charging at 80 percent, park outside after charging, skip overnight charging, and avoid DC fast chargers. For a car whose entire pitch is convenient home charging, VW's safety advice was, effectively: do not charge it at home, like an electric car, overnight; read that again.

Worse: in January 2026, VW disclosed that some thermal incidents fell "outside the ranges identified by the [HV battery] supplier," and that it had not determined a "clear root cause" for them. Nearly two years after the first fire, the company still cannot say exactly why its batteries catch fire.

Fairness requires the other side of the story. Four fires across tens of thousands of vehicles is a low absolute rate, and chasing a defect through a supplier's production range before recalling is arguably diligent, not negligent. Recalls did happen, and "No clear root cause" is also what honest engineering looks like mid-investigation. A plaintiff's complaint is a starting gun, not a verdict.

None of that changes what you should do. If you own a 2023-2025 ID.4, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaigns 25V836, 26V028, and 26V030, then book the free remedy: a battery software update and module replacement if needed. Until the fix is done, follow the interim guidance: charge to 80 percent, park outside, and skip overnight charging in the garage. Recalls follow the car, not the owner, so used-car buyers need to check too.

What this story did not prove: whether the complaint's timeline will hold up in court, how many thermal incidents VW has actually logged, or why the root cause remains elusive. What it did prove: VW sat on a known battery-fire risk for the better part of two years while the cars sat in people's garages, plugged in, overnight. That part is in the public record, so sleep on that.
