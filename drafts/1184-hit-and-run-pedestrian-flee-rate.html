One in Four Dead Pedestrians Was Left on the Road. The Drivers Who Fled Are Exactly Who You'd Expect.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

According to the crash reports, and there are 919,292 of them in 2023 alone, America has a fleeing problem. The AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety just published the first national accounting of hit-and-run crashes in nearly a decade, and the headline number is this: 919,292 police-reported crashes in 2023 involved a driver who left the scene, 15.0 percent of every crash in America, a historic high,[1] which works out to roughly 2,500 a day, 242,886 injuries, and 2,872 deaths, the last of those being 7.0 percent of all traffic deaths, the largest share ever recorded.

Now zoom in on who gets left behind: of the 1,818 pedestrians killed in 2023, one in four died in a hit-and-run.[1] Cyclists: 23.5 percent; vehicle occupants: 1.8 percent; a driver who hits a pedestrian is roughly 13 times more likely to flee than a driver who hits another car. From 2017 to 2023, 11,019 pedestrians died in hit-and-runs, which was 23.1 percent of all pedestrian deaths in that span.[1]

Forget the panicked everyman. Of the drivers caught after fatal hit-and-runs, 78.3 percent were male, 71.4 percent were between 18 and 44, and 40 percent lacked a valid license: one in five had never been licensed at all, and another one in five was suspended or revoked.[1] More than half were driving somebody else's car, and seven in ten crashed within 10 miles of home. This is not a whodunit; it is a ledger of pre-existing liability: no license, no insurance, no legal way to be behind that wheel in the first place, which is exactly why that wheel keeps ending up abandoned by the side of the road.

Alcohol is the other motive the data can't see but keeps implying: earlier research the brief leans on found intoxicated crashers more likely to flee, and fear of a DUI bust is a named reason for running.[1] That fits the clock: hit-and-run deaths peak between 11 PM and 3 AM, and in that window a third of dead pedestrians (33.9 percent) and 43.6 percent of dead cyclists were left on the road,[1] which is what darkness does to a conscience when the thing you hit was small, the street was empty, and the bar was still serving.

From the mid-80s until 2010, the hit-and-run share of traffic deaths sat between 3.4 and 4.4 percent; then it started climbing, spiked in 2020, hit a record 2,972 deaths in 2022, and reached a record 7.0 percent share in 2023.[1] Two weeks ago in Fresno, a 78-year-old man pushing a cart across McKinley Avenue was struck at 8:24 PM and the driver kept going; police found the car days later, abandoned, covered in tarps weighted with rocks, and arrested a 30-year-old driving on a suspended license who had been identified through a license plate reader.[2] Unlicensed, somebody else's liability, near home: the profile fit the national data like a glove with the fingers still wet.

Where you are matters as much as when: in big cities, 32.6 percent of pedestrian deaths are hit-and-runs versus 17.3 percent in rural areas, and in the most socially vulnerable zip codes, hit-and-runs are 11.7 percent of all traffic deaths with six times the absolute death count of the least vulnerable.[1] Poverty doesn't cause fleeing; it correlates with everything that does: dark streets, no sidewalks, unlicensed drivers, old cars nobody bothered to register. Last Sunday in Newark, a 62-year-old was killed in a marked crosswalk at 7:44 PM by a pickup that fled; the driver was arrested within days.[3]

Fair counterpoint: part of the long rise is better counting, not worse behavior, because hit-and-run coding in federal data improved over decades, so some of the climb from 4 to 7 percent is the lens, not the subject. And there is a mechanical explanation for the pedestrian asymmetry, which is that hitting a person at night is easier to not-feel than hitting a car, and a pedestrian crash leaves less evidence a crash happened at all, which is why the brief's central finding doubles as its prescription: preventing crashes prevents hit-and-runs, and nothing else scales. After that, the lever is deterrence, and deterrence is cameras plus courts that actually clear cases. The Fresno driver was caught by a plate reader.[1][2]

What you can actually do. A dashcam, front and rear. Non-negotiable. When the other driver doesn't stop, your footage is the only witness. Reflective gear and facing traffic at night, because one in four pedestrian deaths is a hit-and-run and the share hits a third after 11 PM; and if you are in a crash, get the plate in the first 10 seconds, because fleers decide in seconds. Report immediately: most fleers crashed within 10 miles of home and the ones who get caught get caught near home,[1] and carry uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage while you're at it. Four in ten fatal fleers had no valid license, which is the closest federal data gets to saying they had no insurance either.
