A Cracked Capacitor Can Make Your Airbag Fire at a Child

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. Your Honda's passenger airbag doesn't simply go off in a crash; it makes a decision first. A weight sensor in the seat estimates who is sitting there, and if the answer is an infant in a car seat, a child, or anyone smaller than a 5th-percentile adult woman, the system is supposed to stand down, because a deploying airbag can injure the small occupant more than the crash itself. Honda just recalled 98,892 vehicles because a single cracked capacitor can override that decision.[1]

98,892
Honda and Acura vehicles recalled under NHTSA campaign 26V332000. Honda estimates about 1% carry the defective seat sensor, with zero injuries reported so far.

Exposure to humidity cracks a capacitor on the sensor's printed circuit board, and that crack causes an internal short circuit. A shorted sensor can no longer weigh the passenger, and in the resulting confusion the front passenger frontal and knee airbags may deploy in a crash despite the presence of an occupant for whom deployment should have been suppressed.[1] The component that decides whether your kid gets hit by an airbag is, functionally, a bathroom scale wired to an explosive. And its circuit board got swapped for an unverified one.

When a natural disaster struck the manufacturing plant of a tier-2 supplier, the tier-1 supplier, Aisin Electronics Illinois, temporarily changed the printed circuit board base material without verifying it was fit for the job.[1] That substitute material allowed extra strain on the board, and extra strain cracked capacitors. Honda switched back to the original verified base material on January 7, 2022, which means the defective sensors went into cars for years before anyone connected the dots. Your airbag's judgment call was being made by a board that was never qualified for the position.

This is also a sequel, and sequels mean the first one missed something. In January 2024 Honda recalled more than 750,000 vehicles for the same sensor defect under campaign 24V064. These 98,892 exist because of three distinct bookkeeping errors, stated plainly in the federal filing: the supplier miscalculated the production end date of the defective part population, Honda used inaccurate data correlating defective parts to actual vehicles, and nobody adequately verified vehicles that received defective sensors as replacement service parts.[1] Read that third one again. Some of these cars got the bad sensor installed as a repair; the fix was the defect.

Start with the warning sign, which is specific: the SRS warning light illuminates and the passenger airbag indicator stays off.[3] If you drive a 2016-2022 Honda Civic, Accord, CR-V, Pilot, Odyssey, Ridgeline, Passport, Fit, HR-V, or Insight, or a 2017-2026 Acura MDX, RDX, or TLX, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today.[4] Dealers replace the seat weight sensor free of charge, and Honda logged 228 warranty claims with no injuries as of the filing.[1] Do not wait for a letter that was mailed in July.

Fairness requires the other side, so here it is at full strength. Two hundred twenty-eight warranty claims across nearly 99,000 vehicles, with roughly one percent estimated to carry the defect and zero injuries on record, makes the risk to any individual owner small. Honda investigated for over a year, from February 2025 to May 2026, before determining the defect, which is diligent rather than evasive. The remedy replaces the sensor instead of reprogramming it, and the production line has used the verified material since January 2022. For the overwhelming majority of owners, the airbag will do exactly what it was designed to do.

Limitations: Honda's one-percent defect estimate is an estimate, and the 228 warranty claims come from Honda's own records, so both could understate the real population. The filing describes only the deploy-when-suppressed failure; a cracked sensor could in principle also fail the other way and suppress an airbag an adult needed, but NHTSA's report does not describe that mode. Nothing here uses FARS fatality data, because there are no fatalities to count, which is genuinely good news. And the humidity mechanism means cars in dry climates may never see the failure at all.

An airbag exists to make one violent decision correctly, once, in 150 milliseconds. Honda's filing is unusually honest about how that decision got outsourced to an unverified circuit board, miscounted in three different ways, and in some cases installed as the repair for itself. Check the VIN and watch for the SRS light. Your passenger seat should be weighing your kid, not gambling with the airbag.
