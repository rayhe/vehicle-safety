The Gap

The Buick LeSabre Aced Its Crash Test. The Real World Gave It an F.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
January 18, 2028

In its final model year, the 2005 Buick LeSabre was America's best-selling full-size sedan, a plush V6 cruiser sold to sensible people who wanted a safe car. IIHS gave its frontal crash test a Good, with Good marks for structure, chest protection, and restraints, while NHTSA handed its passengers five stars. Then the decade from 2014 to 2023 happened, and NHTSA's own Fatality Analysis Reporting System counted 1,344 dead LeSabre occupants.

0.82
LeSabre occupant deaths per fatal-crash involvement, 2014–2023, 4th highest of 163 high-volume models

That number is not a body count. FARS records 1,633 fatal-crash involvements for the LeSabre over the decade, and 1,344 deaths among its occupants. Divide one by the other and you get 0.82. When a LeSabre is in a crash that kills someone, a LeSabre occupant is the victim 82 percent of the time. Across all vehicles the figure is 0.57, and across sedans it is 0.65. A Ram 2500, a truck built to flatten whatever it hits, posts 0.20.

Nor does the death rate flatter it. At an estimated 2.67 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles, the LeSabre kills at nearly double the sedan average of 1.39 and nearly triple the all-vehicle average of 0.91. And this is not a story about the rusted-out 1988s. Of the 1,344 deaths, 924 (69 percent) come from the 2000 to 2005 generation, the exact cars that carry that Good IIHS rating, with model year 2004 alone accounting for 188. The good test score did not save these cars; it described a different car entirely, a new one, in a lab, hitting one barrier at one speed from one direction.

That is the gap, and it matters before you spend three grand on one. IIHS never ran an original side-impact test on this car, and its head-restraint rating was Poor. No LeSabre of this generation ever got electronic stability control or side curtain airbags. A frontal-offset test measures one scenario beautifully and says nothing about the T-bone at an intersection, the rear-ender on a freeway, or the rollover on a county road. Meanwhile every LeSabre on the road in 2014 to 2023 was 9 to 24 years old, with two decades of wear on belts, bags, and brakes, carrying a test score from when it was new.

Now the strongest case against everything you just read. LeSabre buyers skewed old, and old bodies break more easily in any crash. FARS does not adjust for occupant age, so some of that 0.82 is frailty, not sheet metal. That objection is real, yet it does not explain why the LeSabre's death rate is nearly double the sedan average while its impairment rate, 23.5 percent of its drivers in fatal crashes versus 20.0 percent nationally, is only modestly elevated, so drunk drivers are not driving this result. Nor does frailty explain why its sibling, the Buick Century, posts a nearly identical 0.77. Two GM sedans from the same era, same pattern, same ending.

So here is the buying advice, plain: if you are shopping under $5,000, a LeSabre's Good rating is a 25-year-old snapshot of one crash scenario, and the real-world record says it loses its own occupants in four out of five fatal crashes it touches. A 2012 or newer sedan with stability control and side curtain airbags costs about the same money and brings a safety record from this century. If you already own a LeSabre, retire it from highway duty first, hand the teenager something newer, and check the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change. A crash-test trophy is not a safety record; ten years of FARS is.

Limitations. FARS captures fatal crashes only, so injury-only performance is invisible here. VMT and fleet figures are estimates from sales data, not odometer readings. The lethality index counts deaths among the vehicle's own occupants per fatal-crash involvement; in multi-vehicle crashes, occupants of several vehicles can die. Most importantly, these figures are not age-adjusted for occupants, so part of the LeSabre's lethality may reflect its older buyer base rather than its structure alone. The NHTSA star ratings for 2005 are as reported by secondary summaries; the IIHS Good rating is verified directly from IIHS's own page.
