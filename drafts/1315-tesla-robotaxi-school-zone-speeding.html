The Driver Was Speeding in a School Zone. There Was No Driver.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 29, 2026

On October 9, 2026, NHTSA’s Office of Defects Investigation opened file DP26010 — a defect petition about a traffic violation with no violator: Tesla’s robotaxis allegedly speeding through active school zones. The count: roughly 1,000 Model Ys and Cybercabs, the agency’s first public number on how big Tesla’s driverless operation actually is.[1]

1,000
Robotaxis in NHTSA’s estimate — the first federal number for Tesla’s fleet

What makes this one weird: a human blowing through a school zone gets a ticket, a fine, higher insurance. Here, the only entity deciding how fast to go past the school is Tesla’s Automated Driving System. If the petition holds, the software broke the law, and there is nobody in the car to punish. Per the case file: four incidents and three social media videos. Zero crashes, zero injuries, zero deaths: just the principle of it.

NHTSA is required to grant or deny the petition within 120 days under 49 CFR Part 552, which puts the decision around January 21, 2027. A grant means a formal defect investigation, and those can end in a recall. This is the third federal action against Tesla’s driverless fleet in about five weeks, following an audit query and a Special Order demanding Tesla prove a car with no pedals or steering wheel meets federal standards.[2]

The larger timeline matters more. NHTSA opened a Preliminary Evaluation of FSD in October 2024, then a separate traffic-law probe in October 2025. A week later, Tesla shipped a “Mad Max” FSD profile that ignores speed limits. The difference: with FSD (Supervised), a human picks the profile and stays responsible. A robotaxi has no owner behind the wheel to accept the ticket. When the ADS decides how fast to go past a school, Tesla made that decision.

Waymo has its own entry: Austin ISD reported about 20 Waymo vehicles illegally passing stopped school buses late last year; Waymo answered with a voluntary software recall that December.[3] That is the playbook for how this ends if the petition is granted — a software recall, filed with NHTSA, for a vehicle no one drives.

Here is the honest accounting of the limits: the petition is unevaluated; NHTSA has not found a defect, and the evidence file is thin. School-zone limits confuse human drivers too, and GPS lag around construction zones has embarrassed worse systems. Four incidents against a 1,000-vehicle fleet is not an epidemic. Strongest counterargument: zero crashes, so this could be regulatory theater over the most politically radioactive violation on the books — speeding around kids.

Still. A machine that cannot be ticketed is a machine that cannot be deterred, and deterrence is the whole theory behind a school zone fine. If you live in Austin, Dallas, or Houston and a robotaxi runs a school zone, you can file a complaint with NHTSA — complaints are exactly what feeds petitions like DP26010. Mark the calendar for January 21, 2027: that is when NHTSA must decide whether three videos of a driverless car speeding past a school become a real investigation, or just a file number.
