## Verified facts (primary sources first)
1. **The bill — Anthony Amoros Law Right to Know Act, Rep. Mike Lawler (R-NY, District 17), introduced October 2026 (bill text dated October 5, 2026; Wisevoter lists it as H.R. 10762, referred to the House Committee on Energy and Commerce and the Committee on the Judiciary).** It would require the seller of a used motor vehicle sold through an auction, dealership, individual sale, or any other resale channel to sell it with a functional airbag system or clearly disclose that the system is missing or disabled. Covered vehicles are those manufactured on or after September 1, 1997. Disclosure must be BOTH a written statement in at least 10-point bold type on the front of the sales contract, receipt, invoice, or other sale document AND a conspicuous sign affixed to the windshield. Enforcement by the Federal Trade Commission and state authorities after FTC notice; the FTC may intervene in actions or file appeals. (Bill text via lawler.house.gov; reported by Autobody News, Oct 13, 2026; Repairer Driven News, Oct 9, 2026; Wisevoter, Oct 14, 2026.)
2. **Penalties — a new Title 18 section on resale of unsafe motor vehicles.** A seller who knowingly sells a covered vehicle with a nonworking or missing airbag system without the written statement or windshield disclosure faces fines and prison: up to 10 years for attempts and all other cases; up to 20 years if serious bodily injury, including paralysis, results; up to life in prison if a death results. Courts may also award an additional $25,000 in an action involving death or paralysis. (Autobody News penalty tiers; Repairer Driven News $25,000 award.)
3. **Namesake — Anthony Amoros, Rockland County, New York, died in 2013 at age 18** after crashing on a windy stretch of road in a car his family had purchased without knowing it lacked an airbag. (CBS News New York via Repairer Driven News.) New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed a state Anthony Amoros Law in 2021 prohibiting used-car dealers from selling vehicles without a working airbag and requiring certification that the vehicle is equipped with an airbag. (Spectrum News via Repairer Driven News.) New York Senate bill 2025-S9615 (introduced March 30, 2026, Sen. Weber) would extend the same disclosure to automobile auctioneers, retail dealers, AND individual sellers, with the same 10-point bold written disclosure plus windshield sign. (NY Senate bill text, primary.)
4. **Why now — NHTSA confirmed September 3, 2026 an 11th death tied to a banned replacement inflator from China.** The death involved a 2018 Chevrolet Equinox in Dallas on August 27, 2026, described by NHTSA as an otherwise survivable crash. Before the Equinox case, all deaths plus three severe injuries involved Chevrolet Malibu and Hyundai Sonata models. In each case the original frontal driver airbag inflator had been swapped for a unit marked DTN60DB, likely imported illegally. NHTSA barred importation and sale of the inflators earlier in 2026, its first such ban in more than 20 years. A VIN lookup will NOT identify these inflators because they are aftermarket components with no link to the vehicle identification number; inspection is the only way to tell. Suspect units bear a DTN60DB marking; a label on the electrical connector side may show 144415654 666631 or 144415654 666633. (NHTSA via Autobody News; NHTSA technician guidance dated April 2, 2026, listing suspect-module signs: mismatched trim texture, absent or poorly formed tear seams, faint SRS lettering, mismatched inflator labels, tool or grind marks, missing warning labels, connector colors not matching OEM, missing shorting bars, burn marks, and SRS lamps bypassed with resistors or diodes.)
5. **The gap the bill closes — current federal anti-tampering law, 49 U.S.C. 30122(b), bars a manufacturer, distributor, dealer, rental company, or motor vehicle repair business from knowingly making a safety device inoperative, but private individuals are not on that list.** A private seller rebuilding and flipping a wrecked car falls outside it. The Amoros bill covers any seller in any channel, including private sales and auctions. (Statute as quoted by The Auto Wire, Oct 12, 2026, DTN60DB order piece.)
6. **The salvage pipeline that feeds the problem — reported context, flagged as reported, not primary data.** About 2.5 million vehicles were totaled by insurers last year before eventually returning to U.S. roadways (Automotive News opinion piece via Repairer Driven News). Vehicles often go to auction; public auctions may report a deployment to NMVTIS, but wholesale or private auction crash information is not always captured; retitling inspection varies by state and many states do not require airbag inspections (Teena Bohi, Automotive Anti-Counterfeiting Council board member, Collision Industry Conference, July 2026, via Repairer Driven News). In September 2026 a California judge suspended a Los Angeles dealer's license after a Bureau of Automotive Repair investigation found fraudulent vehicle safety systems inspections, and a Michigan police officer was sentenced for falsifying salvage vehicle inspections (both via Repairer Driven News).

## Original contribution (required)
The bill does not mention counterfeit airbag parts at all (both Autobody News and Repairer Driven News note this expressly). That omission is the story's hinge: the disclosure duty turns on whether the system is "missing or disabled" / "functional," and a car carrying a banned DTN60DB inflator has an airbag that looks present, passes a casual glance, and cannot be caught by a VIN check, yet may be the most dangerous configuration on the lot. Whether such a car counts as "deficient" under the bill will turn on enforcement interpretation, and the article says so rather than assuming it. Second original cross-check: the bill's two-part disclosure (contract front page + windshield sign) is strictly stronger than New York's 2021 dealer-certification law and matches the pending NY Senate S9615 text, so the federal bill effectively nationalizes a disclosure regime New York is still trying to extend to private sellers at home.

## Actionable insights (for article)
1. If you buy used: do not accept a clean VIN/recall check as proof the airbags work. Demand a written airbag certification, check the history report for deployments, total-loss events, and noncertified repairs, and pay a certified technician to inspect before you sign. An SRS lamp that never lights at startup, trim that does not match, or faint SRS lettering are walk-away signs.
2. If you sell used, even privately: once this bill is law, "I didn't know" will not be a defense to a knowing sale without disclosure, and the disclosure costs a bold paragraph plus a windshield sign. Disclose or fix it first.
3. If you own a car repaired after a crash: ask the shop for the airbag part numbers and compare them to the OEM part number. A mismatch is the single most checkable red flag in NHTSA's technician guidance.
