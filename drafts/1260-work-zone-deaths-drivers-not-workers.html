By The Numbers

Work Zones Kill Two People a Day. Almost None of Them Are Workers.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
December 24, 2027

Every spring, the orange signs go up and the campaign begins: slow down, save a worker's life. Federal data says the campaign picked the wrong protagonist. In 2024, 850 people died in American work zones, and 673 of them were drivers and passengers.[1] Another 177 were pedestrians, a category that includes workers but also anyone else on foot near the cones. At least two people a day die in work zones, and the people dying are overwhelmingly the people driving through them.

79%
Share of 2024 work zone deaths who were drivers or passengers (673 of 850)

Run the split a second way and it gets starker. National Safety Council's breakdown of the same 850 deaths puts drivers at 523 (62%), passengers at 150 (18%), pedestrians at 156 (18%), and bicyclists at 13 (2%).[2] Workers, counted separately by the Bureau of Labor Statistics through occupational fatality data, come to roughly 54 worker-pedestrians struck and killed per year.[2] That is about 6% of the annual work zone body count. Protecting workers is a fine reason to slow down. It is not, numerically, the main thing slowing down accomplishes.

Nor is this a shrinking problem wearing a scary costume. Fatal work zone crashes rose 46% between 2010 and 2024, while fatal crashes overall rose 20%.[3] In 2024 there were 763 fatal work zone crashes and 42,094 people injured in work zone crashes.[2][3] Across 2020 through 2024, work zone crashes killed 4,481 people, an average of about 896 a year.[4] Work zone deaths did fall from 905 in 2023 to 850 in 2024, and they are down 12% from the 2021 peak.[1][2] A declining catastrophe is still a catastrophe at two funerals a day.

45%
Share of fatal work zone crashes on interstates, freeways, or expressways, versus 16% of fatal crashes overall

Speed explains most of the inversion. Nearly half of fatal work zone crashes happen on interstates, freeways, or expressways, compared with 16% of fatal crashes overall.[3] A work zone on a 70 mph highway does not ask drivers to be careful in the abstract. It asks a stream of traffic doing highway speed to compress into fewer lanes, at a taper, with a speed limit that dropped 20 mph two signs ago. Drivers who brake late, merge late, or thread the taper at full speed are not endangering a flagger a quarter mile ahead. They are endangering the car in the next lane and themselves, which is exactly what the death certificates say.

None of this argues against protecting workers. It argues for telling drivers the truth about whose life the orange sign is saving, because the current framing lets every driver believe the danger is to someone else. It is not. Work zone safety is driver safety with cones on it, and the driver who slows down early, merges like a zipper instead of a dare, and drops speed before the first sign rather than at it is not performing charity for a construction crew. That driver is cutting their own odds in the one crash category that grew more than twice as fast as crashes overall.

What to actually do with this

Drop your speed before the first work zone sign, not at the taper, and merge early instead of racing to the front of the closing lane. Nearly half of fatal work zone crashes happen on high-speed roads, so the speed differential at the merge is the risk, not the cones themselves. If your daily commute runs through a construction corridor, that repeated exposure is the hazard to manage: leave two minutes earlier and hold a steady, lower speed through the whole zone rather than braking hard at each lane drop.
