The 2025 Telluride Keeps Stalling. No Recall Covers It.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 3, 2026

Your engine quits at 50 miles an hour. An alarm sounds, the dash orders you to shift to park, and the steering goes heavy as the brakes lose their assist. Then a dealer plugs in a scan tool, finds no code, and sends you home. That is the script 35 owners of the 2025 Kia Telluride have now filed with federal regulators, and no recall or investigation covers a single one of them.[1]

35 of 124
2025 Telluride NHTSA complaints describing engine stalling, as of November 2, 2026

Read the complaints and a pattern snaps into focus. Owners describe the engine cutting out while slowing for intersections, in turns, and at 45 to 50 mph, always with the same dash message: "Engine Off! Shift to P or N then start engine."[1] At 28% of everything owners have reported on the 2025, stalling is not background noise. For comparison, stalling accounts for just 6% of complaints on the 2020 model, and owners have already filed 54 powertrain complaints on the 2025, more than the 50 the 2023 drew over two additional years on the road.[1] One owner counted five shutoffs, three within minutes, in creeping traffic and while changing lanes with traffic on all sides.[5]

More than half the trail goes cold at the dealership. In 18 of the 35 stalling complaints, the owner says the dealer could not reproduce the problem or found no fault.[1] That failure is structural, not lazy: an intermittent stall that sets no diagnostic code and lights no warning lamp leaves a scan tool with nothing to read, so "could not duplicate" becomes the correct answer to the wrong question. A defect that leaves no trace defeats the exact process designed to confirm it.

Kia knows something is happening, and its September 2026 technician bulletin, Pitstop PS815, titled "ALLEGED STALL AT LOW SPEED," covers some 2025 Tellurides where customers report stalling with that exact dash message and no warning light or diagnostic code at the dealer.[2] Techs are told to replace the variable force solenoid on each cylinder bank plus the manifold absolute pressure sensor, and the removed parts "will be collected by Kia for further analysis," which read plainly means Kia is running an investigation through warranty parts collection, gathering failed hardware one dealer visit at a time. And customers are never told, because the bulletin states it "does not involve customer notification."[2]

Meanwhile, the 2025's three actual recalls cover everything except the stalling: door belt molding can delaminate (25V494), an incorrect spare tire fitted to just 24 Tellurides can upset the anti-lock brakes (25V745), and front seat-back frames on 85,515 SUVs may not restrain occupants in a rear impact (26V105).[1][3][4] A buyer checking the recall list sees a car with its paperwork in order. Nothing on that list mentions an engine that quits at speed.

Keep the scale honest, because in FARS data from 2014 through 2023, the Telluride recorded 31 deaths at a rate of 0.04 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, among the lowest rates in the dataset.[6] FARS ends at 2023, so it holds no 2025 Telluride records at all, and with numbers like those nobody should call this a death trap, since it is a different and narrower failure: a car that can go dark at speed while the system built to catch defects has no box to check.

What to do, concretely, starts with filing a complaint at NHTSA.gov if your 2025 Telluride stalls, because complaint clusters are how investigations start, and complaint 36 matters more than complaint 35 did. At the dealer, ask specifically about Pitstop PS815 and the variable force solenoid and MAP sensor replacement, not a generic engine check. Note the speed, the gear, and whether any light appeared, since "no code" is itself diagnostic information. If you are shopping used, drive a 2025 through slow turns and stops, and ask for stalling service records before signing. With no 2026 Telluride in existence, Kia jumped straight to a redesigned 2027, which makes the 2025 the final year of the V6 and its defect record the one used buyers inherit.[1]

What this analysis does not prove. Complaints are owner-reported and unverified, and 35 complaints do not mean 35 confirmed defects; some reports may describe the auto stop-start system or other causes, and most owners never file at all, so the true incidence could run either way. Kia has not named a root cause, and the Pitstop's sensor swap is a diagnostic step for parts collection, not a confirmed fix. No crash, injury, or fatality has been tied to the 2025 stalling cluster in the sources reviewed, and "no recall or investigation" is as of November 2, 2026.

Strongest counterargument. Thirty-five complaints on 167,060 vehicles is 0.02% of production, and owner complaints are the noisiest dataset in auto safety, mixing real defects with misunderstood features and one-off electrical gremlins. More than half the dealers found nothing, which is also consistent with nothing being wrong. Kia's Pitstop is standard practice, not a cover-up: collecting parts on an emerging issue is how manufacturers learn, and swapping two inexpensive sensors is a cheap diagnostic sweep, not an admission. On that view, this article is 35 anecdotes in search of a defect, and the honest headline is that a cluster worth watching is not yet a defect worth recalling. That distinction is exactly what the next 35 complaints will decide.
