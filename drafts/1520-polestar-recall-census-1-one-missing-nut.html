Polestar Filed One Recall in 2026. It Was for a Nut Nobody Tightened.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 15, 2026

Before you buy the story that Polestar had a quiet safety year, look at the count: one recall, 213 cars, and a defect that is almost insultingly simple. The striker bracket that holds the rear seat backrest in place was supposed to be tightened with two nuts, and workers at the Charleston, South Carolina plant tightened one.[2]

1
NHTSA recall campaigns Polestar filed in 2026, covering 213 vehicles, the smallest non-zero recall year in our census series

Count the year, because the number is the story. Polestar filed seven recalls in 2025, including the third backup-camera recall that NHTSA spent fourteen months auditing before closing the file on September 1.[5] In 2026, the filing cabinet stayed shut until January 23, when the seat-bracket recall landed, and then nothing: no Polestar 2 recall, no Polestar 4 recall, nothing at all after January. Our census queried every Polestar model across every model year and found exactly one campaign with a 2026 filing date.[1]

Now the part that stings is that Polestar moved Polestar 3 production to Charleston to build an American-made EV, the car that was supposed to survive the crackdown on Chinese-connected vehicles. Its one recall of the year is a Charleston assembly error. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 207 exists so a seat backrest holds in a crash; with half its fasteners, the backrest can fall forward and let cargo into the cabin.[3] Two hundred thirteen cars, each missing half the hardware on a safety-critical bracket. Fix is a wrench, not a part: inspect it, mount it right, torque both nuts.

None of this is the brand's real 2026 story, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. On June 25, the Commerce Department declined to authorize Polestar under the Connected Vehicles Rule, which bars sales of new cars with China-linked connected-vehicle technology from the 2027 model year, and Polestar said it would not appeal.[4] Volvo, its Geely sibling, got the authorization; Polestar did not. So the brand spent its last American year selling down existing Polestar 3 and Polestar 4 stock while its service network stays open for the cars already here. A one-recall year is what a wind-down looks like on paper.

Fair point, and it deserves its full weight: one recall is the safety system working. 213 cars, zero injuries, a free fix, a manufacturer that caught its own assembly error and filed it, while Ford filed 85 recalls in 2026.[8] By that measure Polestar looks like the best-run quality operation in the industry. Grant all of that, then look at the other column, where 2025 had seven campaigns and the single 2026 defect is the most basic failure in the book. A seat bracket is not software: it is two nuts, and someone tightened one. Both sentences are true at the same time.

What to do: Own a 2025 Polestar 3? Run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and check for campaign 26V037, Polestar's RP1079.[6] Only 213 American cars are in it, built between October 2024 and September 2025.[7] If the rear seatback feels loose or shifts when you push it, do not load cargo behind it until a dealer torques both nuts, free. For a 2021-2025 Polestar 2, your camera recall is 25V615, and you need it even if the earlier camera recalls were done. If you already own any Polestar, your warranty and service network stay open; the sales ban covers new-model-year sales from 2027, not the car in your driveway.

What this does not prove: A one-recall year does not mean Polestar builds safer cars than brands that recall more. Recall counts track filing behavior, fleet size, and how many new cars a brand is selling, and Polestar is selling down its last American stock. Our census covers filings from January 1 through November 15, 2026; a December filing moves the count. And the exit is trade policy, not safety: a seat bracket has nothing to do with the Commerce Department. Keep the two threads separate, even when they share a year.
