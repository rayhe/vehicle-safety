# Research: #826 — Dale Impactor III — "Utah's 0.05 Law Didn't Just Nab the 0.06 Drivers. It Scared the 0.16 Ones."

## Slug
`utah-005-bac-law-deters-heavy-drinkers`

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
The first rigorous US county-level evaluation of a 0.05 BAC limit (Li et al., AJPM, June 2026) found Utah's alcohol-related crash fatalities fell significantly after the 2018 law relative to six neighboring states, with non-alcohol fatalities unchanged — and the reductions extended across higher BAC levels, meaning the law deterred heavy drinkers, not just marginal ones. Meanwhile the other 49 states kept 0.08 while national alcohol-impaired deaths rose 25% over 2014-2023.

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? YES. The AJPM paper (embargo June 16, 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.amepre.2026.108380) is the first US county-level difference-in-differences evaluation of 0.05. NTSB-endorsed policy; Illinois HB 4333 (Feb 2026) would take Illinois to 0.05 — the first serious legislative push in years. Post-Labor Day timing (NHTSA Drive Sober campaign ended Sep 7) keeps impairment in the news cycle.

- Data-backed? YES. AJPM paper, AJPM press release, NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts 2024 (DOT HS 813815), NHTSA Report to Congress Feb 2026, Colorado State release, medicalxpress/scienmag summaries, saferoads.org IL HB 4333 letter.

## Key numbers for the piece
- 2024: 11,904 alcohol-impaired deaths (30% of US traffic deaths); 68% involved BAC >= 0.15; death every 44 minutes. (NHTSA DOT HS 813815)
- 2023: 12,429 alcohol-impaired deaths; $165B societal safety cost. (NHTSA Report to Congress)
- 2014-2023: alcohol-impaired share 28-32% every year; deaths +25%, fatality rate +15% over the decade. (AJPM release, Vaca)
- Utah law: passed 2017, effective Dec 30, 2018 (article says 2018). Utah remains the ONLY US state at 0.05.
- International research: 0.05 reduces alcohol-related crash injuries/fatalities by 11%+ (cited in AJPM release).
- Li et al.: alcohol-related crash fatalities declined in Utah significantly more than in the six contiguous states; non-alcohol fatalities did not show the same policy-related change (control-group falsification check); reductions spanned across BAC levels including high-BAC drivers.
- **Original arithmetic for the article**: 11% international effect x 11,904 (2024 alcohol-impaired deaths) ≈ 1,300 lives/year if the US replicated the international result; and the heaviest-drinker share: 0.68 x 11,904 = 8,097 deaths involved BAC >= 0.15 — the population the study says changed behavior. Vaca: each year of delay = "thousands of fatalities."

## Novel findings
1. **The deterrence reached the heavy drinkers.** The study's most important twist: reductions were not limited to drivers near the 0.05 threshold — they extended across higher BAC levels. In 2024, the most frequently recorded BAC among drinking drivers in fatal crashes was 0.15, and 68% of alcohol-impaired deaths involved BAC >= 0.15. A law aimed at the 0.05-0.08 margin changed the behavior of people triple the limit.
2. **The falsification check is the story's backbone.** Non-alcohol fatalities in Utah did not show the same decline — if the drop were general road-safety improvement (better cars, safer roads), both would move. This is the diff-in-diff doing its job.
3. **National arithmetic.** 11% x 11,904 = ~1,309 lives/year. Meanwhile 2014-2023 deaths rose 25% nationally while 49 states sat at 0.08. Only Illinois (HB 4333, Feb 2026) has a live bill.
4. **The 2016-vs-2019 subsample is a COVID firewall.** The diff-in-diff used only 2016 and 2019 crashes — pre-pandemic, so the 2020-2021 crash spike cannot contaminate the estimate.

## Limitations
- **Utah is the least-drunk state in America.** Lowest per-capita alcohol consumption of any state (large LDS population); 2023 alcohol-impaired share of fatalities was 21% (tied lowest nationally with Mississippi, vs 30% national). Generalizability to, say, Montana or Wisconsin is genuinely uncertain — the study authors don't claim Utah's effect size ports one-to-one.
- **FARS BACs are partly imputed.** NHTSA uses multiple imputation for missing BAC values (roughly half of drivers in fatal crashes aren't tested). The study inherits that uncertainty.
- **Diff-in-diff subsample is narrow.** The causal estimate rests on 2016 vs 2019 crashes only; full 2013-2023 panel was descriptive.
- **Enforcement publicity confound.** Utah's 2017-2018 rollout came with massive media coverage and a tourism-industry backlash; part of the effect may be publicity, not the statute number itself. (Honest caveat; also an argument FOR the law — deterrence is the mechanism.)
- **This article's national 1,309-lives math** applies the international 11% to US 2024 deaths — a back-of-envelope, not the paper's own national model. Labeled as such.

## Counterargument (strongest)
"The people killing others aren't the 0.06 social drinkers, so moving the limit is performative — you can't deter a 0.16 driver with a number change; you need interlocks and enforcement." Fair as far as it goes — and the site's own FARS data agrees the killers are mostly deep in the bottle. But Li et al. found the reductions extended across higher BAC levels, which is exactly the opposite of what the performative-law theory predicts. The counter-counter: the mechanism is probably publicity-plus-certainty (everyone heard about the law; everyone recalibrated "how many drinks is safe"). Also true: ignition interlocks remain the most proven intervention for convicted offenders — the 0.05 law and interlocks are complements, not substitutes.
