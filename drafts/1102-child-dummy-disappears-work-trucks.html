The Gap

IIHS Crash-Tested Work Trucks for the First Time. The Sensors Keep Losing the Kid.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 8, 2026

IIHS put work trucks through its crash-prevention tests for the first time this week, and the results read like an indictment of geometry. Across 22 test ratings covering 11 cargo vans and heavy-duty pickups, only three came back Good.[1] Not one of the four pickups earned a single Good in either test.

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds, because that is the window where everything goes wrong here. Automatic emergency braking runs on sensors mounted up near the rearview mirror, high and dry. That placement works beautifully on a Camry, but bolt it into a Class 3 pickup with a hood tall enough to hide a kindergartener, and you have built a system that cannot see over its own nose.

Good ratings across the first-ever IIHS commercial crash-prevention tests. Zero pickups earned one.

Watch the child dummy in the pedestrian tests, where several trucks, including the acceptable-rated Ford F-350 and Ram 3500, had a harder time detecting the child in daylight than detecting an adult at night. IIHS reports the child dummy was detected at a distance and then “disappeared” as the truck got closer.[1] Physics, not malice: the smaller dummy drops into the blind wedge between the sensor’s line of sight and the tall hood, exactly the place a real kid stands when they dart into the street in front of a work truck.

Motorcycles fared even worse, since several systems failed to issue a warning or slow at all in most motorcycle trials, which means a motorcycle in front of a Super Duty is apparently invisible to the computer designed to save it.[4] And then there are the two that did not even show up to the fight: the Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500 earned Poor in both tests because they have no automatic emergency braking at all. Forward collision warning is an option, in 2026, on a van you can buy brand new. GM should be embarrassed to still sell that.

None of this is a secret to the people writing the rules, which is the part that makes me furious. IIHS launched this commercial-vehicle program in June precisely because the federal standards that force AEB onto passenger cars mostly do not apply to vans, box trucks, and heavy pickups.[1] As IIHS research VP David Kidd told the Detroit Free Press, “when there are large crashes, those vehicles are usually doling out the harm.”[2] IIHS president David Harkey called the results “a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers.” NHTSA proposed AEB for heavy vehicles back in June 2023, and the safety advocates’ math, built on NHTSA’s own estimates, says the rule would save 155 lives and prevent 8,814 injuries a year. That rule is still not final.[2]

Model by model, for the record: the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 is the only vehicle to earn a Good in vehicle-to-vehicle prevention. Good in pedestrian prevention went to the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 and the Rivian RCV-Delivery 500, the new delivery-van class.[1] Pickups were a pileup: the Ford F-350 could only avoid the passenger-car target at 31 mph, and in a 43-mph test it failed to reduce its speed at all; the Chevy Silverado 3500HD failed all six vehicle-to-vehicle trials.[3] Every pickup landed at Acceptable or below.

To steelman the industry, and Mia always steelmans before she swings: stopping a 14,000-pound truck is a fundamentally harder problem than stopping a Camry, and IIHS graded these machines on the passenger-car rubric. Sure, the Express and Savana are legacy platforms on their way out, and the child-dummy scenario is a controlled edge case, not a daily occurrence, all fair, but the test is supposed to be hard, because the physics of a work truck hitting a child is harder, and the two vans that score zero are on dealer lots right now. Which brings me to the only part that matters.

If you run a fleet, here is your shopping list, straight from the ratings. Only one van earned a Good vehicle-to-vehicle rating: the Sprinter 2500. On pedestrian protection, the BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 lead the pack. And do not buy an Express 2500 or Savana 2500 for routes that run through neighborhoods or school zones, because those vans have no automatic braking, period, and the law does not require it. That gap, between what passenger cars must have and what work trucks get away without, is the whole story.

Last year, crashes involving heavy- and medium-duty vehicles or light vans killed 6,215 people in the United States, sixteen percent of every road death in the country.[1] We finally tested whether these machines can stop themselves, and mostly, they cannot. Hood too tall, sensor too high, regulation too far behind. That is a design choice someone keeps making, and today the receipts arrived.
