Daimler Is Recalling 2,404 Trucks Because the Repair Was Wrong Too. Welcome to the Recalled Recall.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 17, 2026

Let's talk about what happens when the truck brakes for you. Daimler Trucks North America just told NHTSA it is recalling 2,404 Freightliner Cascadia and Western Star 49X tractors because the brake modulator valves dealers installed during the previous round of recalls were the wrong hardware. The replacements corrode, the same corrosion the original recall was supposed to fix.[1]

2,404
Tractors recalled a second time because the first repair used the wrong valves, which corrode just like the ones they replaced

A modulator valve is the middleman of air brakes. A driver stomps the pedal, air pressure races down the lines, and the modulator meters how much pressure reaches each wheel end. When the valve corrodes, it can stick and dump full system pressure into one front wheel while the other gets the metered dose; that is brake pull. At 65 mph in a combination vehicle, one front wheel grabbing harder than its twin does not slow you down so much as it points you somewhere new.[5]

And the trigger is the part that should make your blood run cold. The pull does not happen under normal braking, which still works fine. It happens during braking initiated by the truck's own stability systems: Electronic Stability Control or Roll Stability Control. The one moment the truck intervenes to save you is the one moment the corrosion can steer you sideways.[1] Four years ago, the original recall (22V817, 200,000-plus trucks) described the same failure through the same hardware, painted ZF valves corroding during adaptive cruise control braking events. Dealers replaced those valves under 23V073 and 22V817. They installed the wrong ones, so the fix for corroded valves was valves that corrode. Two thousand four hundred trucks are back on the lift because the repair reproduced the defect it was repairing.[3]

Full honesty, because engineering deserves it. DTNA found this in its own internal process review in late August, before a single crash, injury, or unexpected brake pull was ever reported. Zero. The re-recall population is about one percent of the original campaign, owner letters go out November 8, and the fix is free.[2] This is arguably the system working: a manufacturer audits its own repairs, catches a one-percent hardware mixup, and owns it. It is also, simultaneously, a dealer network installing 2,404 wrong valves and not noticing for years.

What this article did not prove. I do not know the chemistry of the corrosion; the filings say "chemical corrosion" and leave it there. I do not know which part number got installed or how the mixup survived dealer inventory controls, because DTNA's public materials do not say. And that ABS malfunction light the company mentions? It signals ABS degradation, not valve corrosion; a valve can be compromised before the light ever fires. If you drive one of these tractors, the action is the same as always: run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls.[6]
