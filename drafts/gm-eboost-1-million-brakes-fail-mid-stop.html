GM Replaced a Mechanical Brake Booster With Software. It Failed 745 Times. Now 1.16 Million Vehicles Are Under Federal Investigation.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
August 25, 2026

For a century, pressing a brake pedal activated a vacuum-assisted booster that multiplied your foot's force against the rotor. Mechanical, redundant, boring. GM decided boring wasn't good enough and swapped the booster for an electronic motor called eBoost, built by Continental. When it works, engineers can tune brake feel across driving modes. When it doesn't, the pedal goes hard and you get a dashboard message: "Brake System Failure."

1,160,000
GM vehicles under NHTSA engineering analysis for eBoost brake failures (EA26006, opened Aug. 21, 2026)

NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened a preliminary evaluation in April after complaints piled up about the 2023 Cadillac Lyriq. Four months later, the agency escalated to an engineering analysis and expanded the scope to every GM vehicle running eBoost: the Colorado, Canyon, Blazer EV, Equinox EV, Traverse, Enclave, Envision, Acadia, Lyriq, Celestiq, Optiq, and even the abandoned Cruise Origin. Honda Prologue and Acura ZDX owners are included too, because GM built those vehicles.[1]

Across 1.16 million vehicles, NHTSA tallied 745 incidents. Twenty-one crashes. Five injury events. Six people hurt. Zero fatalities so far.[2]

GM told regulators the root cause is spindle fractures inside the eBoost unit and that when it happens, ABS and stability control stay functional until the vehicle reaches a complete stop. NHTSA's response: driver complaints describe something different. Owners report immediate loss of brake assist while actively braking, not a gradual fade after stopping. One 2025 Lyriq driver lost brakes pulling into a parking space and plowed through a storefront, coming to rest midway inside the building. A 2024 Blazer EV driver deliberately steered into a concrete curb to avoid what they called a "catastrophic intersection collision."[3]

An engineering analysis is the last investigative step before NHTSA can compel a recall. If the complaint data shows something structurally worse than what GM described, the math changes fast.[4]

Mechanical brake boosters failed too, but they stiffened gradually over days or weeks. An electronic system that drops brake assist mid-pedal-stroke is a different category of failure. GM engineered software into the space between your foot and the rotor, and 745 times, the software blinked first.

If you drive any of these vehicles, search your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. No recall has been issued yet, but the investigation is open. If you experience a hard brake pedal or see "Brake System Failure" on the dash, pull over. Do not assume the system will recover. Call your dealer and file a complaint at nhtsa.gov/report-a-safety-problem.
