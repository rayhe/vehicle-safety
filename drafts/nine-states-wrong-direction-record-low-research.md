# Research: Nine States Went the Wrong Direction While America Celebrated a Record Low

## Angle
NHTSA's 2025 early estimate shows 36,640 deaths — a 6.7% drop and the second-lowest rate (1.10/100M VMT) in FARS history. But 9 states saw MORE deaths in 2025 than 2024, and 2 stayed flat. The national average hides a growing geographic divergence.

## Kill Test
- Is this genuinely surprising? YES. NHTSA's press release touted "39 states improved." Nobody dissected the 9 that didn't. The magnitude of some increases (Hawaii +26.5%, Wyoming +15.9%) is startling.
- Original contribution? YES. Cross-referencing the 9 increasing states with their characteristics (rural %, speed limits, seat belt laws, population growth) to find what makes them diverge.
- Novel data cross-tabulation? YES. Combining NHTSA 2025 state estimates with Census rural/urban data and IIHS state law databases.

## What the 9 Increasing States Have in Common
- **Population growth:** Idaho (#1 fastest growing), Colorado (#10), New Hampshire (growth corridor). Fast-growing states add VMT before road infrastructure catches up.
- **Rural character:** Wyoming, Kansas, New Mexico, Idaho, Vermont — all significantly rural. Higher speed limits, longer response times, less infrastructure investment.
- **Speed limit profile:** Wyoming (80 mph highway), Idaho (80), Kansas (75), New Mexico (75), Colorado (75). Higher speed limits correlate with higher severity.
- **Seat belt enforcement:** New Hampshire is the ONLY state with no adult seat belt law. Wyoming has secondary enforcement only.

## Original Calculation
Hawaii's rate went from 0.97 to 1.22 — a 25.8% rate increase. It crossed above the national average (1.10) for the first time in the dataset period. An island state with a 60-mph speed limit and 25-mph urban zones now kills at a higher rate per VMT than the national average.

Wyoming's rate went from 1.10 (exactly the national avg) to 1.26. It jumped from "average" to 15% above national.

Combined, the 9 states added ~309 more deaths (from ~2,829 to ~3,138). The other 41 states + DC + PR dropped by ~2,923. The national improvement required 41 states' gains to overwhelm 9 states' losses.

## Limitations
- 2025 numbers are statistical PROJECTIONS, not final FARS counts. Subject to revision.
- Small states (Vermont, Wyoming, Hawaii) have high variance — a few multi-fatality crashes can swing percentages dramatically.
- State-level estimates use regional inflation rates applied proportionally — not independent counts.
- Cannot determine causation from these correlations (speed limits, rural %, growth).

## Strongest Counterargument
Small-number states are noisy. Hawaii going from 102 to 129 is +27 deaths. On an island of 1.4 million people, a handful of multi-fatality crashes could account for the entire increase. Statistical noise, not a trend. The honest response: true, but that argument applies to the decreases too. Rhode Island going from 52 to 37 is -15 deaths. If we celebrate the decreases as meaningful, we have to take the increases seriously. And New Mexico (+49 deaths) and Colorado (+26) and Louisiana (+21) are not small-number artifacts.
