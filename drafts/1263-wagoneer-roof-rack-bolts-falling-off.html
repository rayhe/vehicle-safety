The Gap

The Roof Rack Bolts on 97,349 Wagoneers Were Never Tightened Right. The Driver Behind You Pays for It.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
December 27, 2027

Before you sign anything on a big three-row SUV, check the roof, not the sunroof and not the headliner, but the crossbars. On 97,349 Jeep Wagoneer and Grand Wagoneer SUVs, the pivot bolts holding those crossbars were never tightened to the proper specification, and FCA's own filing says a crossbar that comes off at speed becomes a hazard for everybody else on the road. NHTSA campaign 26V584, filed September 10, 2026 under FCA recall 81D, puts the estimated defect rate at 100 percent of the recalled population. Every single truck in the recall carries the defect.[1]

100%
Filing estimate of recalled vehicles carrying under-torqued roof rack pivot bolts: all 97,349 of them

Read that number twice, because it is not the number recalls usually carry. Most filings estimate some fraction of the population actually has the defect, a bad batch inside a big build window, a supplier slip on certain shifts; this one estimates all of them. Wagoneer accounts for the majority, 54,249 trucks or 55.7 percent of the population, followed by 22,144 Grand Wagoneers, 11,606 Wagoneer L models, and 9,350 Grand Wagoneer L models. Add those four numbers and you get 97,349 exactly, which means FCA is not guessing at the edges of this population. It knows which trucks got these crossbars, and it is saying all of them got bolts that were short of spec.[2]

97,349
Wagoneer and Grand Wagoneer SUVs recalled, model years 2022 through 2026, built February 2021 through October 2025

Now read FCA's own risk statement, because it names the victim and it is not the Wagoneer owner. "Crossbars which detach from a vehicle may create a road hazard to operators and occupants of other vehicles, and can cause such vehicles to crash without prior warning." No warning, and none for the person it hits. A Wagoneer driver might hear a rattle, might notice a bolt missing, might see a crossbar coming apart at the pivot joint, and those are FCA's own listed warning signs. The driver behind gets a metal bar at highway speed with no rattle, no light, no chance to react to anything except the object itself.[3] That asymmetry is the whole story. Most recalls ask an owner to protect themselves. This one asks an owner to protect strangers, and the strangers cannot check a VIN to opt out.

Fairness requires the counterweight, and it is a real one. A 100 percent defect estimate does not mean 100 percent of crossbars will detach. Under-torque is a spectrum, not a switch: a bolt tightened to most of spec may hold for the life of the truck, and only bolts far enough short of spec will work loose under years of vibration, expansion joints, and crosswinds. Against 97,349 trucks, FCA had identified 15 warranty claims worldwide that may be related as of August 31, 2026, which is 0.015 percent of the population, and no crashes or injuries were reported in relation to this issue.[4] If you own one of these SUVs, the most likely outcome by far is that nothing ever happens. But "most likely nothing happens" is a strange thing to say about a part whose entire job is staying attached at 70 miles per hour, and it is no comfort at all to the driver in the lane behind the one truck where the bolt finally backs out.

Credit where it is due: the fix here is not a re-torque. FCA's remedy replaces the pivot bolts with bolts of a different design, free of charge, which is the honest response to a torque failure. A bolt that was never tightened right the first time does not become trustworthy because a technician tightens it a second time; if the joint design let an under-torqued bolt pass through assembly on 97,349 trucks, the joint needed changing, not just a wrench. Contrast that with the pattern this site keeps documenting, where a recall remedy fails and the same defect comes back for a second or third campaign. Here, at least, the manufacturer is not asking owners to trust the same hardware twice. Interim owner letters went out October 8, 2026, with a second notice to follow once the final remedy is available, and VINs have been searchable since September 17, 2026.[5]

What this recall really exposes is a gap in how we think about car safety. Ratings, crash tests, and recall notices all frame risk as something that happens to the buyer. Airbags protect the buyer, and so do brakes. Even the much-covered defect investigations of the last two years mostly end with the owner's car catching fire or losing control. A roof crossbar is different: it turns a flagship family SUV into a hazard for people who never bought it, never drove it, and never got a letter about it. Nobody shopping for a Wagoneer test-drives the car behind them. Regulators do not crash-test what a car drops onto the highway. Owners are told to listen for a rattle, which is another way of saying the safety system here is a human ear at 70 miles per hour.

What to actually do with this

If you own a 2022-2026 Wagoneer or Grand Wagoneer, do not wait for a second letter. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, where these VINs have been searchable since September 17, 2026, and call FCA at 1-800-853-1403 with recall 81D if yours is listed. Until the redesigned bolts are in, inspect the crossbar pivot joints yourself and treat any rattle from the roof as a stop-and-look signal, not background noise: a rattle is the only warning this defect gives, and it is warning you on behalf of the driver behind you. Do not load the crossbars with cargo until the remedy is done; extra load on an under-torqued joint is exactly the wrong experiment. Shopping a used Wagoneer? Get the 81D remedy confirmed in writing before you sign, not just a clean inspection report. And if you are the driver behind any big SUV with roof gear, the honest advice is less satisfying: you cannot check anyone else's bolts, so give roof-rack-equipped trucks a little extra following distance on the highway, the same way you would a truck hauling an unsecured load.
