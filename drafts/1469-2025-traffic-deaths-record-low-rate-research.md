## Story in one line
NHTSA announced in April 2026 that traffic deaths fell to an estimated 36,640 in 2025, down 6.7 percent, with the second-lowest fatality rate ever recorded — and the same release quietly confirms the 2024 final count of 39,254. The trend is real. It is also still about 100 people a day, and the 2025 estimate remains above the pre-pandemic 2019 total.

## Key verified facts
- **2025 early estimate:** 36,640 traffic fatalities, a 6.7% decrease from 2024. Fatality rate 1.10 per 100 million VMT — the second-lowest rate in recorded history. VMT rose about 0.9% (+29.8 billion miles). Deaths fell in 39 states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. (Source 1)
- **2024 FARS (Annual Report File):** 39,254 people killed, down 4.3% from 41,025 in 2023 (1,771 fewer). Rate 1.19 per 100M VMT, down 5.6% from 1.26. Third consecutive year-to-year decrease in both fatalities and rate since 2021. 36,297 fatal crashes (down 3.9%). 2.42 million injured (down 0.8%, not statistically significant). 6.18 million police-reported crashes. VMT 3,294 billion miles, up 1.5%. (Source 2)
- **The pandemic arc:** 2021 was the peak at 42,939 deaths (FARS final file). 2024's 39,254 is 3,685 below that peak; the 2025 estimate (36,640) would be 6,299 below it — more than half the spike unwound. 2019, the last pre-pandemic year, was 36,096 deaths. The 2025 estimate is still 544 above 2019. (Sources 2, 3)
- **2024 categories that fell:** passenger car occupant deaths −1,153 (−9.7%); unrestrained occupant deaths −775 (−7.4%); ejected occupants −537 (−11%); speeding-related −625 (−5.2%); alcohol-impaired-driving −478 (−3.9%, to 11,904); pedestrian deaths −287 (−3.9%, to 7,080); motorcyclist deaths −138 (−2.2%, to 6,228); young-driver (15–20) fatalities −370 (−6.6%); distraction-affected −75 (−2.3%); hit-and-run −153 (−5.3%). (Source 2)
- **2024 categories that rose:** fatalities in crashes involving older drivers (65+) +115 (+1.4%); SUV occupant fatalities +112 (+1.6%). Only two categories went up. (Source 2)
- **The injury paradox:** while deaths fell in 2024, injuries rose significantly for motorcyclists (+5.2%) and nonoccupants — pedestrians injured +5.0%, pedalcyclists +5.8%. Fewer people died; more people got hurt and survived. (Source 2)
- **Distracted driving (press release):** 18 people injured every half hour in distracted-driving crashes; one person killed about every 2.5 hours. (Source 1)
- **Who died in 2024:** vehicle occupants 62% of the 39,254; pedestrians 18% (7,080); motorcyclists 16% (6,228); cyclists 2.8% (1,103). (Source 2, via FARS)
- **"Second-lowest rate ever" context:** the annual rate 1.10 in 2025 trails only the lowest annual rate on record. The Q1 2026 quarterly rate of 0.99 (covered in #814/#856) is a different, quarterly figure — do not conflate it with the annual record.

## Original contribution (Axle's math and synthesis)
1. **The rate-vs-count gap.** The press release leads with the rate: 1.10, second-lowest ever. Rates are honest — they divide deaths by miles driven, and miles rose. But the count is what the morgue measures: 36,640 estimated dead, about 100 a day, and still above 2019's 36,096. A rate can set records while the count stays above the last normal year, because VMT keeps growing. Both numbers are true; only one of them makes a good headline.
2. **The unwinding, quantified.** From the 2021 peak (42,939) to the 2025 estimate (36,640) is a 14.7% decline over four years — 6,299 fewer deaths a year. That is the whole pandemic spike more than half reversed. The 2024 file alone accounts for 1,771 of it. The decline is broad-based (39 states, nearly every category) rather than a single-state or single-cause artifact.
3. **The two exceptions are the next story.** Older drivers and SUV occupants were the only fatality categories that rose in 2024. An aging driving population and a fleet that keeps getting taller and heavier are structural, not cyclical — the two trends that do not reverse on their own.
4. **Deaths down, injuries up — for the people outside the car.** Pedestrian deaths fell 3.9% but pedestrian injuries rose 5.0%; motorcyclist deaths fell 2.2% but injuries rose 5.2%. The gap between dying and getting hurt is widening for the most vulnerable road users. Possible readings: better trauma care, safer speeds in the crashes that do happen, or more low-speed collisions in denser traffic. The data does not pick one. Do not claim it does.
5. **The estimate caveat, stated plainly.** 36,640 is an early estimate. FARS finalizes a year later; the 2023 total was revised upward from the early estimate (40,901 → 41,025 final). The 2025 number will move. The direction of the revision is not guaranteed.

## Strongest counterargument
The good news is real and should not be buried under the "still 100 a day" framing. Three straight years of decline, deaths down in 39 states, nearly every category improving, and the rate at its second-lowest ever while Americans drove more miles — that is a genuine public-health improvement, not a statistical artifact. The pandemic spike is more than half unwound. Passenger-car occupant deaths fell nearly 10% in a single year. Unrestrained deaths fell 7.4%. The "still above 2019" comparison is fair, but 2019 was itself a bad year by historical standards — 36,096 deaths was not some golden age. And the estimate is preliminary: if the final file revises down, the 2025 count could land below 2019 after all. The honest headline is that the roads are getting safer and are still dangerous; both clauses matter.

## Limitations
- The 2025 figure (36,640) is an early estimate, not a FARS count; it will be revised when the 2025 Annual Report File lands (expected spring 2027). The 2024 figure (39,254) is the Annual Report File, also subject to revision when finalized.
- 2019 (36,096) and 2021 (42,939) totals are FARS final-file counts via the FARS Encyclopedia trends table.
- Injury estimates come from CRSS, a sample, not a census; the research note flags which injury changes are statistically significant.
- Rate comparisons across years use FHWA VMT estimates, which are themselves revised.
- The press release's "second-lowest rate in recorded history" is NHTSA's characterization of the annual rate; do not present the Q1 2026 quarterly 0.99 rate as the annual record.
