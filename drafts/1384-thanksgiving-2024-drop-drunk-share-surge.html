Thanksgiving Got Safer. Its Drivers Got Drunker.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 3, 2026

Thanksgiving travel just pulled off a statistical magic trick. Total deaths during the holiday period fell from 490 in 2023 to 344 in 2024, a drop of nearly 30 percent, the sharpest single-year improvement in a five-year series compiled from federal fatality data.[1] Drunk-driving deaths fell from 174 to 164 over the same year, a drop of ten. Subtract one column from the other and the trick dissolves: 136 of the 146 fewer deaths came from crashes with no drunk driver involved, while the drunk count barely moved at all.

47.7%
Share of 2024 Thanksgiving deaths tied to drunk driving, up from 35.5% in 2023 and the highest in the five-year series

For four years, alcohol's share of the Thanksgiving toll sat in a narrow band: 35.9 percent in 2020, 35.8 percent in 2021, 34.8 percent in 2022, 35.5 percent in 2023. Then 2024 broke the band by twelve points. Speeding moved the same direction, from 36.5 percent of deaths in 2023 to 38.4 percent in 2024, and young drivers aged 15 to 20 rose from 17.8 percent to 19.8 percent. Put those three shifts together and a clear picture forms: Thanksgiving 2024 did not get safer in general. It got safer for sober, law-abiding, adult drivers, while every risky subgroup held its ground or gained share.[1]

NHTSA's own tally points the same way, with a different window and a different total. Across Thanksgiving holiday periods from 2020 through 2024, the agency counts 910 people killed in alcohol-impaired crashes, or 36 percent of all fatalities in those periods.[2] Zutobi's five-year drunk total from the same underlying federal data is 897, close enough that the small gap reads as definitional noise rather than disagreement. Both counts agree on the point that matters: at Thanksgiving, alcohol kills at a rate well above its roughly 30 percent share of annual traffic deaths. NHTSA clearly believes the trend is live, not historical, since its Thanksgiving impaired-driving campaign runs November 23 to 29 this year.[2]

Before anyone prints a good-news headline, read the rest of the report. Christmas deaths in the same series fell 61 percent in 2024, from 389 to 153. Two holidays do not independently get 30 and 61 percent safer in one year, which means the 2024 counts are probably incomplete: federal fatality files take time to finalize, and late-reported crashes tend to be the routine ones. That caveat cuts against the level of the drop, and it cuts less hard against the share shift, because inflating the share by twelve points would require the missing crashes to be skewed sharply toward sober drivers. Still, a share built on provisional counts deserves a provisional label, and this article gives it one.[1]

What this analysis does not prove: nothing here explains why non-drunk deaths fell, since weather, travel volume, enforcement, and reporting all vary and none are measured in the cited tables. Nothing here claims the 344 is final; if 2024 counts revise upward, the drop shrinks and the share may shrink with it. Nothing here contradicts the earlier Crash Report finding that the deadliest impaired nights of Thanksgiving week are Wednesday and Saturday, which came from day-level data this holiday-level series cannot reproduce.[4]

Strongest counterargument. A skeptic could fairly call the entire story a data artifact: provisional 2024 reporting, a driver-education company's compiled tables, and a share computed on counts that may yet move. Fair, and worth sitting with, because the Christmas plunge in the same report is exactly what an artifact looks like. But the artifact theory needs a second, stronger assumption to kill the share finding, namely that missing crashes were overwhelmingly sober ones, and no evidence in the report supports that skew. Meanwhile NHTSA's independent 36 percent figure already establishes that impairment is over-represented at Thanksgiving without touching Zutobi's tables at all. Treat the 30 percent drop as unproven and the 48 percent share as the number to watch, and the story survives its own harshest critic.

Do not let a "Thanksgiving got safer" headline change your plan, because the deaths that disappeared were the sober ones. Set the sober ride before the first drink, and set it for Wednesday night and Saturday night both, since those are the nights where reunion drinking turns into driving. Drive home in daylight when you can, buckle up without exception, and check your vehicle for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls before the trip, since a holiday weekend is the worst time to discover one.[2][3]
