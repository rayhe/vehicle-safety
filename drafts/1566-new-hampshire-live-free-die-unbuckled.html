Live Free or Die Unbuckled: New Hampshire's Seat Belt Exception Has a Body Count

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 22, 2026

In New Hampshire, buckling up is a suggestion. Adults face no law, no fine, no consequence for riding loose in the front seat. Seven years of federal crash data now price that suggestion. When a fatal crash happens inside New Hampshire, it is 2.8 times more likely to kill someone who was not wearing a seat belt.[1] That is not a rounding error. That is the whole experiment, measured.

2.8x
Higher odds that a fatal crash in New Hampshire kills an unrestrained occupant, adjusted for demographics, environment, vehicle, and driver factors, FARS 2017–2023

Surgeons at Duke, Dartmouth Hitchcock, and Eastern Virginia Medical School ran the numbers and published them in July in the Journal of Trauma and Acute Care Surgery. They pulled every fatal crash in America from 2017 through 2023, all 146,655 of them, and sorted by where the crash happened and what license the driver carried. Inside New Hampshire, 71.5 percent of fatal crashes killed someone unrestrained. For occupants holding a New Hampshire license, anywhere in the country, the figure was 68.7 percent.[1]

Read that second number again. It is the finding that should end the argument. New Hampshire drivers who crash outside their home state are still 2.06 times more likely to die unrestrained than drivers from anywhere else.[1] A missing law is not a local quirk. It is a habit, formed on roads where nobody enforces the belt, and carried across every state line the driver crosses. Vermont, Maine, and Massachusetts all require belts. Their roads do not re-teach a New Hampshire driver in the ten minutes it takes to cross the border.

NHTSA's fresh 2024 state tables, published in October, confirm the pattern is not fading. New Hampshire's observed belt use was 78.2 percent last year, against 91.2 percent nationally.[2][3] Of the 77 passenger-vehicle occupants killed in the state, 39 were unrestrained, and among deaths where restraint use was known, 59 percent were unbelted, against 48 percent for the country as a whole.[2] Nationally, 9,758 unrestrained occupants died in 2024. Nearly half of everyone killed in a passenger vehicle, in a country where nine in ten people buckle up, died without the belt on.[2]

Look at Wyoming in that table before you crown New Hampshire the worst. Wyoming has a belt law, secondary enforcement only, and it beats New Hampshire on both bad numbers: 73.4 percent use, 61 percent of deaths unrestrained.[2][3] A law with no teeth and no law at all rhyme. What actually separates the safe states from the deadly ones is enforcement with consequences. Among jurisdictions with primary enforcement, where an officer can stop a car for the belt alone, 68 percent clear 90 percent belt use. Among secondary-enforcement and no-law jurisdictions, 7 percent do.[3] Sixty-one points of difference, riding on one line of statute.

Fairness demands the other side get its say. New Hampshire voters have rejected belt bills again and again, most recently 206 to 162 in the House in 2023, and "Live Free or Die" is a considered position about bodily autonomy, not ignorance.[5] The state's overall death rate, 0.96 per 100 million miles in 2024, beats the national rate of 1.19, so the experiment is not producing a statewide bloodbath.[6][7] And 39 unrestrained deaths is a small number in a state of 1.4 million people.

All true. None of it answers the reply. An unrestrained occupant in a crash becomes a projectile that injures the belted people around them, and the trauma care for the unbelted is paid socially, through hospitals and insurance pools, not by the person who chose the risk. Seat belts cut the risk of fatal injury by about 45 percent for front-seat occupants.[4] Freedom includes the freedom to make a bad bet with your own body. It has never included the freedom to bill everyone else for the outcome.

What the data cannot prove matters too. This is an observational study, Level IV evidence. It shows a strong association after adjusting for the confounders the authors could measure, not proof that the missing statute caused every excess death. New Hampshire is rural, its fleet is old, and its numbers are small: 474 fatal crashes over seven years, 77 occupant deaths in 2024. Restraint use was unknown in 11 percent of 2024 deaths nationally, 14 percent in New Hampshire. Observational belt-use surveys only measure daytime front-seat use. Treat the 2.8 as a well-measured association with a plausible mechanism, not a laboratory result.

So here is the practical version. Buckle up in every state, including the one that does not require it. If you drive in New Hampshire regularly, treat the missing law as a missing guardrail, not a permission slip. When you buy a used car, check that the belt reminder works in every seating position: in 2024, 58 percent of second-row occupants killed were unrestrained.[2] And parents should know the adult exemption stops at age 18. New Hampshire's child-passenger law carries primary enforcement for everyone under 18.[4] Your kids are covered by a law. You are covered by a choice. Make the same one the law would make for you.
