# Research: The Extinct Trucks Are Still Killing

## Angle
The Chevrolet S-10 was discontinued in 2004 — 22 years ago. Its FARS death rate (4.83 per 100M VMT) is **17 times higher** than its replacement, the Chevrolet Colorado (0.28). Same manufacturer, same segment, same factory. 1,427 people died in S-10 fatal crashes during 2014-2023. The truck hasn't been made in two decades, and it's still one of the deadliest vehicles on American roads.

This isn't unique to the S-10. An entire generation of compact pickups — the S-10, Ford Ranger (pre-2019), Dodge Dakota, GMC Sonoma — was discontinued in the mid-2000s. Their replacements are dramatically safer. But the extinct trucks are still out there, aging into their deadliest years.

**Key finding:** The S-10-to-Colorado transition shows a 94.2% reduction in death rate (4.83 → 0.28). Same company, same segment, same factory lineage.

### S-10 Model Year Death Distribution
Peak deaths: 2000 model year (178 deaths), followed by 2003 (149), 1998 (143). Deaths concentrated in 1997-2003 model years, meaning during the FARS 2014-2023 window, these trucks were 11-26 years old.

### What Made the S-10 So Deadly?
1. **No ESC** — Electronic Stability Control wasn't mandated until 2012. S-10 production ended 8 years before that.
2. **Pre-modern crash structures** — The S-10's body-on-frame design with minimal crumple zone technology
3. **No side airbags** — Standard equipment on most modern trucks
4. **Lightweight chassis** — Small enough to lose every weight-class collision
5. **High center of gravity** — Pickup geometry + short wheelbase = rollover risk

### The Fleet Age Problem
NHTSA estimates the average age of vehicles on the road reached 12.6 years in 2024. That means the "average" car was built in 2012. But the S-10's peak death model years (1997-2003) represent vehicles that were 21-26 years old during the FARS window. These are trucks that have survived past the fleet average because pickups are durable — they last longer, which means they kill longer.

### Original Contribution
Cross-tabulating extinct vs. current models within the same manufacturer's lineup reveals a "generational safety dividend" that has never been quantified model-by-model. The S-10→Colorado transition is the single largest same-segment, same-manufacturer improvement in the entire FARS dataset: 17x. The Dakota→no-successor transition removed 1,237 deaths from the FARS window with no replacement deaths to offset. The Ranger gap (2011-2019) similarly removed a high-death vehicle from production.

## Strongest Counterargument
The rate comparison is distorted by fleet composition bias. S-10s still on the road in 2014-2023 are likely:
- Older, with more accumulated wear
- Owned by lower-income drivers who may defer maintenance
- Driven in rural areas with higher speed limits and less emergency response access
- Self-selected survivors (the ones that haven't been scrapped are the ones still being driven hard)

The Colorado's low rate partly reflects that it's newer, maintained better, and driven by different demographics. The 17x gap overstates the pure engineering improvement because it conflates vehicle age effects with design improvement.

However: even controlling for these factors, the ESC mandate alone accounts for a ~50% reduction in single-vehicle fatalities (IIHS, 2011). Side airbags add another ~30% reduction in near-side fatalities. The S-10 had neither. The engineering improvement is real even if the 17x headline number includes fleet-age effects.
