Uber Spent Four Years Trying to Bury a Fatal Crash in Arbitration. Illinois' Highest Court Just Dug It Up.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 28, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your next rideshare: the terms of service you tapped through without reading contain an arbitration clause, and until last Thursday, Uber believed that clause could follow you past death and bind your widow. In April 2022, Mark Geller of Chicago climbed into an Uber headed for Midway Airport. The driver, Ejaz Rathore, lost control on the Stevenson Expressway, and both men were killed.[3] Gloria Sheridan Geller, Mark's widow, sued in Cook County as administrator of his estate. What followed was not a trial. It was a four-and-a-half-year war over whether she would ever see the inside of a courtroom.

53
months from fatal crash to a ruling that the case could be heard in open court

On September 24, 2026, the Illinois Supreme Court ended it unanimously. In Geller v. Uber Technologies, Inc., 2026 IL 132066, the court held that Gloria's wrongful-death claim could not be compelled to arbitration under either of two separate Uber agreements.[1] Mark's agreement bound Mark, a contracting party; it did not bind Gloria, a non-party survivor pressing an independent statutory claim. And Gloria's own agreement, which Uber invoked as its backup theory, covered claims connected with her own use of her own app, not her husband's fatal ride.[2] The court's summary of the whole doctrine fit in one sentence: "Arbitration is a matter of consent, and Uber has failed to establish that the estate or the statutory beneficiary consented to arbitrate this pending wrongful death dispute."[5]

April 2022 to September 2026 is 53 months, and the path between those dates is the story. Circuit judges split the case down the middle: the estate's survival claims went to arbitration under Mark's agreement, while the wrongful-death claims stayed in court because Gloria's agreement did not reach them.[1] Rather than arbitrate, the estate voluntarily dismissed the survival claims, sacrificing an entire category of damages to escape the private forum. An appellate court reversed anyway, ruling that a delegation clause meant an arbitrator, not a judge, should decide whether the claims were arbitrable. Illinois' highest court reversed that. A grieving family gave up claims to avoid a private forum, then spent years litigating the forum itself, and won the right to a public trial 53 months after the crash.

You may reasonably ask what contract law is doing in a vehicle-safety publication. Here is the answer: confidential arbitration produces no public docket, no trackable schedule, and no public verdict benchmark. Every fatal crash routed into it is a crash the public record never sees. No published findings, no citable outcome, no data point for anyone studying how rideshare crashes happen. It is the same information problem as a fully redacted federal crash filing, executed with a terms-of-service checkbox instead of a black marker. Geller converts future Illinois rideshare wrongful-death cases from sealed to trackable, and hands plaintiff lawyers in every other state a high-court opinion to cite when the same motion to compel lands on their desk.[4]

Now the part where I argue with myself, because the strongest objection deserves full strength. Arbitration is not inherently a cover-up. It is faster and cheaper than court, many plaintiffs prefer it, and Uber's position was facially reasonable: both spouses had signed the same terms containing the same clause. The ruling binds only Illinois, and in states that treat wrongful death as derivative of the decedent's claim rather than independent, a survivor can still be swept into the decedent's arbitration agreement. Note as well that arbitration captured part of this very case: the survival claims went to arbitration under Mark's agreement and were dismissed, never litigated. Geller narrows the shield. It does not shatter it.

If someone you love is killed in a rideshare, do not assume the app's arbitration clause binds the survivors, and do not agree to arbitrate before talking to a lawyer. In Illinois the highest court has now said a non-signing survivor's independent wrongful-death claim is not compelled by the decedent's agreement, and that reasoning carries persuasive weight everywhere Uber's materially similar terms operate.[4] The forum is the fight. Gloria Sheridan Geller just proved it can be won, 53 months after the crash that started it.
