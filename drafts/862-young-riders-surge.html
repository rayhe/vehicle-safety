Motorcycle Deaths Fell 2.2% in 2024. Young Riders Went the Wrong Way.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

Some good news, briefly. NHTSA's motorcycle fact sheet for 2024, published in July, says rider deaths fell 2.2 percent last year, from 6,366 to 6,228.[1] Then you scroll to the age table and the good news ends. Motorcycle deaths among children under 15 more than doubled, from 14 to 30. A 114 percent jump in one year.[1]

+114%
Change in motorcycle deaths among children under 15, 2023 to 2024

Now do the math nobody in the coverage of this report bothered with. Riders under 25: 1,135 dead in 2023, 1,344 in 2024, which is 209 more young bodies, an 18.4 percent increase. Riders 25 and older went the other direction: 5,231 dead in 2023, 4,884 in 2024, a drop of 347. So the entire national decline, the whole headline, was produced by older riders while everyone under 25 went the other way. One demographic supplied the victory lap; another supplied the wreckage.[1]

Speed explains some of it, and NHTSA's own table names the culprit. Among 15-to-20-year-old riders in fatal crashes last year, 56 percent were speeding, the highest speeding involvement of any age group on the road, up from 51 percent the year before. The next tier, ages 21 to 24, sat at 52 percent. For context, passenger car drivers in fatal crashes speed at 22 percent, and the all-motorcycle average is 37 percent. Teenage riders are not just crashing more; they are crashing fast.[1]

Plenty of them should not have been on a bike at all. Thirty-five percent of riders in fatal crashes in 2024 held no valid motorcycle license, against 14 percent of passenger-vehicle drivers.[1] And the deadliest daypart split sits exactly where you would guess: the 15-to-20 surge ran +20 percent on weekdays and +19 percent on weekends, which means this is not a Saturday-night problem but an every-day one.

Two honesty notes before anyone quotes the 114 percent figure at a dinner party. First, the under-15 count is tiny: 30 deaths. A couple of multi-fatality incidents swing a percentage that large, and the category sweeps in minibikes, mopeds, and off-road machines ridden on public roads, not just highway bikes. Second, speeding involvement comes from police reports, which undercount by nature, and NHTSA publishes no age-stratified mileage data. Nobody can prove from this table that young riders are more dangerous per mile. That matters.

Because the strongest counterargument runs through exposure, and it deserves the full paragraph. If riders under 25 simply logged far more miles in 2024, through cheap used bikes, delivery gigs, and stunt content filmed for phones, then rising body counts reflect more riding, not more reckless riding, a question the fact sheet cannot settle. What it does settle is this: whatever drove the national decline, it did not touch anyone under 25, and the 56 percent speeding figure among dead teenagers is not an exposure artifact, because nobody speeds by accident.

So what do you do with a fact sheet like this? If there is a young rider in your house, the Motorcycle Safety Foundation Basic RiderCourse is the floor, not the ceiling, and "no license, no bike" should be a house rule with no appeal process.[4] Graduated licensing works when it is actually enforced, and the unlicensed 35 percent suggests it often is not. On helmets, the split is blunt: in states without universal helmet laws, 48 percent of killed riders were unhelmeted, versus 11 percent in states that require helmets for all.[1] Only 17 states plus DC have universal laws. The rest have decided, through their legislatures, what 37 percentage points of helmeted bodies is worth.
