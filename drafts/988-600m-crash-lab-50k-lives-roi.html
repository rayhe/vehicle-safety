IIHS Spent $600 Million Smashing Cars. It Got Back $538 Billion.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 25, 2026

Somewhere in Virginia there is a building whose entire job is destroying brand-new cars, and it just published the receipts. To mark 30 years of crash testing, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety ran the numbers on its own program and found that vehicle improvements driven by its ratings saved an estimated 48,352 lives between 1999 and 2024.[1] That is roughly 1,860 people a year, every year, for a generation.

900-to-1
Societal value returned per dollar IIHS spent on 30 years of crash testing

Before anyone accuses the lab of grading its own homework, and we will get to that, look at the demo they staged for the anniversary. Engineers crashed a 1996 Chevrolet Blazer head-on into a 2026 Blazer in a moderate-overlap test, and the new truck's cabin stayed intact. The old one's collapsed so completely that the dashboard and steering column shoved into the dummy's lap while the fully inflated airbag snapped its head back toward the window.[2] No model can argue with that footage, because physics does not do press releases.

Now the money, because the money is the part nobody else bothered to compute. Insurers funded the whole program to the tune of about $600 million over three decades.[3] IIHS values the averted deaths at $538 billion in societal savings. Divide one by the other and you get a return of roughly 900 to 1. Divide the spending by the lives and you get about $12,400 in program cost per life saved. Find me a better-performing investment in the history of the auto industry; I will wait.

Here is the twist the anniversary coverage mostly skipped: the test that did the heaviest lifting is the oldest one they have. Most of the saved lives trace back to improvements in the moderate overlap front test, the program's original 1995 evaluation.[4] The newer headline tests, small overlap and the updated side impact, contributed less in absolute terms. The first hole they patched was the biggest leak, which makes sense when you think about it. Everything since has been chasing smaller and smaller margins, which is exactly what a maturing safety program looks like.

All right, the homework-grading objection, stated at full strength because it deserves it. Fatality rates fell for a dozen reasons across those 26 years: the federal stability-control mandate, curtain airbags, better trauma care, seatbelt use climbing from around 70 percent to 92 percent[6]. Comparing Good-rated cars against worse-rated ones in the real world cannot perfectly isolate the test program from that rising tide, and some of those 48,352 lives belong to other forces. The $538 billion is built on the statistical value of a life, an economic construct, not cash anyone received. And yes, the institute that ran the tests also ran the study.

Two things blunt that objection without killing it: first, the comparison was within-era and within vehicle class, which washes out much of the tide, and second, the Blazer footage. You can argue about attribution models all day. You cannot argue with a crushed cabin next to an intact one.

The question is what you actually do with this: if you are shopping used, pull the rating history at iihs.org/ratings[5] and check the test date, not just the word Good. A 2010 Good predates the small-overlap test entirely, and a small-overlap Good, a test introduced in 2012, is the cheapest meaningful safety filter in the used market. It separates structures engineered this decade from structures engineered last decade. Thirty years of smashed cars bought you that information. Use it.
