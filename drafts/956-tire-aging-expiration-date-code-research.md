# Research: 956-tire-aging-expiration-date-code

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
Tires have an expiration date that nobody prints as a date: it is a four-digit week code molded into the sidewall. NHTSA's own estimate of the aging-tire death toll (90 dead, 3,200 injured a year) is built on 2005-2006 crash data and has never been refreshed; the NTSB told the agency in 2015 to determine the current risk, and eleven years later the official number is still frozen while the fleet aged to a record 12.8 years.

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy: YES. The federal government has not updated its aging-tire body count in two decades, and the 2014 decision not to regulate rested on oven tests of NEW tires while the NTSB's own 2014 case files show 10- and 12-year-old tires failing in fatal crashes. A fresh 2026 peg: NHTSA recall 26T008 (Feb 2026, General Rubber Thailand Xcellent Roadbreaker A/T) failed FMVSS 139 with the exact failure signature of aging — tread-shoulder cracking, inner-liner separation — identified by DOT week code 4324, the same four-digit code consumers are supposed to use to read their tires' expiration.
- Not a data dump: original calculations — (a) the frozen-estimate arithmetic: 2005-2006 data published 2014, no refresh in 20 years, NTSB H-15-32 recommendation (2015) unfulfilled; (b) the fleet-age collision: average vehicle age 12.8 years (S&P Global Mobility 2025, record) vs. 6-year tire service life, meaning the average car is on its second set but low-mileage garage cars run expired originals; (c) the 23% figure: NHTSA's own weighting put aging behind ~23% of towaway/police-reported tire crashes (per NTSB), so the 539 tire-related deaths in 2013 imply on the order of ~120 aging-attributable deaths that year — above the frozen 90; (d) the denominator gap: no crash database records tire age (SRS 2012), so the true toll is structurally uncountable.

## Primary sources (5, all opened live 2026-09-20)
1. **NTSB Special Investigation Report NTSB/SIR-15/02, "Selected Issues in Passenger Vehicle Tire Safety"** (adopted Oct. 27, 2015) — ~33,000 tire-related passenger-vehicle crashes/year, ~19,000 injuries; 2013: 539 deaths in tire-related crashes. Findings: "NHTSA has not established the crash risk posed by tire aging, and little guidance has been made available to consumers"; 12-year-old tire failed in the 2014 Patterson, CA rollover; 10-year-old tire failed in the 2014 Centerville, LA crash; NHTSA estimated ~23% of towaway/police-reported tire crashes involved tire aging; "NHTSA's reasons for not pursuing tire aging regulation lack support." Recommendations H-15-32 (determine crash risk of tire aging since FMVSS 138/139), H-15-33 (consensus document on aging/service life). https://www.ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/documents/SIR1502.pdf
2. **Tire Review Magazine, "Decoding NHTSA's Tire Aging Report"** (quoting NHTSA, "Tire Aging: A Summary of NHTSA's Work," March 2014, 42 pp.) — "Based on analysis of data from 2005 to 2006, NHTSA estimates that with pre-FMVSS 139 tires, 90 fatalities and over 3,200 injuries occurred annually as the results of crashes that were probably caused by tire aging or where tire aging was possibly a significant factor" (databases: NMVCCS, NASS-GES, NASS-CDS). Agency declined to add an aging requirement, citing FMVSS 139 robustness, TPMS, and crash-data trends; its aging tests baked tires at 60-70C for 6-8 weeks, "approximates a tire operated in sunny Phoenix for a period of two to five years." NHTSA did not test field-aged tires older than six years. https://www.tirereview.com/decoding-nhtsas-tire-aging-report/
3. **Safety Research & Strategies (Sean Kane), "Tire Safety"** — "In the absence of any existing database documenting tire age in crashes in which a tire was a causal factor, SRS conducted original research. Our findings thus far, submitted to NHTSA in 2012, have identified 252 incidents in which tires older than six years experienced tread/belt separations — most resulting in loss-of-control and rollover crashes. These incidents were the cause of 233 fatalities and 300 injuries." Also: NHTSA's Aug. 2007 Research Report to Congress found 77% of insurance tire claims came from five hot-climate states (incl. California) and 84% of those claims were for tires more than six years old; BRMA (June 2001): unused tyres not put into service over 6 years old, all replaced 10 years from manufacture; Ford (2005): 6-year replacement recommendation regardless of tread; ADAC (1989): "Even tires that are just six years old — though they appear to be brand new — can present a safety risk"; Kansas City 2008 undercover: 9 of 10 service techs at major retailers could not identify tire age or read the TIN date; NHTSA June 2008 Consumer Advisory on tire aging. https://www.safetyresearch.net/safety-issues/tire-safety/
4. **NHTSA Part 573 Safety Recall Report 26T008** (General Rubber (Thailand) Co., Ltd; importer Gremax Industrial Inc.; submitted Feb. 11, 2026) — 65 Xcellent Roadbreaker A/T LT305/65R17, production week 4324 (Oct. 21-27, 2024), TIN 045 KLFF9L 4324. NHTSA's FMVSS 139 compliance testing showed "tread shoulder groove cracking, inner liner separation, cracking, and air loss." Cause: insufficient rubber in shoulder/sidewall during vulcanization, patched, voids remained; trapped air heated and expanded in endurance testing, "ultimately leading to cracking at the tread shoulder and separation of the inner liner." Chronology: NHTSA letter Jan. 27, 2026; company determination Feb. 4, 2026. https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2026/RCLRPT-26T008-7020.pdf
5. **NHTSA TireWise / NTSB guidance record (via NTSB SIR-15/02)** — NHTSA's TireWise site "provides guidance on determining the chronological age of a tire by decoding its TIN" and states "You cannot detect tire aging simply by looking at your tires." Average tire service life (2004): 3.6 years (NHTSA 2007); low-use vehicles see aging dominate. S&P Global Mobility via AAPEX (June 27, 2025): average age of U.S. light vehicles a record 12.8 years in 2025 (verified 2026-09-20 in prior repo research #955).

## Original contribution (required)
1. **The frozen estimate.** NHTSA's 90-deaths/year figure is 2005-2006 crash data, published 2014, never refreshed. NTSB recommendation H-15-32 (2015) told NHTSA to determine the CURRENT crash risk of tire aging post-FMVSS 138/139. Eleven years later the official toll is still 90 — from a fleet that has since aged to a record 12.8 years, the exact direction that makes aging failures more likely, not less.
2. **The denominator gap.** No crash database records tire age — SRS had to build a litigation survey in 2012 because the reporting system doesn't ask the question. FARS captures tire failure as a vehicle factor but not the tire's age. The true aging-tire death toll is structurally uncountable; the 90 is a lower-bound artifact of an old analysis, not a live measurement.
3. **The 23% arithmetic.** NHTSA's own weighting: ~23% of tire-related crashes involved aging. Apply to the NTSB's 539 tire-related deaths (2013): on the order of ~120 aging-attributable deaths — already above the frozen 90, and that year is a decade old too.
4. **The code on the sidewall.** The DOT date code (last 4 digits of the TIN, WWYY) is simultaneously the recall system's batch tracker (26T008 was identified by "4324") and the consumer's expiration reader — yet 9 of 10 retail techs in a 2008 undercover test couldn't decode it. The information exists; the literacy doesn't.

## Counterargument (full strength)
- The 90/year estimate is pre-FMVSS 139 (2007 TREAD Act standards). Post-139 tires are genuinely more robust — NHTSA's oven tests and the NTSB both found post-139 tires performed better — and mandatory TPMS (FMVSS 138, 2007) cut the severe-underinflation co-factor. The current toll is probably lower than 90. The frozen estimate cuts both ways: it may be overstating today's risk, not understating it.
- Correlation is not causation: old tires travel with old cars, deferred maintenance, chronic underinflation, overloading, and hot climates. NHTSA's own weighting puts aging behind ~23% of tire crashes — meaning ~77% weren't aging. Age is one ingredient in a compound failure, and the warm-state skew (77% of claims from 27% of policyholders in five states) means a Minnesota driver with 8-year-old tires faces a different risk than a Phoenix driver.
- A federal age-based removal rule would be nearly unenforceable at the consumer level and regressive: it would land hardest on used-tire buyers and low-income drivers, while plenty of 8-year-old tires in cool climates are fine. The BRMA's 10-year backstop is a compromise, not science.
- Defect vs. age: recall 26T008 shows manufacturing defects produce the exact same failure signature (tread-shoulder cracking, inner-liner separation) in two-year-old tires. An "aging" framing can let manufacturers off the hook for building tires that fail young — the Firestone/Wilderness ATX catastrophe was a defect story wearing an age costume.

## Limitations (explicit)
- The 90/yr and 3,200/yr figures come from 2005-2006 data on pre-FMVSS 139 tires; modern tires differ, and the NTSB noted NHTSA never published its full methodology or controlled for confounders like the recession.
- SRS's 252 cases are a litigation survey — adverse selection by construction; it collected cases where age was plausibly causal, so it cannot yield a population rate.
- The NTSB's 539 deaths (2013) are tire-RELATED (all tire failures), not tire-AGING; the ~120 figure is the article's arithmetic (23% x 539), an estimate built on an estimate.
- Cannot prove any specific crash's counterfactual; cannot say how many of the 90 would have died with fresh tires.
- Warm-climate skew is severe: cold-climate risk at 6-8 years is materially lower; the article must not universalize the Phoenix number.

## Actionable insight (hard gate)
- Read the date code: the last 4 digits of the TIN molded into the sidewall are WWYY — week and year of manufacture (e.g., 4324 = week 43 of 2024). It is stamped on only one sidewall, so check both sides.
- Replace at 6 years regardless of tread (Ford/Bridgestone/automaker guidance); 10 years from manufacture is the absolute ceiling (BRMA), not a target.
- Check the spare: aging happens whether the tire is driven or not (ADAC found unused tires may age faster); the spare is the most likely expired tire on the car.
- Never buy "new" old tires: check the date code before purchase — retailers have sold 11- and 12-year-old tires as new (Denver, 2008).
- Sun Belt drivers: park in shade when possible, avoid sustained high speeds on older tires, and check inflation monthly — heat and underinflation accelerate aging.
