Tesla’s Self-Driving Cars Can’t Tell When Their Cameras Go Blind

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 15, 2026

On March 18, 2026, federal regulators escalated their investigation of Tesla’s Full Self-Driving into an engineering analysis, the last step before a recall order, covering 3,203,754 cars.[1] Nine crashes are under investigation, one of them fatal. In the ones NHTSA reviewed, the car gave its driver no warning that anything was wrong until the moment before impact, because the system that watches the road cannot tell when it has gone blind.

3 of 9
Crashes under investigation that Tesla’s own degradation-detection fix may have changed, by the company’s own post-incident analysis. No result claimed for the other six.

Read the alleged defect slowly, because NHTSA wrote it like an indictment. When FSD encounters reduced roadway visibility, its degradation detection system fails “to detect a degraded state and to warn the driver appropriately.”[1] Reduced visibility means glare, fog, airborne dust. Conditions a human driver notices instantly, because squinting is involuntary and slowing down is instinct. A camera-only car has no squint reflex. Since Tesla stripped radar from its vehicles starting in 2021, cameras are the only eyes the car has, and when the sun hits the lens at the wrong angle, those eyes are gone while the software keeps driving like the road is clear.

Now the timeline, which is where this stops being a software bug and starts being a story. A fatal crash happened on November 28, 2023. Tesla submitted its federal crash report on June 27, 2024, and the next day it began developing an update to the degradation detection system.[6] Two years later, NHTSA wrote in its July 2, 2026 information request that it has no information on when that update was deployed or which vehicles now carry it.[1] A fix exists, but its deployment is a mystery. Somewhere in a fleet of 3.2 million cars, an unknown number are running software that may or may not warn their drivers before the cameras go dark, and the agency investigating the problem cannot tell you which ones.

NHTSA is asking sharper questions than that. Its letter demands an internal Tesla document from the earlier investigation that the agency believes shows what the company already understood about its camera-only approach before it shipped the system nationwide.[3] Regulators also want a technical description of something called Tesla’s “Direct Photon-Count Capability” system and how far it actually mitigates these conditions. And then there is the sentence NHTSA asked Tesla to justify: a company post claiming “FSD Supervised can drive in whiteout conditions.”[2] Federal investigators would like to know whether that claim is, in their words, “accurate and consistent with the system’s intended capabilities as designed.”

While the probe sits open, the software keeps shipping. Tesla pushed FSD v14.3 on October 20, 2026, adding an Automatic Collision Evasion feature for Hardware 4 cars, with no public deadline on NHTSA’s information request.[6] And this is only the first of two open probes. A second, PE25012, opened October 7, 2025, covers roughly 2.88 million Teslas whose FSD allegedly ran red lights and drove on the wrong side of the road, with 58 incidents at opening, 14 crashes, 23 injuries, and about 80 reports by December.[4] Between the two files, essentially every FSD-equipped Tesla ever sold in America is under a federal defect investigation right now. Not recalled. Investigated. There is a difference, and it matters, but so does the scale.

Fair hearing, because the other side has one. FSD is Level 2 driver assistance, not autonomy, and the driver is always responsible for supervising it. Nine incidents across 3.2 million vehicles over two years is a small absolute count, and an engineering analysis is an investigation, not a finding; NHTSA escalates probes routinely and closes many without recalls. Glare and fog blind human drivers too, and the company’s own per-mile figures, unverified by any third party, show FSD crashing less often than the average human. Maybe the fix is already everywhere and the paperwork is just behind. All of that can be true, and it still leaves a car that cannot see that it cannot see, a fix of unknown reach, and a driver who may not learn any of this until the warning arrives a fraction of a second before the crash.

What to do: if you drive a Tesla with FSD, treat glare, fog, and dust as hands-on-the-wheel conditions, because the car may not warn you it is degraded. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and watch for EA26002’s outcome; an engineering analysis can end in a recall order. Ignore any marketing that suggests the car can handle whiteout conditions. FSD is Level 2, which makes you the backup system, and the system that is supposed to tell you when to take over is the one under investigation for failing to do exactly that.
