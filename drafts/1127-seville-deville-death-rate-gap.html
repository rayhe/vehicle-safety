The Crash-Test Dummies Walked Away: The Drivers Didn't.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 10, 2026

In 1999, engineers strapped a crash-test dummy into a Cadillac Seville and fired it at a barrier at 40 miles per hour. Every injury measure came back Good, the structure held, the dummy's head never touched anything hard, and the Seville earned one of the best frontal ratings of its era. Dummies walked away from that barrier; drivers, it turns out, did not: 391 people died in Sevilles between 2014 and 2023, at 3.89 deaths per 100 million miles traveled.[1]

How many times deadlier the Seville was, per mile, than the Cadillac DeVille

Park the Seville next to its showroom siblings and the numbers get rude: the DeVille at 0.38 deaths per 100 million miles, the DTS at 0.40, the Lincoln Town Car, Detroit's other velour barge, at 0.86.[1] Same buyers, same church parking lots, same mints in the center console. One of these cars kills at ten times the rate of the others, and it is the one with the Good crash rating hanging on the wall.

Your first instinct is to blame the drivers, but don't. Of the 171 Seville drivers in fatal crashes with toxicology results, only 10.5 percent showed any impairment, against 20.4 percent for the average sedan driver in a fatal crash.[1] Seville drivers were the soberest luxury-sedan cohort in the entire dataset. Whatever is killing them, it isn't the bar. When drivers are sober and bodies pile up anyway, the machine is the suspect.

So what is it, if not the drivers? Start with what that Good rating actually covered: exactly one crash mode. IIHS fired the Seville at a barrier head-on, gave it Good marks, and never side-tested it.[2] Reality does not restrict itself to moderate-overlap frontal impacts, and a car can ace the test while being ordinary everywhere else. Then consider the calendar: the last Seville rolled off the line in 2004, which means every Seville in this dataset was at least a decade old, riding on a safety design frozen in 1998 and structurally revised in 2000.[2] A Good rating from 1999 is a time capsule, not a promise.

Softer explanations deserve daylight too, starting with the weakest link in the whole calculation, the denominator: estimating how many 20-year-old Sevilles were still on the road, and how far they drove, is educated guesswork, and if the surviving fleet was twice the estimate, the rate halves. It would still be five times the DeVille, computed the same way, but the headline number would soften. Consider also the STS trim, Cadillac's attempt at a BMW fighter, with 300 horsepower of Northstar V8 wrapped in that same formal roofline; toxicology measures substances, not speed, and sober is not the same as slow. And the Sevilles still registered in 2020 were the most worn examples of the breed, because the cream got scrapped years ago.

If someone you love still drives a Seville, and at current used prices somebody does, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before the next oil change.[3] Both the tie-rod separation campaign (99V301000) and the fuel-rail fire campaign (04V110000) are still open on affected cars, and neither one cares how the car did in 1999. Bigger than one discontinued Cadillac is the actual lesson here: crash ratings have expiration dates. A dummy survived the nineties; you are driving now.
