Truck Deaths Fell 2.5% in 2024. Injuries Rose 5%. Somebody Needs to Explain the Math.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 13, 2026

NHTSA's annual truck fact sheet wrote its own headline: 5,340 people died in crashes involving large trucks in 2024, down 2.5 percent from the year before, 13.6 percent of all U.S. traffic deaths.[1][2] Three pages later sat the unheadlined number: an estimated 161,201 people were injured, up 5 percent.[1] Same document, opposite directions.

8,809
additional occupants of other vehicles injured in truck crashes in 2024 vs. 2023 — even though 90 fewer of them died

Deaths fell everywhere: truck occupants down 4.4 percent, occupants of other vehicles down 2.3 percent, pedestrians and cyclists down 0.9 percent.[1] Yet injuries to occupants of other vehicles jumped 8.2 percent, an extra 8,809 hurt people, while truck-occupant injuries barely moved.[1] The people dying less are the same people going to the hospital more. They are not the truck drivers; they are you, in the sedan next to the Peterbilt.

Nobody prints the ratio, so I ran it: 28.0 injuries per death in 2023, 30.2 in 2024, a 7.7 percent increase. For occupants of other vehicles alone: 28.0 to 31.0, up 10.8 percent. Then the exposure wrinkle: registered trucks jumped 8 percent while total miles held near 330 billion, so the fatal crash rate per hundred million miles fell 1.63 to 1.58 while the injury rate rose 35 to 37.[1] Per mile driven, trucks got less lethal and more injurious in the same twelve months.

And the vehicle doing more of the hurting is not the 18-wheeler. Medium trucks, the 10,001-to-26,000-pound class of delivery vans and box trucks, now make up 44 percent of trucks in injury crashes, up from 39 percent a year earlier.[1] That is 9,063 more of them in the injury column. Heavy trucks' share fell from 53 to 47 percent. Filling your mirror on the residential cut-through: a box truck with a logo, not a sleeper cab with chrome.

The boring explanation deserves its day in court: more registered trucks on the same total miles means more urban, short-haul, stop-and-go exposure, and city crashes injure far more than they kill. Maybe the death decline is a mix shift, not a safety gain, with the violence relocating to lower speeds. One caveat: injury figures are CRSS estimates from a sample, not census counts like deaths, and the 2024 file is still the annual-report version, subject to revision.[1] The divergence is real in the data we have; the cause is not settled.

Seventy-two percent of truck-crash injuries, 116,475 people, are occupants of other vehicles.[1] Leave four seconds behind a truck: the crash that used to kill is increasingly the one that hospitalizes. Never cut in front of a truck and brake. In 22 percent of two-vehicle fatal truck crashes the truck was struck in the rear: a polite way of saying a car drove into the back of it.[1] Fleet buyers should check IIHS's October 8 commercial crash-prevention ratings before ordering vans: most big trucks rated marginal or poor at avoiding cars and pedestrians.[3] Run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change.
