NHTSA Will Pay States $1.5 Million to Tell You About Your Recalled Car. That Is Five Cents Per Car.

Rex Driverton • Senior Crash Correspondent
November 8, 2026

On November 5, NHTSA announced it will hand states $1.5 million to tell drivers their cars have open safety recalls, delivered with the paperwork nobody reads: the vehicle registration renewal.[1] In 2025, the agency oversaw nearly 900 recalls covering more than 29 million vehicles.[3] Divide the grant by the cars and you get about five cents per recalled vehicle. That is the whole program, priced by the nickel.

$0.05
Grant dollars per vehicle recalled in 2025: $1.5 million divided by more than 29 million vehicles

Administrator Jonathan Morrison unveiled the money at a Washington summit with the bureaucratic title “Recalls Next,” where he framed the scoreboard honestly for once: what matters is vehicles repaired, not mailers sent.[4] He also used the occasion to praise over-the-air recall fixes, which he said deliver high completion rates because owners can skip the dealer visit entirely, and to launch a bulk VIN lookup tool that checks up to 100 VINs at once.[4] A public docket on the changes takes comments through December 5.[4]

Why does the envelope need federal money? Because completion rates fall off a cliff with age. A new recall on a three-year-old vehicle averages about 68 percent completion, while the same kind of campaign on a 15-year-old vehicle averages about 18 percent.[3] Owners of older cars drift away from dealers, warranties expire, addresses go stale, and the manufacturer’s letter goes to a house the car left years ago. On average only about 60 percent of recalled vehicles ever get repaired, and the older the car, the worse the odds.[1]

Now the part that saves this story from being a punchline. Maryland ran the pilot. NHTSA gave the state $222,300 in 2017 to slip recall notices into registration renewals, and from April 2018 to January 2020, Maryland renewed 4.6 million registrations, found 456,000 vehicles carrying 943,000 open recalls, and got 371,000 of those recalls repaired.[1] More than 32 percent of the remedies were air bags. Run the division and the pilot cost about sixty cents per completed repair. Earlier grants in Maryland, California, Texas, and Ohio reached owners of about 9.5 million vehicles with open recalls, and nearly 2 million were repaired during the short grant windows.[4] Letters from a DMV sometimes produced repair rates two to three times higher than manufacturer notices alone.[5]

Sixty cents a repair changes the arithmetic, and it changes the joke. Five cents per car is a rounding error only if the money is supposed to pay for the repairs, which it is not: recall repairs are free by law, and have been since before most of these cars were built. What the grant buys is the envelope, the database match, and the two-year notification program the states must run. Morrison is not funding mechanics; he is funding postage with a track record.

Give the honest objection its full weight, because the pilot’s success does not settle the question. $1.5 million split across five expected awards is a pilot dressed as a program, and the grants.gov listing caps the whole pot at exactly that number.[2] Worse, the 18 percent completion rate on 15-year-old cars may not be a notification problem at all: those owners are the ones who no longer visit dealers, and a letter that arrives does not conjure a Saturday appointment, a loaner car, or a reason to care about a defect the car has carried silently for a decade. If the letter lands and the car still never gets fixed, the bottleneck was never postage. It was indifference, and indifference does not open envelopes.

What you can do with this. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls right now, and use the new bulk tool if you have a fleet, a family, or a used car on your shopping list: 100 VINs at once, no account, no excuse.[8] If your state is not one of the five grant winners, registration renewal will not warn you, because the letter is not coming. Owners of cars ten years or older should check twice, since your recall is the least likely to ever get fixed and the repair is free. And if you have opinions about how recall notices should reach you, the docket is open until December 5. Say it there, or say it to a registration envelope that will never answer back.
