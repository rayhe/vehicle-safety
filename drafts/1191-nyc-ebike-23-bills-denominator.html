By The Numbers

E-Bike Deaths Are Up 30%. The Denominator Says Slow Down.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They did not get better, but they did get more interesting. New York City counted 738 injuries and 13 deaths among e-bike riders in the first half of 2026, up from 601 and 10 a year earlier.[1] In response, the City Council previewed a record 23 bills on September 30, including a plan to phase out Class III e-bikes, the ones whose motors can push past 25 miles per hour.[1]

54%
of NYC e-bike deaths from 2017 through 2025 involved devices capable of exceeding 25 mph, devices already illegal in the city

Start with the numerator problem. The city's own August 2026 enforcement figures show that 45 e-bike riders died in New York from 2017 through 2025, and 54% of those deaths involved devices capable of exceeding 25 mph.[4] Any device whose motor can reach past 25 mph is not legal to sell or operate in New York City.[1] So more than half the fatalities came from machines that were already banned. Do the subtraction: the legal fleet accounts for roughly 21 deaths over nine years. That is about 2.3 per year, in a city where 680,000 adults ride regularly and electric Citi Bikes alone logged more than 29 million trips in 2024.[1][4]

2.3
deaths per year from legal, Class I/II e-bikes, estimated from the city's own 2017-2025 breakdown

Now the denominator. 13 deaths against 680,000 regular riders annualizes to roughly 3.8 deaths per 100,000 regular riders per year.[1] Compare that to the city's own accounting: e-bikes, e-scooters, and mopeds together caused roughly 4.5% of all pedestrian injuries from 2020 through 2023, per the 2024 Comptroller report.[5] Cars caused the other 95.5%. A 30% fatality jump on a base of 10 is a rounding error in a city's traffic ledger, and it is being used to justify a bill package that mostly targets the bikes that were not killing people.

This is not a claim that e-bikes are safe. It is a claim about which e-bikes are dangerous, and the answer keeps coming back to the illegal ones: throttle-driven machines that go 30, 35, 40 mph with no pedals, sold by shops the city already has authority to shut down. Council Member Lincoln Restler said it plainly in the hearing preview: delivery companies incentivize drivers to deliver as fast as possible regardless of the vehicle.[1] The incentive structure is the numerator. The banned device is the mechanism.

Meanwhile the other body count sits in a different column entirely. The Consumer Product Safety Commission's April 2026 staff report attributed 367 lithium-ion battery fires and 45 fatalities across all micromobility products from 2017 through 2024.[3] These deaths happen at night, indoors, while batteries charge. On June 24 the CPSC proposed a mandatory rule requiring e-bikes to meet UL 2849 and batteries to meet UL 2271; comments closed August 24.[3] Two weeks ago, Senators Gillibrand and Schumer wrote the Commission urging it to finish the job.[7] That is the product-safety track, and it moves at rulemaking speed while the council track moves at headline speed.

Here is the honest accounting of what the numbers cannot tell us. We do not have per-trip exposure for privately owned e-bikes, only for the Citi Bike system, so the per-rider rate above is a ceiling, not a measurement. We do not know impact speeds, so "capable of exceeding 25 mph" is a device proxy, not proof that speed caused each death. And Miami's first-responder data shows micromobility incidents rising 6.5% year over year through August, which suggests the trend is real even if the scale is small.[6]

The strongest case against my framing: a 30% fatality increase is still 30%, illegal devices are unenforceable without registration, and legal Class III bikes can legally assist past NYC's own 15 mph operating limit, so the phase-out has a real argument behind it.[1] Fair. But if 23 bills land and the illegal 30-mph throttle machines stay on the street, the next press conference will show the same numerator with a new denominator.

What to do with this. If you ride: speed capability is the killer, so buy a legal Class 1 or Class 2 bike, not a machine built to outrun the speed limit. If you charge indoors: never charge while sleeping or away from home, and look for UL 2849 certification on the bike and UL 2271 on the battery before you buy.[3] If you run a delivery app: your incentive structure is in the death count, and the bills coming for it are numbered in the double digits. And watch the CPSC rule. When UL standards go mandatory, the cheapest uncertified batteries leave the market, which is the one intervention that targets a numerator nobody disputes.
