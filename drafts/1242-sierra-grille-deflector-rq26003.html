GM Fixed the Sierra's Flying Grille. Then the Fixed Trucks Started Losing It.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 22, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds after eight plastic attachment tabs give up. On October 9, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Recall Query RQ26003 against the 2022 through 2024 GMC Sierra 1500, over trim that detaches at highway speed. So far the file reads: 118 complaints, and 21 of them allege the deflector came off after the recall repair was already applied.

21
complaints allege the deflector detached after the recall fix was done

At issue is the upper grille deflector, the wide trim bar across the top of the Sierra's grille from the truck's 2022 mid-cycle refresh. GM's own recall filing describes the failure mode with unusual candor: the attachment points fracture over time, and if all eight break, the part lets go while you are driving. Eight plastic tabs are the entire structural margin between a sixty-thousand-dollar pickup and a projectile. Per GM, the only warning is a rattle, which is less a tell than a death rattle for the trim piece.

GM heard about this from its own employee on January 19, 2024, through the company's Speak Up for Safety program. It opened an investigation on February 27, closed it on May 23 after finding "no defect trend," and reopened it on December 11 when the warranty claims started climbing. Recall 25V-060 finally landed on February 6, 2025, covering 70,768 trucks: 2022 Sierras with chrome deflectors. Trucks built without the chrome deflector in 2022 were left out. Every 2023 and 2024 was left out too. GM told NHTSA those trucks carried deflectors built outside the supplier's suspect production window, and the new query is NHTSA asking, with a straight face, whether that cutoff was right.

Run the numbers the query file is practically begging you to run. RQ26003 covers an estimated 535,639 trucks, which is 7.6 times the 70,768 that ever got recalled. About 13 percent of the trucks under review were ever offered the fix. And 21 of the 118 complaints, 17.8 percent, allege the deflector came off after the remedy was applied. A recall remedy is supposed to be the end of the story, but this one reads like a prologue.

What the part does once it leaves is simple ballistics. Owners reported cracked windshields and following drivers swerving around debris. NHTSA lists the parties at risk, in order: the truck itself, surrounding vehicles, motorcyclists, pedestrians. A detached deflector at 70 miles per hour is not a fender bender waiting to happen; it is a fender bender that already happened and is now airborne.

If you drive a 2022 through 2024 Sierra 1500, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 25V-060, and do not assume a closed recall means you are done. Only about 64 percent of the recalled trucks had the repair as of January, so plenty of eligible trucks still wear the original eight tabs. If you hear a rattle from the front end, treat it as the warning GM itself described and get to a dealer before the part finds someone else's windshield. And if you are the car behind one, give it room, because that truck ahead is shedding its face.

What this does not prove yet: 118 complaints across 535,639 trucks is a thin signal, about 22 per 100,000, and complaints are allegations, not engineering verdicts. Those 21 post-repair detachments have not been confirmed as remedy failures; GM has offered no theory for why a repaired truck would lose the part again, and road strikes or unrelated damage could be in the mix. Now steel-man the other side: GM moved fast once the warranty data turned, recalling within two months of reopening the file, and the remedy reinforces every attachment point. It may well close with a finding that the recall was adequate. But until it does, the feds are investigating whether GM drew the recall boundary around the cheapest 13 percent of the problem.
