Your Tesla Can Lose All Power at Speed. The Warning Comes After It’s Already Gone.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 5, 2026

Tesla recalled 12,963 Model 3 and Model Y cars under NHTSA campaign 25V690 because a battery pack contactor can open without warning and cut all propulsion while the car is moving.[1] One moment the accelerator works. Next moment it does nothing. Steering still works, brakes still work, but the car is a very expensive glider, and the dashboard helpfully displays an alert telling the driver to pull over safely.[3] After the power is already gone. That alert is a post-mortem with a touchscreen.

0
Warnings the driver gets before the contactor opens and propulsion dies

Trace the failure two suppliers deep and the story gets stranger. Tesla buys the contactor from SongChuan of Taiwan, part number 1089334-01-K, and SongChuan buys the solenoid inside it from a company called Sistemas Mecatronicos InTiCa.[4] InTiCa’s coil termination connection is poor, resistance climbs, and eventually the contactor opens on its own. Nobody at Tesla touched the bad joint. Nobody at SongChuan made it. It was made two contracts away from the name on the badge, which is exactly why Tesla’s Field Reliability team found it in service records in August 2025 instead of a supplier audit catching it at the factory.[4] As of October 7, 2025, the count stood at 36 warranty claims and 26 field reports, with zero crashes, zero injuries, and zero deaths attributed to the defect.[2]

Same defect, two opposite failures. Some cars with the bad contactor simply refused to enter drive from park, which is annoying and safe. Others lost propulsion at speed, which is neither. One root cause wearing two costumes, and the driveway version is the only warning the highway version ever gives. If your 2025 Model 3 or 2026 Model Y ever refused to go into drive once, that was the car telling you something it will not repeat at 70 miles an hour.

Now the math that makes this recall unusual. Tesla estimates only about 1% of the 12,963 recalled cars actually contain the defective contactor.[5] Roughly 130 bad parts hiding in a population of nearly 13,000. Tesla is replacing a hundred contactors to catch one, because there is no test that can tell a good contactor from a bad one without opening the part. That is the honest price of a defect you cannot detect from outside, and it is also the reason the recall covers cars built between March 8 and August 15, 2025 rather than a smaller, cleverer slice.[3] Suspect production ended August 15, 2025. Cars built after that date are not affected.

Fairness requires the other side, and the other side is strong. Tesla caught this itself, through its own reliability monitoring, before anyone was hurt. The fix is free, the replacement contactor drops the InTiCa solenoid entirely, and a 1% defect rate with zero attributed crashes is a rounding error in a 13,000-car population. Every manufacturer ships a bad batch of something eventually. If the standard is that no supplier defect ever reaches a customer, no car company passes it, and this recall is evidence the monitoring works rather than evidence it failed. Still, none of that changes the sequence a driver experiences: power first, warning second, and a car that can only tell you to pull over after it has already decided you are going to. If you own a 2025 Model 3 or a 2026 Model Y built between March and August 2025, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and check for 25V690.[6] Buying one used? A car from that build window should show a completed contactor replacement in its service history. Ask before you sign, not after the accelerator stops answering.
