The Gap

Two Sisters Died in a Recalled Rental Car, and Congress Banned the Practice. Your Uber Never Got the Memo.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
September 19, 2026

Before you slide into that Uber tonight, sit with this number: Carfax pulled the VINs of 438,000 taxi and rideshare vehicles across 16 major U.S. cities this month, and more than one in four was carrying at least one safety recall nobody ever fixed.[1] Your paid ride is statistically worse than your own car. The national average for private vehicles is one in five, so for-hire cars run about a third worse than the cars sitting in driveways. In New York City, a medallion cab has a 38.6 percent chance of riding on an open recall, nearly two in five. In Los Angeles, it is 11 percent, which tells you this is not physics, it is policy.

1 in 4
Taxi and rideshare vehicles across 16 major U.S. cities carry at least one unfixed safety recall (Carfax, September 2026)

Rent a car from Hertz and federal law requires the company to ground any vehicle under a safety recall within 24 hours of getting the notice. That rule has a name and a body count: the Raechel and Jacqueline Houck Safe Rental Car Act of 2015.[4] In October 2004 the sisters, ages 20 and 24, rented a Chrysler PT Cruiser from Enterprise that had been recalled a month earlier for a power steering hose that could leak, ignite, and kill the steering. The hose failed on Highway 101, the car crossed the median into an 18-wheeler, and both women died. A jury awarded their parents $15 million in 2010, Enterprise admitted its negligence was the sole cause, and Congress responded by making it illegal for rental fleets to hand you keys to a recalled car.[5] One exception in the law tells you how seriously they meant it: the only recalled rental you can legally be given is one sold for scrap.

Your Uber got no such law, and the Government Accountability Office put it in plain English: federal law does not require vehicle owners, with the exception of rental car companies, to fix open safety recalls.[2] Uber and Lyft told the GAO they deactivate drivers whose cars carry a "Do Not Drive" recall and push recall notices to everyone else. That is the floor of the obligation and also the ceiling. Of six states the GAO examined, only Maryland requires ridesourcing vehicles to be certified recall-free every year. New York's Taxi and Limousine Commission inspects cabs three times a year and checks for open recalls exactly zero of those times.[3] And you, the passenger, cannot look up the VIN of the car picking you up, because the apps do not show it. You are buying a ride in a machine whose repair history is legally nobody's business but the driver's.

Fairness demands the other side of the ledger. Not every open recall is a death trap: the taxi industry's own trade group notes that recalls range from software updates and labeling corrections to genuine mechanical failures, and the GAO found only about 0.6 percent of affected rideshare vehicles carried the urgent "Do Not Drive" designation.[1][2] Some of that 27 percent is fleet age, not negligence. Taxis run brutal mileage, drivers buy used, and used cars accumulate recall campaigns the way docks accumulate barnacles. Carfax, for its part, sells the recall checks it is frightening you with, and its sample is 16 cities that publish VINs through public records, not a national draw. All true, but it still leaves roughly 118,000 paid rides in those cities carrying a known, unrepaired defect, and no law on earth requires a single one of them to be fixed before your next trip.

What to do about it: the next time the choice is between a rental car and a rideshare, know that only one of them is federally required to be recall-free. Drivers, the check is free at nhtsa.gov/recalls and the repair is free at any dealer; downtime is the real cost, which is exactly the excuse a New York fleet operator gave Consumer Reports back in 2019.[3] Buying a used car to drive for Uber or Lyft? Run the VIN first, because the seller has no legal duty to disclose open recalls. And Maryland's annual certification is sitting right there as a template for 49 other states, none of which have copied it.[2] Ask your state legislator why, and mention the Houck sisters by name, because it worked once.
