## 1. General Editor — 8.6/10
Lede does the job in one paragraph: the national good news, then the county-level contradiction, with the 183 stat bolded on first sight. Structure is clean: national frame, the split, the table, the concentration math, the mode split, the counterargument, the action. The table is the article's spine and it earns its place. The real deduction is length: 629 prose words against a 300-500 guide, and the last run shipped at 531 by the same count. The piece justifies some of it — a seven-row table story needs the numbers — but the Bronx paragraph tries to hold two ideas (absolute risk vs. trend) and the "safest story and scariest at once" line is slightly muddled on first read. Headline is long but it is the whole thesis, which is what this site's headlines are for.

## 2. Voice Coach — 8.5/10
Axle's number-obsession is present and distinct: "Read that stack again," the table, the running share math, the regression-line energy of the counterargument paragraph. No banned phrases, no em dashes, no throat-clearing. Sentence rhythm passes all three script metrics, but the fix overcorrected: long sentences are 72.4% of the total, with 12 sentences at 31+ words, and the short-sentence share collapsed to 3.4%. That is the mirror image of the usual AI tell — a human writer with this much long-sentence density would break it up with more fragments, and the merges that fixed the short-sentence failure removed most of them. "Wear the hi-vis, take the separated route" survives as the one fragment cluster. Voice is still clearly Axle and not interchangeable with Rex, so this stays above the bar, barely.

## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0/10
All aggregate data, no individual victims named, no gore, no pathos mining — appropriate for a statistical piece. The Bronx framing is careful: the doubling is stated alongside the absolute rate (3.7 per 100,000, a third of the national average), so a reader does not leave thinking the Bronx is the most dangerous place in America. The county-by-county "got deadlier" framing could read as shaming places rather than systems, but the action paragraph redirects to systems (speed management, pedestrian infrastructure, enforcement) and to local accountability rather than blame. No self-congratulation, no moralizing about drivers. The strongest counterargument gets its own paragraph.

## 4. Social / Shareability — 8.6/10
Headline is the share: a paradox ("celebrated fewer deaths" vs. "183 counties got deadlier") with a concrete number. The Bronx-doubling line is the most repeatable stat in the piece, and the pull stat "183" is clean and screenshot-able. Weakness: the title is long for social cards and truncates awkwardly, and there is no second quotable line in the back half — the action paragraph is useful but not viral. The "look up your own county" hook is the best social mechanic here, since it personalizes a national stat, and the article buries it in the final paragraph rather than teeing it up earlier.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
Every number checks against sources read directly this run. 183 of 286 is 64.0% ("nearly two-thirds" is correct). Harris +162 and Maricopa +154 sum to 316, and 316 of the 2,899 national increase is 10.9% ("over a tenth" is correct). Motorcyclist increase 1,181 of 2,899 is 40.7% ("about 41 percent" is correct). 39,254 minus 36,355 is 2,899. County rates, state rates, and the seven "statistically clear" rises match the Censal tables exactly. "Deaths fell three straight years" (2022-2024: 42,721 to 41,025 to 39,254) and the "15th consecutive quarterly decline" and "second-lowest rate on record" (1.10, behind 1.08 in 2014) all match DOT HS 813 800. Quibble: the lede says the 2025 estimate "drops further, to 36,640" without the word "estimated" in that sentence; the disclaimer carries the caveat, but the lede itself presents an estimate as a fact.

## 6. Research Rigor — 8.7/10
Original contribution is real and threefold: the share math (64% of large counties), the concentration math (two counties account for over a tenth of the national increase), and the mode-split attribution (motorcyclists alone are 41% of the added deaths). Limitations are stated in the disclaimer, including the two-year comparison problem, per-resident denominators, through-traffic distortion, and the associational nature of the study. Strongest counterargument is stated at full strength (the improvement is real, the 2019 baseline flatters the bad news, compare with 2021 and nearly every county looks better). The honest weakness: the core thesis rides on a single study. Censal is a re-analysis of NHTSA FARS, and the national figures are independently cited from NHTSA publications, but the county counts themselves were not independently recomputed from FARS in this run — the article trusts Censal's pipeline, which is disclosed ("according to an analysis of NHTSA data by Censal Research" is the study's own suggested attribution) but should be named as a reliance, not just a citation.

## 7. Data Presentation — 8.8/10
Numbers are the article's spine and they are all clean, with counts and percents paired everywhere it matters (pedestrians +808, cyclists +219, motorcyclists +1,181). The seven-county table is exactly what Axle's beat calls for, and the "16.1 in 2019, 16.1 in 2024" repetition is the most effective line in the piece. Two soft spots: the table carries no source line beneath it (the citation lives in the surrounding prose and the references, but a table this central should carry its own attribution), and the pull label repeats the lede's 183 figure, the same minor duplication the last run had. Neither is fatal.
