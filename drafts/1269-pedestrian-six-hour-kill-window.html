Half of Pedestrian Deaths Happen in Six Evening Hours. Those Hours Are About to Get Darker.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
January 2, 2028

According to federal fatality tables, 3,351 pedestrians died between 6 p.m. and midnight in 2024, which is 47.3 percent of all 7,080 pedestrian deaths that year, packed into a quarter of the day. Daylight saving time ends Sunday, November 1, 2026, and the evening commute slides into darkness right inside that window. October is National Pedestrian Safety Month, which makes this a fitting moment to stare at the clock and admit what it says.[1]

Darkness does most of the explaining. About 76 percent of 2024 pedestrian fatalities happened in the dark, and alcohol showed up in 43 percent of fatal pedestrian crashes, counting the driver, the pedestrian, or both. Evening is when darkness, drinking, and foot traffic overlap, and the FARS tables make that overlap look less like coincidence and more like a schedule. A pedestrian died every 74 minutes in 2024, and one was injured every 7 minutes. Most of those deaths were not freak accidents at noon. They were evening deaths, in the dark, with alcohol somewhere in the report.[3]

Where people die is nearly as concentrated as when. Only 1,246 of the 7,080 deaths happened at intersections. Another 5,104 happened somewhere else: mid-block crossings, unlit stretches, shoulders, driveways. Older pedestrians break the pattern slightly, with the 65-to-74 group dying at intersections at the highest rate of any age group, 28.9 percent. Children under 5 go the other way, with 67.9 percent of their deaths happening away from intersections. Whatever your age, the intersection you worry about is not where most of the dying happens. It is the dark stretch between intersections.[1]

None of this means walking has never been more dangerous. Pedestrian deaths fell about 4 percent in 2024 versus 2023, and early 2025 estimates point down again. Cars with pedestrian-detecting automatic braking crash into pedestrians 27 percent less often than cars without it, according to IIHS. Progress is real. It just has not touched the evening window yet, because no sensor repeals sunset, and no software update makes a drunk driver sober or a dark jacket visible.[2]

So treat 6 p.m. like a curfew for invisibility. Walkers: wear something reflective, carry a light, and use a marked crossing instead of dashing mid-block, because that mid-block dash is where 72 percent of the body count sits. Drivers: slow down in residential and commercial areas after dark, and treat every unlit stretch as a crossing zone, because for six hours a night it is one. Drinkers: do not drive, and do not walk home along a fast road in the dark either, since alcohol shows up in 43 percent of these fatal crashes. Car shoppers: put pedestrian-detecting automatic braking on the must-have list, since IIHS ties it to a 27 percent lower pedestrian-crash rate. And everyone: remember that next Sunday the dark arrives an hour earlier, right on schedule.[4]
