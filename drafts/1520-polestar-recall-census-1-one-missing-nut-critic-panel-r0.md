## 1. General Editor — 8.5
Lede earns its keep: one recall, 213 cars, one nut, and the plant that built them, all in the first paragraph. Structure is clean: the count, the Charleston irony, the exit context, the counterargument, the action list, the limitations. At 930 words it sits well under the recent census range, and nothing in it is filler. The dock is the exit paragraph: the Commerce Department denial is the most consequential fact in the piece and it gets a single paragraph that has to carry the rule, the Volvo contrast, the no-appeal decision, and the wind-down in five sentences. It holds together, but the paragraph is doing four jobs. The counterargument paragraph and the "What this does not prove" paragraph also overlap on the same point (low count, small danger), stated twice with different framing. Neither overlap breaks the piece, and the length is a genuine virtue for a one-campaign census.

## 2. Voice Coach — 8.5
Clara is present and distinct: consumer-first, practical, faintly furious on behalf of 213 people who bought a $67,500 SUV held together by half its fasteners. The opener echoes her catchphrase register ("Before you buy the story...") without repeating it verbatim, and the action list reads like advice to a neighbor, not a press release. Rhythm metrics pass with wide margin; the sentence-length spread is genuinely human after the revision pass that merged the fragment run. Zero banned phrases, zero em dashes, The-starters at 2.3%. Two seams: "Both sentences are true at the same time" is the census-series template line, also in the #1519 panel, and "Grant all of that, then look at the other column" is the same series seam in different clothes. Two seams, neither breaking the voice. Ship it.

## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 9.0
There are no victims to exploit here: zero injuries, zero crashes, and the article says so rather than implying a body count. Blame lands on the manufacturer and on an assembly process that let a seat bracket ship half-fastened, never on owners, and the call to check VINs is legitimate civic action with a real mechanism behind it. The US-exit thread is handled without schadenfreude: the article notes the service network stays open and warranties remain valid, which is the information existing owners actually need, rather than treating the ban as a punchline. The RQ25004 camera saga is cross-referenced, not re-reported for a second helping of the same outrage. No self-congratulation, no moralizing.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 9.0
The headline is built to travel, and the pull stat (1) is the number people will quote: a whole brand, a whole year, one recall. Quotable lines are spread through the piece rather than front-loaded: "It is two nuts, and someone tightened one," "A one-recall year is what a wind-down looks like on paper," and "the car in your driveway" in the action list. The one-recall framing survives a screenshot on its own. The piece loses a point only because the most shareable context, the Commerce Department ban, sits in the middle of the article rather than in the lede, where a skimming reader might miss why one recall is a story at all. Fine for the site's audience; the back half carries its weight.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0
Every checkable claim verified against primary documents. The census count (one 2026 campaign, 26V037, 213 vehicles) matches the NHTSA API sweep and the independent AnyRecalls cross-check (17 all-time, newest January 23, 2026). The RCMN confirms the one-of-two-nuts defect, the Charleston plant, FMVSS 207, and the March 22, 2026 owner-letter date. The Reuters June 25, 2026 account of the Connected Vehicles Rule denial, the 2027 model-year cutoff, the continued sale of existing stock, and the no-appeal decision all match the article. FMVSS 207 is the correct standard for seating systems. The 2025 count of seven campaigns matches the API results (25V280, 25V293, 25V409, 25V420, 25V453, 25V555, 25V615). The build window and Ford's 85 recalls were uncited in the first draft and were fixed pre-score with references [7] and [8]. One nit: reference [1] links to AnyRecalls rather than the API query set; the raw results are saved with the research file, so the citation is still verifiable, but a methods note link would be stronger.

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0
Original contribution is real and stated: the 1-campaign corrected-vocabulary census (~36 API queries, with the 2019-2020 gap-check that caught 24V835 and closed the 16-to-17 all-time count), the 7-to-1 collapse from 2025, the Charleston irony, the one-nut math, and the quietest-year-in-the-series framing. Limitations are explicit and honest: the filing window ends November 15, 2026; campaign populations are recall actions not unique vehicles (moot here, and the article says so); the API 400 and vocabulary risks are disclosed with the direction of bias stated; no FARS data exists for the 2025 Polestar 3; and the exit thread is trade policy, not safety, with the two kept separate. The counterargument is genuinely steelmanned ("one recall is the safety system working... the tally is big, the danger per vehicle is small" is granted in full before the answer). Methodology caveat is in the disclaimer as well as the body. Eight references, four of them primary NHTSA documents plus the live API.

## 7. Data Presentation — 9.0
The pull stat is the thesis number and it is correct: 1 campaign, 213 vehicles, the smallest non-zero recall year in the series. All numbers are consistent between body, pull stat, and disclaimer: 7 campaigns in 2025, 17 all-time, 6 Polestar 3 campaigns, 27,816 Polestar 2s in the camera recall, 275 complaints down to 3. The campaign-population vs unique-vehicle distinction is stated in the limitations paragraph. The 2025-vs-2026 comparison uses the same census method on both years, so the 7-to-1 collapse is an apples-to-apples comparison rather than a method artifact. No chart needed: the story is a count, and the count is the chart.
