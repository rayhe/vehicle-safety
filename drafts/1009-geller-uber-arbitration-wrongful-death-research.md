# Research: 1009-geller-uber-arbitration-wrongful-death

## Article thesis
Uber spent four and a half years trying to force a fatal 2022 rideshare crash into confidential arbitration. On September 24, 2026, the Illinois Supreme Court unanimously ruled the company could not use either the dead rider's Uber agreement or his widow's separate Uber agreement to compel her wrongful-death claim into arbitration. The Crash Report angle: confidential arbitration is a safety-data black hole. No public docket, no trackable schedule, no public verdict benchmark. When fatal crashes disappear into private arbitration, the public loses the crash record the same way it loses data to redacted NHTSA filings. Geller is the first state high-court ruling severing non-party TNC arbitration in wrongful death, and it has persuasive reach everywhere Uber's materially similar Terms of Service operate.

## Key facts (grounded)
- Case: Geller v. Uber Technologies, Inc., 2026 IL 132066, Supreme Court of Illinois, decided September 24, 2026, unanimous.
- The crash: April 2022. Mark Geller of Chicago hired an Uber through his own app for a trip to Midway Airport. Driver Ejaz Rathore lost control on the Stevenson Expressway. Both Rathore and Geller were killed.
- Plaintiff: Gloria Sheridan Geller, widow, as independent administrator of Mark's estate. Sued in Cook County Circuit Court: survival, wrongful-death, negligence, res ipsa loquitur theories against Uber Technologies, Inc., Rasier, LLC, and Rathore.
- Both Mark and Sheridan had separately accepted Uber terms containing arbitration provisions and delegation clauses (threshold arbitrability questions go to an arbitrator).
- Circuit court: compelled the estate's survival claims to arbitration under Mark's agreement, but refused to compel the wrongful-death claims under Sheridan's agreement, reasoning her agreement covered her own use of her own app, not a third person's use.
- The estate voluntarily dismissed the survival claims, sacrificing them to get the wrongful-death claims into open court.
- First District Appellate Court reversed: Sheridan's delegation clause required an arbitrator to decide whether the wrongful-death claims were arbitrable.
- Illinois Supreme Court reversed the appellate court and affirmed the circuit court: no clear and unmistakable evidence that Sheridan, as statutory beneficiary or personal representative, agreed to delegate arbitrability of wrongful-death claims arising from her husband's use. Remanded for litigation. Quote: "Arbitration is a matter of consent, and Uber has failed to establish that the estate or the statutory beneficiary consented to arbitrate this pending wrongful death dispute."
- Argued for the estate by Clifford Law Offices partner Charles R. Haskins (argued May 2026).

## Novel calculations / analysis (original to this article)
1. **The 53-month sealed timeline.** April 2022 crash to September 24, 2026 ruling = 4 years 5 months (53 months) before a court confirmed the case could proceed in public. The timeline ran: circuit court split ruling, voluntary dismissal of survival claims, appellate reversal ordering arbitration, supreme court reversal. For over four years the only forum Uber offered was confidential.
2. **The sacrificed claims.** The estate voluntarily dismissed its survival claims after the circuit court sent them to arbitration under Mark's agreement. A grieving family gave up an entire category of claims to escape the arbitration forum. That tactical detail quantifies how much leverage the arbitration clause carried.
3. **The two-agreement failure.** Uber tried two separate contractual hooks and lost on both as applied to the wrongful-death claims: Mark's agreement (bound only the contracting party, and the survival claims under it were dismissed) and Sheridan's own agreement (limited to claims connected with her own use of her own app). The opinion severs both the decedent-signs and survivor-signs theories at once.
4. **The data-opacity mechanism.** Per legal-industry analysis, open-court TNC litigation produces trackable docket schedules and public verdict benchmarks; confidential arbitration produces neither. Every fatal crash routed to arbitration is a crash the public safety record never sees. Geller converts future Illinois TNC wrongful-death cases from sealed to trackable.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Arbitration is not inherently a cover-up. It is faster, cheaper, and many plaintiffs prefer it to years of litigation. Uber's position was facially reasonable: both Mark and Sheridan had signed the same Terms of Service containing the same arbitration clause, and the company argued the widow's own agreement covered the dispute. The ruling is binding only in Illinois; other states' wrongful-death statutes differ, and in states that treat wrongful death as derivative of the decedent's claim, the survivor can still be bound by the decedent's arbitration agreement. And arbitration still captured part of this very case: the survival claims went to arbitration under Mark's agreement and were dismissed, not litigated.

## Limitations
- Findings rest on the published opinion syllabus, the winning firm's announcement, and legal-press summaries; the full slip opinion was not independently re-read line by line for this article.
- The exact April 2022 crash date and the vehicle involved are not in the public summaries reviewed.
- There is no public count of how many TNC fatality cases sit in confidential arbitration. That number is unknowable by design, which is the point, but it means the scale of the data black hole cannot be quantified.
- FARS does not isolate transportation-network-company crashes, so no FARS cross-tab anchors the rideshare-specific toll.

## Actionable takeaways
- If a family member is killed in a rideshare, do not assume the app's arbitration clause binds the survivors. Under Geller, a non-signing survivor's independent wrongful-death claim is not compelled by the decedent's agreement, at least in Illinois.
- Do not agree to arbitrate before consulting counsel. The estate in Geller sacrificed its survival claims to escape arbitration; the forum decision is the whole fight.
- Plaintiff-side intake economics shifted: the arbitration threat was a primary friction point making rideshare wrongful-death files expensive to develop on contingency. That shield is now cracked in Illinois and weakened as persuasive authority elsewhere.
