IIHS Made the Side Crash Test 81% More Violent — Two-Thirds of Good Ratings Did Not Survive

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

I ran the numbers on the updated side-impact test twice, because the first answer looked like a typo. The old barrier weighed 3,300 pounds and struck at 31 mph; the new one weighs 4,200 pounds and strikes at 37 mph. Kinetic energy is mass times velocity squared, so the new test delivers (4,200 ÷ 3,300) × (37 ÷ 31)² = 1.81 times the energy of the old one. The test got 81% more violent, and it is aimed at the thinnest part of your car.[1]

81%
More kinetic energy in the updated side-impact barrier than the original — own calculation from IIHS specs

Somebody tabulated every vehicle rated under both versions of the test. Of 338 vehicles that earned Good on the original, only 106 kept it. Another 133 fell all the way to Poor or Marginal, and 99 more landed on Acceptable. Same cars. Same structures. Different barrier. Nothing about those vehicles changed between the two ratings except the ruler.[3]

A frontal crash gives you a meter of crumple zone. A side impact gives you a few centimeters of door, and then it is up to the B-pillar, the seat structure, and the curtain airbag to do the absorbing. Add 81% more energy and the margin evaporates. IIHS rebuilt the test because its research showed that real-world side impacts — still good for nearly a quarter of passenger-vehicle occupant fatalities — had outgrown the old barrier years ago.[1] That barrier was modeled on a mid-2000s SUV. Ride heights rose, weights rose, and the barrier stopped representing anything on the road.

Here is the part that surprised me: the fix came fast. Among model-year 2023 vehicles rated on the updated test, 17% scored Poor or Marginal; for 2024 it was 10%, for 2025 6%, and for 2026 5%. That is roughly a two-thirds decline in the failure share in three model years, and it happened because the fixes are structural — stronger B-pillars, better door beams, curtain airbags that cover more area for longer.[3] Structural fixes arrive with redesigns, not mid-cycle tweaks, which means a 2023 and a 2025 of the same nameplate can be different cars in a side impact even when the brochure says otherwise.

Now the part that should annoy you: a “Good” side rating from 2020 and a “Good” side rating from 2024 are not the same grade. One was earned against a 3,300-pound barrier at 31 mph; the other against a 4,200-pound barrier at 37 mph, carrying 81% more energy. Listings show the word “Good” with no version tag. If you are comparing a 2020 and a 2024 of the same model on a used lot, you are comparing scores from two different tests that happen to share a vocabulary.[2]

And the new barrier still flatters reality. IIHS sized it to today’s midsize SUV, but the heaviest vehicles on the road weigh roughly twice that, and a test can measure how well a small car survives being struck by a large one without changing the fact that it is being struck by a large one. A test can grade the armor, but it cannot shrink the sword.

What to do with this

Shopping used: open iihs.org/ratings and confirm the side rating is the updated test, not the original — the grade alone tells you nothing about which barrier it faced. On older cars, curtain airbag coverage is the spec to verify, since it does most of the work in exactly this crash. And if the choice is between a 2023 and a 2025 of the same model, the newer one likely carries the structural fixes the new test forced; same nameplate, different B-pillar.

What this does not prove: the collapse is not evidence that cars got more dangerous — the cars were identical and only the ruler changed, so a 2022 car rated Marginal on the new test may still protect you better than a 2018 car rated Good on the old one. That 338-vehicle tabulation is a secondary analysis of IIHS ratings data, not an independent re-scrape, and the 81% is barrier energy, not energy absorbed by any particular cabin — intrusion is a structures problem, and a deformable barrier is not a pickup truck. Some of that fast recovery could also be teaching-to-the-test; B-pillars are physical, but test-specific tuning is a known sport. Treat the rating as what it is: a grade against a protocol, not a prophecy.
