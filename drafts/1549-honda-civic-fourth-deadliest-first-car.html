The Gap

America’s First Car Is Its Fourth-Deadliest. The Drivers Aren’t the Problem.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 20, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. A Honda Civic hits something solid, the front structure folds, the belts catch, the bags fire. That sequence is supposed to be the great equalizer. It is not. Over ten years of federal crash data, the Civic killed 6,553 people, the fourth-highest body count of any nameplate in America, behind only the Silverado, the F-150, and the Accord.[1]

6,553
People killed in Honda Civics, 2014-2023. Fourth of 337 nameplates. Rate: 2.25 deaths per 100 million miles.

Raw counts flatter big sellers, so here is the rate: 2.25 deaths per 100 million miles, 30th worst of 337 models. Now the part that should make you put the coffee down. Run the same decade of data through the toxicology file and Civic drivers test impaired in 20.4 percent of fatal crashes. Kia Forte drivers: 20.1 percent. Subaru Impreza drivers: 21.9 percent, the highest in the comparison. Yet the Forte’s death rate is 0.40 and the Impreza’s is 0.52. Same drivers, same bad decisions, a fifth of the dying. At some point you stop blaming the humans and start looking at the first 150 milliseconds.[1]

Impairment across that table spans 18.6 to 21.9 percent. Death rates span 0.40 to 3.07, a 7.7-fold spread. If drunk and drugged drivers were doing the killing, the columns would move together. They do not move at all. And it gets worse inside Honda’s own showroom: the CR-V posts 0.53 on the same table, 4.2 times lower than the Civic, and the HR-V posts 0.13. Honda knows how to build a car that keeps people alive. It just did not build it into its best-selling car.[1]

Before you write this off as an old-car problem, look at the model years. Divide each recent model year’s deaths by its years on the road inside the window: 2015 through 2020 Civics run about 21 to 25 deaths per exposure-year, while 2021 through 2023 run about 10 to 15, roughly a 40 percent drop. That improvement is real, and it lines up with the 11th-generation redesign and Honda Sensing going standard. But the new car has its own problem, and it is sitting in the back seat.[1]

IIHS tested the 2026 Civic twice, once per body style, and the two cars disagree with each other. The hatchback earns a Top Safety Pick: Good in the updated moderate overlap test, rear-passenger restraints Acceptable. The sedan scores Acceptable overall in that same test, with rear-passenger restraints rated Marginal, and under the 2026 rules a Marginal anywhere in the three crashworthiness tests means no award at all. Same platform, same brand, same year. One body style protects its rear passengers and one does not, and the sedan is the one most people buy.[2][3][4]

Fairness check, because the counterargument is strong: a 2026 Civic is a genuinely safe car in the front seats, with Good small-overlap and side ratings, Good headlights, and standard crash prevention. The decade body count is dominated by older cars, a 2008 is the single deadliest model year in the record at 402 deaths, and nobody shopping new should be judged by what a 2008 did. All true. Also true: the Corolla is just as old a nameplate and posts 1.85 against the Civic’s 2.25, the Forte is cheaper with the same drivers and a fifth the rate, and the sedan carries Marginal rear restraints into model year 2027. Newer is better. Better is not the same as fixed.

Shopping used? Model years 2021 and newer show roughly 40 percent fewer deaths per year on the road than 2015 to 2020 cars in this data, and a 2008 is the one to avoid. Buying new? Buy the hatchback, not the sedan: the 2026 hatchback is an IIHS Top Safety Pick and the sedan is not, and the difference is entirely the rear seat. Already own one? Belt every rear passenger, every trip. In this car, the back seat is the least-protected seat in the vehicle, in the lab and on the road. Cross-shopping the segment? A Forte or an Impreza posts a fifth of the Civic’s death rate with drivers who test just as impaired. The gap is the car.[1][2]
