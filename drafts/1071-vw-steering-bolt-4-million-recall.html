The Biggest Recall Since Dieselgate Is Over a Single Screw

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
June 16, 2027

Let's talk about the steering rack in your Volkswagen. Two bolts hold it to the subframe. One of them can rot through. Volkswagen Group is now recalling roughly four million cars across four brands because a single screw corrodes, its largest recall since the Dieselgate emissions scandal a decade ago.[1] Not an engine. Not software. A fastener.

4M
Vehicles recalled across VW, Audi, Skoda, and Seat over one corroding steering bolt

Here is the failure chain. Corrosion attacks the left-hand-drive steering rack-to-subframe mounting connection and can fracture the head of the bolt. The rack is held by two bolts, so when the first head fractures, the second mount starts carrying cyclic loads it was never sized for alone. It fatigues. It fractures. Then the rack is attached to the subframe by nothing, which is how you lose steering.[2] Two bolts was the entire margin. That margin was measured against a showroom floor, not eleven years of road salt.

The KBA puts the count at 2.16 million Volkswagens (Golf, Golf Variant, Tiguan, Touran, Caddy), 700,000 Audi Q3s, more than a million Skodas, and 200,000 Seats, with VW builds spanning September 2013 to July 2024.[1][3] In the US the campaign (NHTSA 26V590) covers 208,724 cars: 82,601 2018-2019 Atlas SUVs, 85,713 2018 Tiguans, and 40,410 2019-2021 Audi Q3s.[2] Volkswagen investigated its first corrosion case in October 2024 and did not classify it as safety-relevant until September 2026. Two years from first sighting to recall, for a steering bolt.

1%
Estimated share of covered cars that actually carry the defect — about 40,000 vehicles

Two mercies bury themselves in the fine print. First, US authorities estimate roughly 1 percent of covered cars will actually exhibit the defect, which is 40,000 genuinely bad cars hiding inside four million service appointments.[4] Second, the NHTSA-filed documents say the bolt head is most likely to fracture during parking and low-speed maneuvering, because steering loads are highest there.[2] Corrosion chose the most forgiving possible envelope: if you are going to lose steering, lose it at five miles an hour next to a shopping cart. This is the best-case scenario of a defect that should never have shipped.

Somebody ran this cost-benefit in 2012 and the answer was wrong. A steering rack mounting is a load path with exactly one layer of redundancy, sitting in the splash zone of every winter road on earth, specified with a fastener whose corrosion resistance apparently bought the farm sometime before the second owner. Volkswagen calls the campaign "precautionary" and says the screw swap takes under an hour, while declining to say what four million screws cost.[1] Repairs are already slowed by parts shortages and strained workshop capacity, so the precautionary hour might be scheduled weeks out.

What to do with it: if you drive a 2018-2019 Atlas, a 2018 Tiguan, or a 2019-2021 Q3, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today and book the appointment early, because capacity is the real bottleneck now.[5] Salt-belt owners should go first; corrosion is the entire defect. European owners of 2013-2024 Golfs, Tiguans, Tourans, Caddys, Q3s, Skodas, and Seats should check their national registration database, since many of these cars are on their second or third owners and recall letters only reach whoever the registry knows. The fix is free and fast. The corrosion had an eleven-year head start.
