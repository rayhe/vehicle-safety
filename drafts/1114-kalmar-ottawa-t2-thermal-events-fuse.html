The Trucks That Move Every Shipping Container Have an Electrical Fire Problem. The Fix Is a Fuse.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 9, 2026

Let's talk about what happens inside an electrical cabinet when insulation gives up. Insulation is a promise: this much resistance between this conductor and everything else. Heat, vibration, and moisture all tax the promise, and a yard tractor lives outdoors in all three. Resistance drops, leakage current starts flowing where current was never invited, and it heats a small spot, which degrades the insulation further, which invites more current. The spiral has a name in the trade: insulation resistance breakdown. At the end of it is a short circuit, and at the end of that, Kalmar's chosen euphemism: a thermal event.[1]

On October 5, Kalmar filed a recall with NHTSA for its Ottawa T2 terminal tractor, covering the 4x2 DOT/EPA, Off Road, and 6x4 DOT/EPA configurations. The power distribution board in the cabin, Kalmar says, "can experience a breakdown of electrical insulation resistance and may also be subject to a short circuit and has, in very rare circumstances, led to thermal events."[1] If you have never heard of a terminal tractor, you have still been served by one. It is the stubby yard spotter that shuffles shipping containers and trailers around ports, rail yards, and distribution centers. Every container you own rode behind one.

of the Ottawa T2 fleet that Kalmar says has experienced thermal events from the cabin power board fault. Kalmar disclosed the rate but not the fleet size, so the absolute number is unknowable from the filing.[1]

That missing denominator is worth pausing on. "Less than 0.001% of total units" is a rarity claim with no population attached. One thermal event in a fleet of 100,000 and ten in a fleet of a million both satisfy it. A real disclosure would give the population, the event count, and whether anyone was hurt. Kalmar gave a percentage and moved on. Percentages without denominators are how you describe a risk without describing it.

Now the remedy, which is the most honest sentence in the filing. Kalmar will add "a remote ground stud (RGS/fuse) to completely isolate certain electrical circuits and prevent thermal events from occurring."[1] Read that as an engineer. A fused ground stud does not stop insulation from breaking down. It gives the fault current somewhere safe to go and a fuse to sacrifice when it gets there. That is defense in depth for a failure you cannot fully characterize. Kalmar does not know which boards will break down or exactly when; it knows where the current will go when they do. The fuse is a confession wearing a hard hat.

This is not the T2's first recall, which is worth knowing before you file this one under "freak occurrence." In May 2022, Kalmar recalled 687 Ottawa T2s because the trailer handbrake and parking brake valve vent lines were plumbed together, a Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 121 noncompliance that could set the trailer brakes unexpectedly.[5] In February 2023, it recalled 128 more because the cab-tilt hydraulic cylinder could come apart and let the entire cab fall forward onto anyone standing in front of it.[4] Three recalls, three unrelated systems: brakes, hydraulics, electrics. That pattern does not describe a single bad batch; it describes a vehicle whose safety record is whatever gets filed.

3 recalls
for the Ottawa T2 across three unrelated systems since 2022: brake valve plumbing (687 units), cab-tilt cylinder (128 units), and now cabin power board insulation breakdown.[4][5]

Fairness requires the other column, and it is solid. Less than 0.001% is genuinely rare; this is a precautionary recall, not a burning fleet. Kalmar filed voluntarily with no reported injuries attached, which is the responsible move. A fused ground stud is proper engineering, not a band-aid; fuses exist precisely for faults you cannot predict. And yard tractors work at walking speed inside fenced industrial sites, so a thermal event's harm radius is a cab and a parking lot rather than a highway, and the prior recalls were also caught and corrected, which is the system working.

What this article does not prove: how many thermal events actually occurred (the denominator is undisclosed), whether any caused injuries (none reported), or that the T2 is unusually fire-prone for its class (there is no class-wide fire data for terminal tractors to compare against). It also does not prove the three recalls share a root cause; they are filed as unrelated defects in unrelated systems. This is a story about a remedy and a disclosure gap, not a verdict on the vehicle.

Actionable insights. If you run a fleet with Ottawa T2s: check your VINs against the NHTSA recall lookup, schedule the RGS retrofit when Kalmar's service team calls, and have your techs inspect cabin power boards for heat discoloration or the smell of cooked insulation in the meantime. Insulation breakdown announces itself before it arcs, if anyone looks. For everyone else: there is nothing to do, which is the point. Terminal tractors are the least-scrutinized powered vehicles in American logistics. No IIHS ratings, no NCAP stars, no consumer reviews. They work where the public never goes, so their safety record is whatever the manufacturer files. This recall exists because Kalmar filed it, which raises the question of what doesn't get filed.

A fuse is a small, cheap, honest device. It admits the fault will happen and arranges for the consequences to be boring. There is a lesson in that for the whole commercial vehicle class, which IIHS just graded mostly Poor at avoiding crashes in the first place. The vehicles that move the economy do not get the safety engineering the economy assumes they have. They get a ground stud and a prayer, and this week, at least the ground stud is new.
