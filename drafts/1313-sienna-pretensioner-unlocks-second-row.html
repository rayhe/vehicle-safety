Toyota's Seat Belt Pretensioner Can Shake the Sienna's Second Row Loose

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 28, 2026

Before you buckle your kid into a Sienna this morning, you might want to see this. Toyota is recalling 228,367 Sienna Hybrid minivans because the seat belt pretensioner, the device whose entire job is yanking you safe in the first milliseconds of a crash, can fire hard enough to shake a second-row seatback out of its locked position.[1]

228,367
2022–2026 Sienna Hybrid minivans recalled under NHTSA campaign 26V690

This is a seven-passenger problem only. The affected vans have second-row captain's chairs with integrated seat belts and pretensioners, built between September 16, 2021 and July 20, 2026. If yours has an eight-passenger bench seat, you are not in the recall.[2] Check your VIN anyway, because Toyota's notification letters do not go out until December 7, and waiting six weeks for a letter about your children's seat is not a plan.

Now the mechanism, because it is genuinely wild. When a frontal crash fires the pretensioner, the yank sets up high-magnitude oscillations between the locking cam and the pawls inside the seat's outboard recliner mechanism. Under the wrong combination of impact force, seatback recline angle, and pretensioner firing timing, that vibration rotates the internal cam far enough to let the recliner unseat from its locked position.[3] The seatback then does whatever physics wants, with a child belted to it.

That is not a theory; Toyota's own engineers stumbled on it by accident, watching a recliner disengage during prototype sled tests for a future vehicle between January and November of 2025. Nobody complained and nobody was hurt. They then ran dozens of sled and full-scale crash tests through this month to prove the failure mode, varying floor acceleration, internal friction, and pretensioner timing until they could reproduce the unlock on demand.[3] As of October 12, the company counts zero field technical reports and zero U.S. warranty claims, and the recall was decided October 14 anyway.

Credit where due: this is what a recall system is supposed to look like, and it almost never looks like this. No deaths forced the issue and no lawsuit. An engineer saw something wrong on a sled and the company spent a year proving it before recalling a quarter-million vehicles.

Credit ends there, because the fix is a software patch to the supplemental restraint system ECU that retimes the pretensioner, free at any dealer, plus physical replacement of both second-row seatbacks on roughly 200 vehicles with the Mobility as a Service package.[2] A software update for a mechanical oscillation is elegant engineering and also a reminder: your seatback is now one bad line of code away from being ornamental. And the December 7 letter timeline means owners sit exposed for six weeks while the fix already exists.

Now the counterargument, stated fairly: zero injuries, zero complaints, a failure found in a lab on a sled, and one could call this a phantom risk and the recall a costly overreaction. But full-scale crash tests reproduced the unlock, and the trigger conditions, frontal impact plus pretensioner fire plus reclined seatback, describe millions of real family road trips, not some corner of the simulation envelope. A precautionary recall on a quarter-million family vehicles carrying kids is the cheapest insurance in the industry, and punishing Toyota for catching its own defect would teach every automaker the wrong lesson.

What this article did not prove: the actual on-road failure probability is unknown, and neither Toyota nor NHTSA has published one, so we cannot say how many of the 228,367 vans would ever experience this. Toyota has not named the recliner or pretensioner suppliers, so independent scrutiny of their hardware is impossible from public documents. Whether the excluded 2021 model year shares any affected hardware is unclear from the filing. This article is news-driven, not FARS-driven; the claims above trace to NHTSA filings and contemporaneous press coverage, not to crash statistics.

What to do: if you own a 2022 through 2026 Sienna Hybrid with seven seats, enter your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today and schedule the ECU update. It is free, it takes one dealer visit, it exists right now, and there is no reason to wait for December.
