# Research: Range Rover Steering Knuckle Cracks — EA26003, 331,559 Vehicles, One Recall That Stops at 2017

**Slug:** 1405-range-rover-knuckle-crack-331k-ea26003
**Journalist:** Mia Crumplezone (Safety Engineering / design analysis)
**Number:** 1405
**News peg:** NHTSA Engineering Analysis EA26003 (opened April 24, 2026; information request EA26003-01 sent September 18, 2026) into front steering knuckle fractures on 2014-2022 Range Rover / Range Rover Sport. Fresh owner reports continue into October 2026.
**Date:** 2026-11-05

## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. A federal engineering analysis covering 331,559 luxury SUVs is examining a steering part that can crack and let the suspension arm detach at highway speed. The recall that was supposed to fix it covers only 121,509 vehicles and stops at model year 2017, while the newest fracture reports involve 2020 and 2021 vehicles. Owners are still filing reports of knuckles disconnecting on the highway in October 2026.
- Novel angle? Yes, three of them: (1) the recall/reality gap arithmetic — 331,559 under investigation vs 121,509 recalled means 210,050 vehicles (63%) with a substantially similar part sit outside the recall; (2) the July 27, 2026 owner report in which a 2016 Range Rover veered into a semi after its right-front knuckle separated, and police recorded "unsafe lane change" without any component inspection — the defect can masquerade as driver error in the crash record; (3) the brace remedy — for knuckles with no visible crack, JLR's fix is a retaining bracket, not a new part; the brace only prevents complete separation after the casting cracks.

## Verified numbers
- EA26003 population: **331,559** — all 2014-2022 Range Rover (L405) and Range Rover Sport (L494) manufactured for sale/lease in the US
- Recall 25V514 population: **121,509** — 54,720 Range Rover + 66,789 Range Rover Sport, certain 2014 and all 2015-2017, built Oct 1, 2014 - Nov 10, 2017 at Solihull
- Gap: 331,559 - 121,509 = **210,050 vehicles (63.3%)** outside the recall but inside the investigation
- Failure summary (ODI resume, as of April 24, 2026): 236 total incidents (21 ODI complaints; manufacturer reports 522 before de-duplication); **1 crash**, 0 injury incidents, 0 fatalities
- September 18, 2026 IR letter: **41 reports** of a crack developing in the upper clevis portion of the aluminum front steering knuckle, on one or both sides
- PE25006 opened June 27, 2025 after **12 VOQs** reporting front aluminum steering knuckles fracturing; upgraded to EA April 24, 2026
- Two most recent VOQs at EA opening: MY 2020 (ref 11653003, received April 6, 2025) and MY 2021 (ref 11718204) Range Rover L405 fractures — both outside the recall years
- Defect: crack at the upper clevis of the cast aluminum front steering knuckle (supplier: SuperAlloy Industrial Co. Ltd, Taiwan; part numbers CPLA-3K206-AB / CPLA-3K207-AB / JK6M-3K206-AA among ~20 listed). Crack can lead to detachment of the upper control arm; detachment = driver inability to control the vehicle
- Estimated percentage with defect in 25V514: **100%** (Part 573 filing)
- Remedy under 25V514: knuckles with a visible fracture get a new knuckle of substantially similar design; knuckles with no visible fracture get a brace attached to the upper portion, whose stated purpose is to prevent complete separation should a fracture develop
- Earlier recall 24V840 (November 7, 2024, JLR N759) covered the same knuckle concern; 25V514 expands and replaces it
- Owner letters for 25V514: expected September 30, 2025 per the recall filing; owner reports say letters did not go out until May 11, 2026 (secondary source: lemon-law attorney page tracking the campaign)
- 24V840 remedy: owners reported parts unavailable for nearly a year after the November 2024 recall (owner complaints filed with NHTSA)

## Fresh owner reports (VOQs via carproblemzoo, NHTSA complaint text)
- **October 10, 2026** — 2016 Range Rover: "Steering knuckle cracked and became disconnected from car while driving straight on the highway during normal driving conditions." Owner had received 25V514 notification; repair part not yet available.
- **July 27, 2026** — 2016 Range Rover on SR-62: vehicle unexpectedly moved laterally and collided with the left-rear of a commercial tractor-trailer. After the crash, the right-front upper suspension/steering-knuckle connection was "catastrophically separated and displaced," wheel and hub out of normal position. Vehicle was subject to unrepaired 25V514 recall. Police report attributed movement to an unsafe lane change; no component-level mechanical inspection was performed and the recalled knuckle condition was not evaluated.
- Multiple 2016 owners report 25V514 notification received but "part to do the recall repair was unavailable" months later.

## The recall/reality gap (original calculation)
| | Vehicles |
|---|---|
| EA26003 investigation population (2014-2022) | 331,559 |
| Recall 25V514 population (2014-2017) | 121,509 |
| Outside the recall, inside the investigation | 210,050 (63.3%) |
- NHTSA's own resume says the 2018-2022 knuckles are "substantially similar" in design to the recalled 2014-2017 parts, and the two most recent fracture VOQs at EA opening were 2020 and 2021 vehicles. The recall line at 2017 is a production-date boundary (knuckles sourced from SAI Taiwan Oct 2014 - end of 2017 MY), not a design boundary.

## The misattribution problem (original analysis)
- The July 27, 2026 SR-62 crash is the failure mode in the wild: a knuckle separates, the wheel goes out of position, the vehicle moves laterally into adjacent traffic. The police report recorded driver error. No component inspection followed. FARS and police crash data code what officers see at the scene; a steering knuckle that failed a second before impact looks exactly like a lane-change error. Every crash in the ODI resume's count (1) is therefore a floor, not a total — crashes caused by this defect are systematically undercounted whenever the part is not inspected.
- ODI's own resume records only 1 crash among 236 incidents and 0 injuries. That is the number to print, with the caveat above attached.

## The brace remedy (original analysis)
- For the majority of recalled vehicles (no visible fracture at inspection), the remedy is not a new knuckle. It is a retaining bracket bolted to the upper portion of the same casting. JLR's stated purpose: prevent complete separation should a fracture develop. The brace concedes the crack may come; it only limits what the crack can do. A fractured knuckle gets replaced with a new knuckle "of a substantially similar design" — the same design family that cracked.
- EA26003 explicitly evaluates the 25V514 remedies. If ODI finds the brace inadequate or the replacement design still crack-prone, the EA can end in a recall expansion to the remaining 210,050 vehicles.

## Limitations (must appear in article)
- ODI's failure summary counts 236 incidents with 1 crash and 0 injuries as of April 2026; the September letter cites 41 crack reports. These are different cuts of the data (all incidents vs. crack-specific reports) and should not be conflated.
- The July 27, 2026 crash account comes from an owner-filed VOQ (via carproblemzoo's republication of NHTSA complaint text), not from a police or NHTSA finding; the knuckle's role in that crash is alleged, not adjudicated.
- The May 11, 2026 owner-letter date comes from a secondary source (lemon-law attorney page); the Part 573 filing projected September 30, 2025. Present the gap as reported, not as NHTSA-confirmed.
- The 100% estimated defect rate in the Part 573 filing is JLR's estimate for the defined recall population, not a measured failure rate — most of those knuckles have not cracked.
- A crack does not equal a crash: most reported incidents are discovered at inspection or service, and the brace remedy may in fact contain the failure mode. The EA exists precisely because the data is contradictory (ODI's words: "Much of the information is contradictory").

## Strongest counterargument (must appear in article)
- The honest defense of the status quo: 236 incidents across 331,559 vehicles is a 0.07% incident rate; one crash and zero injuries in the federal file; the recall already covers the model years with the most failures; and the brace is a legitimate engineering containment, not a dodge — aircraft and bridges use redundant load paths the same way. JLR's own chronology notes Canadian regulators pushed for the recall even though, in JLR's words, there was "no field evidence suggesting that this issue causes a serious loss of steering control." The counter-read is that this is a slow, well-managed containment of a rare casting defect, and the EA is the system working as designed. The reply: the containment only covers 37% of the vehicles with the part, the newest failures are in the uncovered years, and a 2016 owner whose knuckle disconnected on the highway in October 2026 was still waiting on a part a year after the recall.

## Actionable takeaway
- If you own a 2014-2022 Range Rover or Range Rover Sport: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for 25V514 (and 24V840). If your 2018-2022 is not recalled, there is no fix to get yet — but ask your dealer to inspect the front upper knuckle joint at every service, and photograph it. A crack at the upper clevis is visible before it separates.
- If you own a 2014-2017 in the recall: do not accept "part unavailable" as a final answer. The recall is a year old; press the dealer for the bracket at minimum, and do not drive on a knuckle with a visible crack.
- If your Range Rover ever veers or the steering goes light: stop, do not drive on, and demand a component-level inspection — a separated knuckle can be written up as driver error if nobody looks at the part.
