The Truck That Wasn't: Washington Says Your Crossover Is a Car

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 9, 2026

Washington is about to announce its rewrite of the federal fuel-economy rules, and tucked inside the draft is a taxonomic revolution: your crossover is not a truck anymore. In a public filing, GM told regulators that 70 percent of new light-duty vehicles currently count as trucks under the rules, and the new classification method would roughly reverse that ratio, dumping millions of family haulers into the passenger-car bin, where the 2031 target is 37.4 miles per gallon instead of the truck target's 28.6.

0.19
Deaths per 100M miles for the Toyota RAV4, the crossover "truck" NHTSA wants to demote. Toyota's Camry, a certified passenger car, posts 2.03.

Pull the FARS ledger and the joke writes itself. Vehicles that Washington wants to demote from truck to car are the safest things on the road per mile driven. A RAV4 posts 0.19 deaths per 100 million vehicle-miles, while a Camry, a certified, card-carrying passenger car, posts 2.03, roughly ten times worse. CR-V (0.53), Rogue (0.35), Equinox (0.36) and Tucson (0.34) all run circles around Civic (2.25), Corolla (1.85) and Accord (3.07). By the only metric that ends in a body bag, the legal trucks are beating the legal cars.

Ford, naturally, objects, and its Exhibit A is the one crossover whose data cannot save it. The Explorer posts 1.54 deaths per 100 million miles, squarely in sedan territory, a number that either proves it deserves truck status or proves Ford should have built it better, depending on which funeral you're attending. In its public comment to NHTSA, the company defends the Explorer's truck classification by noting that Explorer-based police vehicles patrol beaches, marshes and intercoastal areas. Sit with that for a moment: the regulatory defense of a family SUV is that cops drive it on sand.

The administration's safety case is its own kind of beach patrol. When Brookings ran the numbers on the rule's claim that freezing standards avoids 12,700 fatalities, vehicle weight accounted for exactly 160 of them. Everything else comes from the rebound effect, the theory that cheaper-to-run cars get driven more, which Brookings describes as new and largely untested modeling. So 160 is the body count the safety argument actually stands on, clipboard in hand.

Still, the honest objection deserves full strength: none of this is about safety, and everyone involved knows it. A RAV4 that has never seen a dirt road enjoying truck-classified mileage targets is a decades-old regulatory fiction, and closing the loophole is arguably the correct move. Crossovers skew family-driven, suburban and lower-speed, while sedans skew younger and riskier, so that ten-to-one FARS gap is demographics wearing a vehicle costume. Ford's beach-patrol brief, meanwhile, is a transparent attempt to keep a family hauler in the laxer bin.

Caveats, because the ledger demands them: FARS rates are estimates built on travel-survey mileage rather than odometer readings, with roughly 15 percent wiggle on low-volume models, and fatal crashes only, which leaves the injury-only wrecks invisible. Reclassification is a fuel rule with second-order safety effects, since pushing the fleet lighter makes the two-vehicle crash math uglier for the smaller car, something IIHS has documented for years. When you shop, ignore the taxonomy: IIHS ratings and FARS per-model rates tell you more than any federal bin label, and a VIN check at nhtsa.gov/recalls is free. The car-versus-truck line is the most consequential fiction in American automotive regulation, and it is about to be rewritten by people who think your crossover is a car.
