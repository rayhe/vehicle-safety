The Gap

The Explorer and the Pilot Both Earned Five Stars. The Explorer Kills at Five Times the Rate.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
June 15, 2027

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. The 2026 Ford Explorer and the 2026 Honda Pilot carry identical lab credentials: NHTSA five stars overall, IIHS Top Safety Pick+.[2] Then federal crash data spoils the symmetry, because the Explorer kills at 1.54 deaths per 100 million miles while the Pilot kills at 0.29, a gap of more than five to one between the two three-row SUVs families cross-shop most.[1]

5.3x
The Explorer's per-mile death rate versus the Honda Pilot, its direct competitor

Look at the raw numbers and the symmetry breaks further: ten years of FARS data record 3,797 deaths in Explorers against 514 in Pilots, and the segment ranking reads like a report card the Explorer failed. Pilot 0.29, Acura MDX 0.30, Toyota Highlander 0.42, Jeep Grand Cherokee 0.51, Nissan Pathfinder 0.93, Lincoln Aviator 1.15, Ford Explorer 1.54, Chevrolet Tahoe 2.49. Nearly a ninefold spread inside one vehicle class, which is what the "same size, same safety" sales pitch is worth on the road.

That 1.54 deserves a second look, because it is worse than the Chevrolet Silverado (1.25) and the Ford F-150 (1.04). A family SUV, the vehicle you buckle car seats into, kills per mile like a pickup truck. Read that again: the laboratory says five stars, while the highway says it moves through traffic like 5,600 pounds of bad news.

So you ask the obvious question, because I did: is it the drivers? FARS keeps toxicology, and the answer is a dead heat. Explorer drivers in fatal crashes tested impaired 19.5 percent of the time; Pilot drivers 19.4 percent, with alcohol readings within a rounding error.[1] Drunk drivers do not explain this; delete that theory.

What might explain it is everything the lab cannot measure. The Explorer nameplate includes Ford's Police Interceptor Utility, a police-spec vehicle that logs high-speed pursuit miles and fleet duty no family SUV ever sees, and FARS does not separate the cruisers from the carpools. Beyond that, Explorer buyers skew younger and more male than Pilot buyers, and IIHS president David Harkey has said outright that a vehicle's image and marketing can shape how it gets driven, which is exactly how a well-engineered car ends up with a grim ledger.[3] The per-mile rates also carry roughly 15 percent uncertainty from estimated VMT, so treat the fifth decimal as decoration and the gap as real: the Pilot's halo may belong to Honda's buyers as much as Honda's steel, and no dataset here can split the difference.

Here is what to do with it: if you are shopping three-row SUVs, the road-data leaders are the Pilot (0.29), the MDX (0.30), and the Highlander (0.42), while the trailers are the Explorer (1.54) and the Tahoe (2.49). If you already own an Explorer, the gap sits in crash occurrence, not crashworthiness, which means speed and following distance buy you more protection than the badge does, and this nameplate carries one of the deepest recall benches in the industry, so check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before your next oil change.[4] Buying used? Eighty-seven percent of Explorer deaths in this dataset are from 2000-2010 model years; whatever the mechanism, the oldest Explorers carry the worst history. Stars and Top Safety Pick grades measure the laboratory; FARS measures the road, so check both before you sign.
