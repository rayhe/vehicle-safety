NHTSA Retired the Rollover Test Nobody Fails. Your Rollover Rating Is Now a Math Problem.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 22, 2026

The numbers don't lie, but they do occasionally smirk. NHTSA has proposed retiring the dynamic rollover test, the infamous Fishhook maneuver that simulates a panicked driver jerking the wheel, because no vehicle has tipped up in it for 15 years. Rollovers still account for roughly 28 percent of passenger vehicle occupant deaths, in barely 2 to 3 percent of crashes.[1][5] Read that twice. The test that cannot be failed is being buried. The thing it tested is very much alive.

28%
Share of passenger vehicle occupant fatalities involving rollover crashes, against under 3% of crashes

The proposal landed in the Federal Register on August 17 as FR Doc 2026-16735, docket NHTSA-2026-1651, signed by Administrator Jonathan Morrison, comments closed October 16.[1] Two procedures get the axe: the Fishhook, and side airbag out-of-position testing, which checks what a side airbag does to an occupant sitting in the wrong spot.[3] NHTSA's case is simple and, on its own terms, honest. Stability control became mandatory with model year 2012 and cut fatal single-vehicle rollovers by more than 70 percent, so a dynamic maneuver test now measures a disaster the industry already stopped having.[5][6]

Starting with model year 2027, the rollover stars on the Monroney sticker will come from the static stability factor: track width divided by twice the center-of-gravity height. A ratio. Geometry homework. Nothing swerves, nothing tips, nobody's hands touch a wheel.

Now the part that smirks. NHTSA says retiring the tests frees staff and funding for higher-priority safety upgrades. In the very same notice, the agency rewrote its 10-year NCAP roadmap and pushed the promised upgrades, the modernized crashworthiness and crash-avoidance evaluations, roughly two years to the right. Cuts land with model year 2027. Replacements arrive in late 2029.[4] Consumer Reports filed comments on October 16 and said the quiet part in a courtroom voice: if the agency is reclaiming resources by streamlining legacy tests, those resources should accelerate the modernized tests, not postpone them.[2] CR also noted that U.S. NCAP has languished for years, with nearly every vehicle collecting four or five stars and buyers getting zero useful differentiation.[2] A dynamic test becomes a tape measure, and the savings get spent on a delay.

In NHTSA's defense: the Fishhook was genuinely vestigial. FMVSS 126 has its own stability test, so NCAP was duplicating a regulatory check at consumer-program expense, and running an unrepeatable maneuver nobody fails is theater, not oversight. But if the savings are real, they should buy something real. Right now the savings buy two years of nothing.

What to do: If you are shopping for a 2027 or later vehicle, understand that the rollover star is now a shape score, not a behavior score. Pickups carry the worst rollover share of any body type at 38 percent of occupant deaths, SUVs sit at 33 percent, cars at 20 percent.[5] Check IIHS roof strength ratings, because that test still involves actual crushing, and treat ESC as the safety feature that matters most. This is still a proposal, not a final rule, so the next checkpoint is NHTSA's response to the comments.

What this does not prove: This is a proposal, so the roadmap dates can still move, and blaming a two-year delay for future deaths would be arithmetic without a body count. The static stability factor is not snake oil; geometry genuinely predicts rollover propensity. ESC's 70-plus percent reduction applies to fatal single-vehicle rollovers, not every rollover scenario, and 2023 shares are the latest solid year available. The honest scandal is informational: the star on your sticker will describe your car's shape while saying nothing about what its electronics do mid-slide, and the tests that would have shown that just slid to 2029.
