The Gap

Harris County Killed 517 People Last Year. Per Crash, It Was One of the Safest Places in Texas to Wreck.

Axle McScatter • Data Visualization Editor
November 8, 2026

Harris County, Texas killed 517 people on the road in 2025, more than any other county in the state and more than Dallas and Bexar counties combined.[1] A crash in Harris County was still one of the least likely in Texas to kill you. Divide deaths by crashes and the deadliest county in the state turns into one of its safest places to wreck: 4.15 deaths per 1,000 crashes, against 6.16 statewide.[2]

4.15
Deaths per 1,000 crashes in Harris County, 2025, vs 24.49 in Polk County and 6.16 statewide (TxDOT CRIS data)

Polk County, population about 53,000, logged 1,021 crashes and 25 deaths in 2025, a rate of 24.49 per 1,000, nearly six times Harris County’s.[2] Rural Texas as a whole produced 22 percent of the state’s crashes and 45 percent of its deaths, with a lethality of 12.67 per 1,000 outside city limits against 4.34 inside, a gap of 2.9 times.[2] Harris County holds 20.1 percent of all Texas crashes and 13.6 percent of all Texas deaths, the exact inverse of rural Texas, and fifteen of the state’s 254 counties account for half of all traffic deaths while 27 counties recorded none at all.[1]

Houston city alone explains the volume. TxDOT’s 2025 Crashes in Cities and Towns report counts 60,317 crashes inside Houston city limits, 250 more than Dallas, Fort Worth, El Paso, and Arlington combined, at a pace of 165 a day, one every 8.7 minutes.[3] Fatal crashes numbered 273 and deaths 293, more than any other Texas city and more than Dallas (172 deaths) and Fort Worth (112) combined.[3] Houston’s fatal-crash rate, 4.53 per 1,000 crashes, sits below the statewide city average of 4.63 and well below Austin (8.52), Fort Worth (6.95), and Dallas (6.69), which means Houston’s crashes were less likely to be fatal than crashes in Austin, Dallas, or Fort Worth.[3] There were just far more of them.

Speed is the variable that splits the state cleanly. Crashes on 75-mph roads killed 21.50 people per 1,000, against 3.19 on 30-mph streets, a 6.7-times gap.[2] Rural roads posted 70 mph or higher ran 27.08 per 1,000, while urban streets at 35 mph or less ran 2.81, a 9.6-times gap, and the clock matters as much as the speedometer: 2 a.m. ran 19.45 deaths per 1,000 crashes against 2.86 at 4 p.m., so the quietest hour on Texas roads was nearly seven times deadlier per crash than the busiest.[2] Christmas Day was the deadliest single day of 2025, 905 crashes and 15 fatal, a rate of 16.57 per 1,000.

Pedestrians absorb the worst of the split. Houston recorded 99 pedestrian deaths and 10 cyclist deaths in 2025, and statewide Texas lost 724 pedestrians, 84 percent of them away from an intersection and 76 percent in the dark.[2][6] In 2024, Houston recorded more than 300 traffic deaths against 330 reported homicides, which puts the scale in terms the city already understands: traffic was closing in on murder as a cause of death in Houston.[6]

Give the paradox its proper discount before believing it. Harris County’s low lethality could be partly an artifact of reporting: Houston’s dense freeway network produces enormous numbers of low-speed fender-benders that inflate the crash denominator, while rural counties under-report minor crashes and deflate theirs, so the gap would narrow if rural minor crashes were reported at Houston’s rate. But the counterargument cannot erase the numerator, 517 real deaths is still the largest body count in Texas, and the speed ladder runs on the same reporting system, which shows lethality tracking speed rather than paperwork. Two independent cuts of the Houston numbers also agree: TxDOT’s Cities and Towns report counts 60,317 crashes and 293 deaths, while a later CRIS export counts 67,076 crashes and 304 deaths, and both land on the same 4.53 fatality rate.[3]

What to do with this depends on where you drive. In Houston, the danger is volume: 165 crashes a day means your odds of being in one are high even though most are survivable, so the fatal risk concentrates after dark, at 2 a.m., and on high-speed roads, not in rush-hour gridlock, since 4 p.m. is the busiest hour and one of the safest per crash. On rural Texas roads, a crash is 2.9 times more likely to kill you than one inside city limits, and 9.6 times more likely on a 70-mph rural road than on a 35-mph city street, so slowing down on two-lane highways is the single highest-value habit in the dataset. On foot, cross at intersections and wear something reflective after dark, because 84 percent of Texas pedestrian deaths happened away from intersections and 76 percent happened in the dark. And before assuming the big city is the dangerous place, check your own county in the TxDOT tables: in Texas, the deadliest crashes per mile happen where the fewest people live.
