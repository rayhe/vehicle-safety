Minnesota Stopped Writing Tickets. The Body Count Noticed.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 7, 2026

Minnesota's legislature ordered a study of 1.2 million traffic cases, and the University of Minnesota delivered an answer nobody in St. Paul wanted to read.[1] Police stopped writing tickets after COVID, and people started dying. Between 2019 and 2021, Minnesota traffic deaths jumped from 364 to 488, a 34% surge, while speeding citations fell 28% and seatbelt citations fell 53%.[2]

53%
Decline in Minnesota seatbelt citations, 2017-2022, the steepest enforcement collapse in the study (CTS 25-15)

Report CTS 25-15, published in January 2026, reads like an autopsy of a policy that quietly bled out. Overall citations fell 25% across the six-year window, largely after the pandemic. Speeding citations dropped 28%, but the enforcement behind them fell off a cliff, down more than 60%, which means the stops collapsed more than twice as fast as the tickets did. Impairment citations declined 12%, and seatbelt citations fell by more than half. Every one of those declines coincided with more fatal crashes, and the national numbers offer no alibi: American deaths rose 19% over the same two years, from 36,355 to 43,230, while Minnesota's rose 34%.[3] Minnesota did not follow the national trend; it lapped it.

Buried in the same dataset is a control group, and it ruins every excuse. Distracted driving was the one violation Minnesota enforced harder, with citations up 90% after the 2019 Hands-Free law took effect, and related crashes fell nearly 40%.[1] More than 20,000 distracted-driving citations a year, every year since 2019, and the crashes bent down, all under the same pandemic, the same staffing shortages, in the same state, while everywhere enforcement went down the body count went up.[4] That is a natural experiment running in both directions inside a single dataset, and it is the closest thing traffic safety gets to a controlled trial.

One more finding deserves a longer look, because it ties the whole mess together. Targeted speed enforcement, not targeted impairment enforcement, is what increased DWI citations.[1] Speed stops are how police find drunk drivers. Cut speed stops by 28% and you quietly cut drunk-driving enforcement too, which is exactly what the 12% impairment-citation decline shows. Speeding killed 11,288 Americans in 2024, 29% of all traffic fatalities, and NHTSA projects that number down 11% for 2025, so the national picture is finally improving.[5] Minnesota's problem was never that enforcement does not work. Its problem was that it stopped doing it.

Fairness requires the strongest case against this story, stated straight. Correlation is not causation, and COVID is the confounder that swallows the whole window. Traffic volumes cratered, speeds rose on emptier roads, and police pulled back from stops for staffing, health, and political reasons all at once. The study's own language is careful: citation declines "coincided with" more fatal crashes, not "caused" them. Minnesota deaths also fell to 446 in 2022 with no documented enforcement rebound, which cuts against a clean enforcement-causes-deaths story. All true, but the counterargument has a hole the study itself points out: distracted driving moved the opposite direction on both variables, in the same state, under the same constraints. If COVID explained everything, the control group would not exist.

What this piece did not prove, stated plainly. This is one state, 2017 to 2022, and the case records are citations and adjudications, not traffic stops, so the study measures enforcement output rather than patrol hours. No causal identification exists here: the study does not control for vehicle miles traveled, speed distribution, or vehicle mix. MnDOT and FARS disagree slightly on Minnesota's 2022 death count, 446 versus 444, a small definitional gap. Enforcement data ends in 2022, and national deaths have since fallen three straight years, so this article cannot say whether Minnesota's enforcement recovered with them.

What you can actually do with this. If you live in Minnesota, the Hands-Free law is the one proven lever in this dataset, so support keeping it funded, and stop treating a traffic stop as harassment theater, because the stop is the enforcement mechanism. If you drive anywhere, know that speed enforcement is the highest-leverage stop on the road, because speed stops are also how police find drunk drivers. And if your state publishes citation data, look at it. A 25% collapse in tickets with rising deaths is a policy choice wearing a pandemic's clothes, and it is checkable in public records.
