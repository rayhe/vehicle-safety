# Research — #1188: Railroad grade crossings (FRA 2025: 2,273 collisions, 286 dead)

**Slug:** `1188-grade-crossing-train-always-wins`
**Journalist:** Mia Crumplezone (Safety Engineering Editor — vehicle design analysis, safety tech; judgmental about bad design)
**Kicker:** The Gap
**Dateline target:** October 16, 2026

## Angle
The most dangerous intersection in America has no traffic light. It has a train. FRA data for 2025, publicized during September's 10th annual See Tracks? Think Train Week: approximately 2,273 collisions at public and private highway-rail grade crossings, killing 286 people and injuring 766. The physics is unforgiving — a train at 55 mph needs about a mile (17+ football fields) to stop after the emergency brakes go in — and human perception is broken: a train's big flat front creates a size-speed illusion that makes it look slower and farther than it is. Meanwhile the infrastructure is a lottery: Colorado alone has 960 passive crossings (crossbuck + yield sign, no lights, no gate) versus 784 active ones, and its PUC says funding to signalize crossings is limited. Awareness weeks are nice. Gates are better. The gap between the two is measured in bodies.

- **Novel angle?** Yes. Zero site coverage of grade crossings (one passing mention in a phantom-braking research file). New primary source (FRA, a DOT agency the site has never cited). ## Secondary/context
- The size-speed illusion: a train's large frontal area makes it appear to approach more slowly than it is — a known human-factors failure mode at passive crossings (widely documented in OLI materials).
- Federal rules require school buses and hazmat haulers to stop at every rail crossing (49 CFR 392.10) — the Sumter County driver allegedly violated both the signals and the professional standard.
- Quiet zones: trains are legally quieter in many communities now (no horn), which removes the last passive warning some crossings had.

## Original contribution (not just synthesis)
- The passive-crossing lottery framed as a design decision: Colorado's own numbers show most crossings in the state have no lights or gates, and the PUC openly says signalization money is limited. The site has never run those numbers.
- The awareness-vs-hardware take: See Tracks? Think Train Week is in its 10th year; the 2025 toll (286) is essentially unchanged from the long-run ~2,000–2,300 collisions/year plateau. A decade of education has not moved the number; gates do.
- The Sumter County case as the professional-driver failure: even the drivers legally required to stop at every crossing sometimes don't — which is an argument for engineering (gates) over training.

## Limitations
- FRA 2025 figures are "approximately" — final agency tabulations can revise; collisions span public and private crossings with different warning levels.
- Crossing-collision counts include vehicle-train and pedestrian-train events at crossings; the release does not break out occupant vs. pedestrian deaths.
- Colorado is one state; its passive/active ratio is illustrative, not national.
- The 10th-anniversary-week framing is mine; OLI does not claim education has failed.

## Strongest counterargument
286 deaths is 0.7% of ~40,000 annual traffic deaths — a rounding error next to speeding (10,035) or impairment. Most public crossings already have active warnings; the residual toll is dominated by deliberate risk-taking (driving around lowered gates), which no sign or slogan fixes and which gates only partly deter. Signalizing every rural passive crossing would cost billions to protect crossings that see a handful of cars a day — the benefit-cost math is genuinely hard. And quiet zones exist because communities asked for them; the horn was noise pollution with its own costs.

## Actionable insights
- **At any crossing:** never race the gate, never stop on the tracks. If your car stalls on the tracks, get out immediately; if a train is coming, run away from the tracks at a 45-degree angle (debris flies along the track line).
- **At passive crossings (no lights/gate):** treat the crossbuck like a stop sign — stop, look both ways, listen, then cross. The train looks slower than it is; that is your visual cortex lying to you.
- **If you drive a school bus or hazmat hauler:** the stop-at-every-crossing rule exists because of drivers like Sumter County's. Open the door, look, listen — every time.
- **If you live near a passive crossing:** petition your city/county for active warnings; PUC signalization funds exist but are limited and competitive — crossings with documented near-misses get priority.
