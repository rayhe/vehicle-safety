Nissan's Miracle Engine Has Been Recalled Three Times. The Warranty Lasts Ten Years.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 24, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again. They didn't get better. In 2019, Nissan shipped the first mass-market engine that changes its own compression ratio on the fly, a genuine engineering flex that debuted in the Infiniti QX50 and became the sole powertrain of America's favorite crossover, the Rogue. Seven years later, that engine has been through a federal investigation, three recall campaigns covering hundreds of thousands of vehicles, and two separate warranty extensions, and the official repair procedure begins with a mechanic staring into your oil pan looking for metal shavings.[1]

13%
Share of Nissan's own estimated bad-bearing engine population already claimed under warranty by February 2026 (690 claims against ~5,328 suspect engines), with the fleet still young.

Start with the timeline, because the intervals are the story. A federal investigation into VC-Turbo engine failures opened in 2023. In June 2025, NHTSA campaign 25V437 recalled roughly 444,000 vehicles (2021-2024 Rogue, 2019-2020 Altima, 2019-2022 QX50, 2022 QX55) for defective main, A-, C-, and L-link bearings that can wreck the engine block, leak oil, and start fires.[2] Then February 2026 brought two more campaigns at once: 26V080, another bearing-failure recall for 2023-2025 Rogues, and 26V081, a separate recall because a software defect weakens the throttle body's internal gears until they fracture. In March 2026, Nissan extended the engine warranty to 10 years or 120,000 miles for the second time.[3] Investigation to recall took 18 months, recall to dual recall took 7, and dual recall to warranty extension took 1. When the fix cadence collapses like that, the problem is accelerating, not resolving.

Now the arithmetic Nissan filed with the government. For the 444,000-vehicle recall, Nissan told NHTSA that only about 1.2% of engines may actually have bad bearings, which sounds reassuring until you multiply: 1.2% of 444,000 is 5,328 engines that Nissan itself expects to be defective. By mid-February 2026, Nissan had already received 690 warranty claims tied to engine failures, roughly 13% of its own suspect population, while insisting less than 1% of the affected vehicles had failed.[4] Thirteen percent of the expected failures have already happened, and these cars average maybe four years old, while bearings fail with heat cycles and mileage. The worst of this curve is still ahead of us, not behind.

NHTSA's complaint database agrees with the pessimists: I pulled the component counts for the 2023 Rogue, the first full model year of VC-Turbo exclusivity, and found 130 complaints tagged ENGINE and 108 tagged ENGINE AND ENGINE COOLING, the two largest buckets by a wide margin, dwarfing everything from wipers to transmissions.[5] And the remedy deserves a moment of silence. For the bearing defect, dealers inspect the oil pan for metal debris, where debris means a new engine and no debris means an oil change, a gasket, and an ECU reflash. Read that again, because the diagnostic for a 2020s variable-compression powertrain is a visual search for metal confetti. Your engine either has glitter in it or it doesn't, and glitter means replacement.

The defense, stated at full strength, because numbers cut both ways. About 99% of these engines have not failed. Variable compression is real innovation, not a gimmick, and the 1.2% estimate means Nissan believes the vast majority of recalled cars need nothing. A 10-year, 120,000-mile warranty extension is one of the strongest powertrain backstops any automaker has offered, Nissan reimburses owners who already paid for repairs, and the complaint counts are owner-reported, which skews toward the vocal and the unlucky. If you own a Rogue that purrs, the data says it will probably keep purring. The recalls are the system working, and the warranty is genuinely generous.

What this means for you: if you drive a 2021-2025 Rogue, a 2019-2020 Altima, a 2019-2022 QX50, or a 2022 QX55, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and get the recall work done. The 10-year/120,000-mile extension covers only the recalled defects, and only if the vehicle is registered and maintained on Nissan's schedule, so keep service records like they are legal documents. Watch for the warning signs Nissan itself names: check-engine light, knocking or ticking, rough idle, oil pressure warnings. A knocking VC-Turbo is not a "monitor it" situation. And keep every repair receipt: Nissan's reimbursement plan covers prior out-of-pocket engine repairs inside the VIN range, which is real money coming back to owners who already paid.[6]
