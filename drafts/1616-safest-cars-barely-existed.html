The Safest Car in America Didn't Exist for Six of the Ten Years It Was Measured

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 28, 2026

Somewhere on this site, the Tesla Model Y holds a number that looks like a typo. Its death rate is 0.03 per 100 million miles, the lowest of any vehicle we track, about thirty times lower than the fleet-weighted average of 0.91. Fifty-seven people died in Model Ys across a decade of federal crash records, and on paper that makes it the safest car in America.[1] Paper is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Customer deliveries of the Model Y did not begin until March 13, 2020, which means the car was on sale for four of the ten years in the measurement window.[4] Our rate credits it with ten.

2.5×
How much the Model Y's published death rate understates the exposure-corrected rate: 0.03 becomes 0.07

Every rate on this site is built the same way. Count the deaths in FARS from 2014 through 2023. Estimate how many miles the fleet drove in that decade: fleet size times 12,500 miles a year times ten years. Divide. For a car that existed the whole decade, that arithmetic is honest. For a car that showed up in 2020, it is not. Six of the ten years in the denominator never happened for the Model Y, because there was no Model Y to drive them. Miles that were never driven cannot dilute deaths that did happen. I ran the numbers again with the calendar put back in. Corrected rate equals deaths divided by miles driven in only the years the model was actually on sale, and the arithmetic is simple enough to check on a phone: 57 deaths divided by (21,875 million miles times 4 years) times 100 gives 0.065, which rounds to 0.07.

Look at the top of that table and a pattern jumps out. Four lowest published rates in the high-volume set, and every one of them belongs to a car launched in 2017 or later. After correction, no model in the dataset sits below 0.07. None of those cars stops being safe, but every one of them stops being miraculous. One correction changes a buying decision outright. Kia's K5 sedan posts a published rate of 0.18, which reads as merely average, a little worse than a Mazda CX-5 at 0.12. K5s went on sale in 2020, so correcting for four years of exposure lands the K5 at 0.45, nearly four times the CX-5. Same car, same deaths, same roads. Only the calendar changed, and the gap between the two cars more than doubled.

IIHS solved this problem decades ago, which is the embarrassing part. Its driver death rates divide deaths by registered vehicle years, one vehicle registered for one year, and a model needs 100,000 of them to qualify for the list.[2] Registration counts are restricted to calendar years after the model year, so a car cannot earn credit for years it did not exist.[3] Our method does the opposite: it hands every model a full decade of driving on day one and never asks when the car was born. That is not a subtle methodological disagreement. It is the difference between measuring exposure and assuming it.

0.08
Subaru Crosstrek's death rate, earned over the full ten-year window with no correction needed

Correct the whole list and the podium barely moves, which is worth saying before anyone accuses me of burying the lede. Model Y and Model 3 tie at 0.07, still the lowest numbers in the dataset. But the most impressive number in the table belongs to a car nobody is writing songs about. Subaru's Crosstrek posts 0.08 with a full decade of exposure, no correction required, 86 deaths across ten years of real driving. It earned its number the hard way: by being there for all of it.

Give the objection its full weight. This correction matters most exactly where the numbers are smallest, and it changes no podium. A skeptic can fairly call it a second-decimal argument, and the raw extremes in our data, the Veloster at 8.54 and the Tracker at 7.83, had full-decade exposure, so they stand exactly as published. Fair. But this site ranks cars to two decimal places and readers use those rankings to choose what their families ride in. A 2.5-times error on the headline number is worth fixing even when it does not flip the winner. Two more caveats belong on the record. Fleet size is itself a binned estimate, 52 values for 337 models, as story #52 documented, so the exposure correction sits on top of that uncertainty.[5] And fleet ramp is a second bias in the same direction: a model whose fleet grew from tens of thousands to nearly two million during its window gets credited with the final fleet size for every year. Under a linear-ramp assumption the Model Y's true rate would roughly double again, to around 0.13. That is a sensitivity check, not a measurement, and I am labeling it as one.

What to do with this. If you are shopping and a death rate is quoted for a model launched after 2017, ask whether the source corrected for exposure. If it did not, the number flatters the car. Compare corrected rates instead: Model Y and Model 3 at 0.07, Crosstrek at 0.08, CX-5 at 0.12. And if you drive a K5, Seltos, or Venue, the number to carry in your head is the corrected one, 0.45, 0.10, 0.26, not the published one. Your car did not get more dangerous while you read this. It was never as safe as the published number said.
