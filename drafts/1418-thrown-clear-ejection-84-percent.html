Thrown Clear: 84% of Ejected Occupants Are Killed

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

In 2024, 5,072 vehicle occupants died after being thrown from their vehicles.[3] NHTSA’s October fact sheet puts a number on what happens next: 84 percent of passenger-vehicle occupants totally ejected in fatal crashes were killed.[1] That is not a crash statistic, it is a containment statistic.

A seat belt is not a cushion, it is a tether, and its first job is keeping you inside the car while the car does the violent part. Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds: the belt locks, the body stays put, and the cabin absorbs the energy instead of the pavement. Unbelted, those same 150 milliseconds end with a body in free flight, and a body in free flight meets the road, a tree, or its own vehicle coming back down on top of it.

Federal data makes the gap almost absurd. Among occupants in 2024 fatal crashes, 24 percent of the unrestrained were totally ejected, against 1 percent of the belted.[1] Seat belts cut the risk of fatal injury by 45 percent for front-seat car occupants and 60 percent for front-seat light-truck occupants, and the ejection numbers explain much of how that protection works.[1] Light trucks eject at a higher rate than cars: 22.7 percent of light-truck occupant deaths involved ejection, against 16.1 percent for passenger cars.[3] Pickups pair the worst ejection odds with the worst belt habits, since 59 percent of pickup drivers killed in 2024 were unrestrained, against 47 percent of car drivers.[1]

Some good news hides in the same tables. Ejected passenger-vehicle occupant deaths fell by 537 in 2024, an 11 percent drop and the steepest improvement of any category in NHTSA’s annual overview.[2] Total traffic deaths fell 4.3 percent over the same period, rollover deaths fell 5.7 percent, and unrestrained deaths fell 7.4 percent.[2] Ejection still accounted for one in five vehicle-occupant deaths, 5,072 of 24,473, so the improvement is a dent, not a solution.[3]

Fairness demands the other side of the ledger. Ejection tracks the worst crashes: rollovers and high-speed impacts that might have killed the occupant anyway, belted or not. Unrestrained occupants also differ from belted ones in ways that have nothing to do with webbing: younger, more often impaired, more often speeding, more often out after dark, when 56 percent of killed occupants were unrestrained against 41 percent in daylight.[1] None of that rescues the alternative, because among occupants in fatal crashes, the belted almost never leave the vehicle and the unbelted leave it one time in four, and the 84 percent kill rate applies to the ones who leave.

Buckle every seat on every trip, including the back seat, where 58 percent of killed second-row occupants were unrestrained.[1] If you drive a pickup, the belt matters more, not less: your truck rolls more easily, ejects more often, and its drivers buckle least. Wear the belt correctly, shoulder strap across the chest and never behind your back or under an arm, because a misrouted belt cannot contain you. Check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls, and remember the number that matters is not the crash, it is whether you stay in the car.
