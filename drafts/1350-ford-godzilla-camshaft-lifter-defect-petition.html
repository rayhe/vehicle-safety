Ford’s Godzilla V8 Allegedly Idles at 6 PSI. One Fleet Lost Five Engines.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 31, 2026

Let’s talk about oil pressure, because everything else in this story is downstream of it. According to a defect petition that pushed NHTSA to open a federal file last week, Ford’s 7.3-liter “Godzilla” V8 can see pressure sag to about six psi at a warm idle[1]. At six psi, the lifter rollers and camshaft lobes stop floating on an oil film and start eating each other. Keep driving, and a work truck can lose its engine at highway speed, with nothing on the dash to warn you.

6 psi
Alleged warm-idle oil pressure in Ford’s 7.3L Godzilla V8. A healthy pushrod V8 should be holding several times that

The file is DP26011, opened October 23 by NHTSA’s Office of Defects Investigation[2]. It names an estimated 54,787 vehicles: the 2021 and 2022 Ford E-350, the 2021 F-59 stripped chassis, and the 2021 F-750[2]. The underlying petition landed June 13 (ODI number 11764421)[3], and the agency now has roughly until mid-October to decide whether to grant it and start a formal probe, or deny it[3]. So far the agency’s own tally counts one incident, zero crashes, zero injuries[1]. A defect petition is the first rung of NHTSA’s ladder, not a finding of anything.

Now the part that made my eyebrows go up. A North Carolina fleet operator told the petitioners that of eleven trucks running the 7.3-liter V8, five needed complete new engines, with failures arriving anywhere from 45,745 to 103,177 miles[4]. And then there’s the allegation that Ford knew: the petition claims the company quietly released a powertrain software calibration that bumps idle speed to roughly 1,000 rpm, a move that raises oil pressure and papers over the wear, without notifying owners or launching a recall[5]. A software update that treats the symptom while the cause goes unnamed in any owner letter. If that’s accurate, Ford shipped the confession and forgot the apology.

Some context from our own data, because nobody else will do this math for you. In The Crash Report’s FARS 2014–2023 by-model set, the Ford E-350 has a fatality rate of 2.51 deaths per 100 million miles, the highest of any van in the dataset and roughly eighteen times the Ford Transit’s 0.14[7]. That does not prove the valvetrain defect kills people; it proves the E-350 is already one of the most dangerous vans on the road by the numbers, and it is exactly the vehicle family now under a federal microscope for losing motive power at speed. Add a defect that can stall a loaded van on a freeway to a platform that already scores worst-in-class, and the stakes of DP26011 get a lot easier to see.

Fairness check, because the file is thin. NHTSA’s tally shows one ODI incident against 54,787 vehicles, with zero crashes, fires, injuries, or fatalities[1]. The five-engine fleet is one operator’s experience, not a failure rate, and the silent-patch story is the petitioner’s allegation; Ford has not publicly confirmed why the idle-speed calibration exists. It is also worth noting that the 7.3-liter Godzilla runs in hundreds of thousands of Super Duty pickups, which this file does not name. A petition is an accusation with a docket number, and the feds deny them all the time.

What to do about it. If you run a 2021–2022 E-350, a 2021 F-59, or a 2021 F-750 with the 7.3-liter gas V8, punch the VIN into nhtsa.gov/recalls and understand that nothing will come up: DP26011 is a petition, not a recall[8]. Watch your idle oil pressure if the truck has a gauge. After any engine failure, file a complaint with ODI and keep every service record, because petitions live or die on owner complaints. And ask your dealer, in writing, whether the PCM idle-speed calibration has been applied to your truck, and what Ford said it was for. If a software patch exists that admits the idle is the problem, the least a fleet owner deserves is a straight answer about it.
