The Government Killed the Truck Speed Limiter in the Name of Safety. Its Own Math Said 214 Lives a Year.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
April 7, 2027

Nineteen years is a long time to study a speedometer. In 2006, an advocacy group called Road Safe America asked the federal government to cap how fast heavy trucks can go. What followed was a 2007 public notice, three thousand comments, a granted petition in 2011, a 150,000-truck safety study in 2012, a formal rule proposal in 2016, seven thousand more comments, a shelving, a revival, fifteen thousand more comments, and then, in July 2025, a two-page obituary. Their own analysis said a 65-mile-per-hour cap would save 63 to 214 lives a year.[1] The government killed it anyway, citing safety.

214
Lives per year the agencies' own 2016 analysis said a 65-mph limiter would save, top of range; killed in July 2025.

This paper trail reads like a cold case file. In September 2016, NHTSA and FMCSA jointly proposed requiring speed limiters on every truck rated over 26,000 pounds, and asked whether the cap should be 60, 65, or 68.[2] Their draft environmental assessment ran the numbers: at 60, the model said 162 to 498 lives a year; at 65, 63 to 214, plus 70 to 236 serious injuries prevented; at 68, 27 to 96.[1] Net benefits at 65 mph: $1.04 to $2.76 billion a year, then the file went quiet: Trump's first administration shelved it, and Biden's administration revived it in May 2022. Fifteen thousand commenters showed up, overwhelmingly opposed.[3] In June 2025, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy announced the rule's death as part of a "pro-trucker" package. On July 24, 2025, Federal Register notice 2025-13928 made it official, withdrawing both the 2016 proposal and the 2022 revival.[4]

Read the obituary closely, because the reasoning is the story. In the notice, the agencies cited "significant policy and safety concerns and continued data gaps that create considerable uncertainty about the estimated costs, benefits, and other impacts of the proposed rule."[4] In plain language: we no longer trust our own math. They added that the proposal “lacks a sufficiently clear and compelling safety justification.” Then came the load-bearing sentence: rear-end crashes where the truck is the striking vehicle, they wrote, "could be reduced by crash avoidance technologies designed to mitigate or prevent such crashes, such as automatic emergency braking (AEB) and forward collision warning (FCW)."[5] The plan, in other words: skip the speed cap and let the robots brake instead.

One problem: the robots are not coming, not soon anyway. Unlike the light-vehicle AEB rule finalized in April 2024, the heavy-truck version was never published in the Federal Register.[6] The 2026 regulatory agenda promised a new supplemental proposal, with a final rule "well into 2027 or 2028" at the earliest, according to July 2026 reporting.[7] The 2023 draft gave Class 7 and 8 trucks three years after the effective date to comply. Do the addition: the overlap technology the withdrawal leaned on goes live around 2030 or 2031. Meanwhile NHTSA's investigation into AEB false activations, opened in 2023, was upgraded to a full engineering analysis in October 2025 and remains open.[7] Run the agencies' own midpoint, 139 lives a year, across a five-and-a-half-year gap. That is roughly 765 lives of modeled benefit, traded for a system that does not exist yet. That is upper-bound arithmetic, labeled as such, and it still smirks.

Physics never filed a comment, but kinetic energy scales with the square of velocity, which means an 80,000-pound truck at 75 miles per hour carries about 25 percent more destructive energy than the same truck at 65. Exactly: 24.9 percent; at 68 versus 75, it is 17.8 percent. Every argument about differentials and flow and federalism has to survive that ratio. Speed does not explain every truck crash, but it does supply the exponent.

Now the honest discount, because the advocates' number is doing quiet work. That 63-to-214 range was modeled against a world where nobody governs their trucks. We do not live in that world. A fleet survey found 85 percent of fleets already cap 100 percent of their trucks, most commonly at 65, and limiter hardware has been standard since the early 1990s.[8] So the mandate's real marginal target is the ungoverned tail, maybe 15 percent of the fleet. Scale the midpoint linearly, crudely: about 21 lives a year. That is not nothing, but it is also not 214. And the crash geography cooperates with the skeptics: in 2012-2014, only about 18 percent of fatal large-truck crashes happened in 70-to-75 zones, while roughly 56 percent happened where the posted limit was 65 or below, the roads a 65 cap barely touches.[9]

Steel-man the other side, because it has a real theory. The Owner-Operator Independent Drivers Association argues that variance kills more reliably than velocity: a governed truck in 80-mph flow is a rolling chicane, and their data says a vehicle running 10 mph under the posted limit interacts with other traffic 227 percent more often.[9] Julie Anna Cirillo, who spent 34 years at DOT including a turn as FMCSA's chief safety officer, told a Senate committee in 2003 that "traffic operating at or about the same speed, regardless of speed limit, is the safest traffic environment," and that regulators "should never require the opposite."[2] OOIDA further notes that the famous 2012 study's first draft admitted researchers "could not definitely attribute the effect to the presence of an active" limiter, and the headline "strong positive benefits" arrived in a second final draft that collected no new data.[10] If the differential literature is right, withdrawing the rule did not cost lives. It prevented a mandate that would have manufactured conflict points on every interstate in America.

What this does not prove fills a paragraph of its own. That 63-to-214 band is 2016 modeling, not observed fact, and the counterfactual of a national mandate is unobservable. All 5,340 large-truck deaths recorded in 2024 happened under today's voluntarily governed fleet, so the midpoint is not a body count the withdrawal caused.[11] The AEB timeline is a regulatory-agenda projection; agendas slip. OOIDA's speed-zone table covers 2012-2014, and posted limits have risen since. The withdrawal notice is quoted here from contemporaneous trade-press accounts of Federal Register 2025-13928; the Register's own site blocked automated retrieval, so the citation is given for anyone who wants to check it directly. That 21-a-year discount assumes benefits scale linearly with the ungoverned share, which is rough on purpose.

What you can actually do starts with the obvious. If you run a fleet, govern at 65 anyway; the 2012 data says governed trucks crash less, mandate or not. If you drive a car, manage the differential: do not linger alongside a governed truck, pass decisively or drop back, because a 15-mile-per-hour closing speed is where the risk lives. If you care about the policy, the live docket is heavy-truck AEB; when the supplemental proposal lands, that comment period is where this nineteen-year argument continues. Nineteen years, two agencies, tens of thousands of comments. The speedometer is still uncapped, the robots are still coming, and the only date on the calendar is 2030.
