946 Failed Engines and One Extension Request: GM's 6.2L Deadline Is November 13

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 2, 2026

On September 15, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation sent General Motors a formal information request in the 6.2L V8 investigation — Engineering Analysis EA26005 — and it opened with a number every owner of a Silverado, Sierra, Tahoe, Yukon, Suburban, or Escalade should know: 946 reports of L87 engine failure in vehicles that already went through the 2025 recall repair or were built after its production window.[1]

946
L87 engine-failure reports in NHTSA's file — nearly double the count when the investigation opened seven weeks earlier

The letter asks six numbered questions, and none of them are softballs.[1] GM has to hand over VIN-level production data for every subject truck and SUV (engine part number and design version at the factory, build date, recall completion date, which remedy it got), counts of every complaint, field report, crash, fire, property-damage claim, arbitration, and lawsuit tied to the defect, per-incident detail with mileage at failure, every paid warranty claim with labor operation numbers and diagnostic codes, and — my favorite — GM's own assessment of the causal factors, the failure mechanism, and what warning a driver actually gets before the engine lets go. Falling short on a response exposes GM to civil penalties of up to $27,874 per violation per day, with a ceiling just shy of $140 million. For a company, that is not a nudge; that is an invoice-shaped threat.

The interesting part is the calendar: GM's answers were due October 30, and on October 23, GM asked for more time. On October 25, NHTSA posted a memo granting it — partially.[2] The VIN-level production data was still due October 30. Everything built on field data — the failure counts, the incident detail, the documents, the warranty records, GM's own defect assessment — now comes due November 13, 2026, eleven days from this dateline.

GM's reason, as recorded in NHTSA's memo, is the single most quotable sentence in the federal file: "GM is still analyzing the field data due to the expanded interpretation of the Alleged Defect from the RQ to the EA."[1] Translation: when this was a narrow recall query in January, the question was small. When NHTSA upgraded it to a full engineering analysis in August, the definition of the defect got wider — and GM is still re-running its complaint, warranty, and field data against the new definition. The regulator redefined the question faster than the manufacturer could answer it. Eighteen months after the recall, the company still cannot tell the government what the failure mechanism is. That is the story, right there in one sentence.

Now the arithmetic, because the complaint-count trajectory deserves its own paragraph. In January, the recall query opened on 36 owner complaints of post-remedy failures. When the engineering analysis opened on August 20, ODI had 499. By the September 15 information request, it was 946 — nearly double in seven weeks, while the vehicles sat under active federal scrutiny.[3] Whatever else you think of this investigation, the reporting rate is accelerating under the spotlight, not tapering. The remedy is failing faster the harder the feds look.

What this article did not prove, honestly stated: the 946 reports are owner complaints to NHTSA — self-reported, unverified, not 946 confirmed engine seizures, and some fraction will turn out to be unrelated failures. The September letter and the October memo are demands for data, not findings; they tell you what the government wants to know, not what it has concluded. These two documents carry no new crash or injury counts, so don't borrow August's numbers and pretend they live here. GM's delay rationale reaches us through NHTSA's memo — the agency's paraphrase of GM's position, not GM's own public statement. And engineering analyses routinely close with no new recall. November 13 is a homework deadline, not a verdict date; the actual decision on the remedy realistically lands in 2027.[1]

The strongest case against getting excited: 946 complaints across roughly a million vehicles is under one-tenth of one percent, and complaint counts inflate once press coverage tells owners the filing exists. Two-week extensions are routine in federal investigations; reading panic into a partial paperwork extension is over-reading. The widened defect definition is standard regulator caution — ODI widening the net before deciding, which is the process working, not GM stalling. And that cuts both ways: if the defect gets defined broadly enough, any manufacturer's data will look thin. NHTSA is asking questions, and it may never demand one. It has not found an answer.

What owners should actually do, since the queue articles always bury the lede on this: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 25V-274 (GM N252494000).[4] This is an engine investigation, not a model investigation — confirm the RPO L87 on your glovebox sticker, because a Silverado with the 5.3L is not in it. Keep the paperwork NHTSA just demanded from GM, for your own truck: recall completion date, which remedy branch you got (0W-40 oil change or engine replacement), every repair order with labor operation numbers and part numbers, dated oil-change receipts. Those are literally the fields the government asked for. Know the warning signs from GM's own filings — knocking or banging, check-engine light, hesitation, reduced propulsion, no-start — and if a knocking L87 is your situation, tow it; don't nurse it down the highway to see if it clears up. If your engine already failed after the recall repair, file the complaint yourself at nhtsa.gov, because the 946 reports are the entire reason this investigation exists, and post-remedy failures are exactly what question two asks GM to count. The homework is due November 13, the verdict comes in 2027, and until it lands, keep your records.
