# Research: #985 — Kia Rio deadliest, Good ratings

Journalist: Rex Driverton (Senior Crash Correspondent). Kicker: "Investigation".
Slug: 985-kia-rio-deadliest-good-ratings
Working title: "The Deadliest Car in America Aces Its Crash Tests"

## Kill test
Genuinely newsworthy? Yes. The IIHS's July 2026 driver-death-rate study — the industry's gold-standard real-world outcome metric — names the Kia Rio the single deadliest vehicle in America: 170 driver deaths per million registered vehicle years, 4.6x the all-vehicle average of 37. Yet the same institute's lab gives the Rio Good ratings in moderate overlap, driver-side small overlap, and side crash; it was once a Top Safety Pick. Nobody on this site has touched the Rio (0 stories, 0 drafts, 0 queue items), and the paradox — lab-Good, road-deadliest — is the exact kind of contradiction Rex lives for. The study is two months old, the finding is current, and the actionable read (crash tests are within-class; the road is not) generalizes to every subcompact shopper.

## The paradox (verified)
- IIHS driver death rates, 2021-24, MY2023 and equivalent earlier models: Kia Rio 170 deaths/million registered vehicle years — highest of any model evaluated. All-vehicle average: 37. (primary: IIHS news release, July 2026)
- IIHS lab ratings, 2023 Kia Rio 4-door hatchback: moderate overlap Good, small overlap driver-side Good, side crash Good, roof strength Good. Passenger-side small overlap Acceptable (head barely contacted airbag, slid off right side — Marginal kinematics). (primary: iihs.org/ratings/vehicle/kia/rio-4-door-hatchback/2023)
- The Rio was discontinued in the US after MY2023; the 2021-24 IIHS window covers the 2018-2023 generation.

## FARS cross-tab (our data, 2014-2023)
- Kia Rio: 268 deaths, 367 crashes, rate 1.07 per 100M VMT (fleet 218,750; VMT 2,516M). Annual: 26.8 deaths/yr.
- Toxicology: 1,009 drivers in fatal crashes; any-impaired 21.5% (alcohol 16.7%, drug 8.4%). Above the ~19-20% FARS average.
- Cheap-subcompact peer rates (FARS per-VMT): Chevy Spark 1.28, Kia Rio 1.07, Ford Fiesta 1.02, Nissan Versa 0.90, Hyundai Accent 0.80, Toyota Yaris 0.76, Mitsubishi Mirage 0.40.
- Note the divergence: FARS per-VMT puts the Spark ahead of the Rio; IIHS per-registered-year puts the Rio #1 nationally. Different denominators, different answers — the article must explain why (see Counterargument).

## Why the lab and the road disagree (evidence-backed)
1. **Mass.** The Rio weighs ~2,700 lbs. IIHS crash tests compare within class; real roads pair it against 5,000-lb SUVs and pickups. IIHS's own study: large luxury SUVs had the lowest driver death rates; "even the safest small cars don't fare well in crashes with large pickups and heavy SUVs" (David Harkey, IIHS president). Physics, not engineering failure.
2. **Demographics survive adjustment.** IIHS adjusts its rates for driver age and gender — and the Rio is STILL #1. So "it's just young drivers" doesn't fully explain it (though unadjusted factors remain: income, geography, road type).
3. **Safety-tech poverty.** Per Consumer Reports' 2023 Rio ratings: forward collision warning optional, automatic emergency braking optional (city/highway/pedestrian all optional), blind-spot warning not available, adaptive cruise not available. The crash-prevention tech that is standard on a $30k car was a checkbox on the $15k one. The Rio's Good ratings measure crashworthiness; nothing rated its ability to avoid the crash.
4. **Impairment.** 21.5% of Rio drivers in FARS fatal crashes were impaired — above average, consistent with a cheap-car/high-risk-driver correlation.

## Price-of-safety gradient (original calculation)
IIHS's top-10 worst list clusters on price: Rio (~$16k new), Versa (~$17k), Mirage (~$17k), Forte (~$20k), K5 (~$26k)... against an all-vehicle average of 37, the Rio's 170 is a 4.6x multiple. The cheapest seats in America carry the highest death rates. The Rio's 2023 MSRP range per Consumer Reports: $14,450-$15,450 — the lowest base price of any new car sold in the US that year.

## Counterargument (full strength)
IIHS's metric is deaths per registered vehicle year, not per mile driven. The Rio is a classic low-mileage second car and commuter runabout; per-year rates inflate relative to per-mile rates for vehicles driven less. FARS's per-VMT rate (1.07) puts the Rio mid-pack among subcompacts — the Chevy Spark (1.28) looks worse by that denominator. The Rio's Good lab ratings are real: in the crashes it was designed for, it protects occupants well. The death rate reflects exposure — what it collides with, who drives it, where — more than engineering failure. And IIHS adjusts for age and gender but not income, geography, or road type; residual demographic confounding is real and unmeasured. A rich retiree's Rio and a 19-year-old's Rio are different risk pools wearing the same badge.

## Limitations
- FARS VMT estimates for low-volume models carry roughly ±15% uncertainty; the 1.07 rate is approximate.
- IIHS death rates are per registered vehicle year (2021-24, MY2023-equivalent), FARS rates are per 100M VMT (2014-23, all model years) — the two cannot be directly compared as magnitudes, only as rankings/signals.
- Cannot cleanly separate vehicle effect from driver effect: cheap cars attract higher-risk drivers on more dangerous roads, and neither dataset fully adjusts for income or geography.
- The Rio was discontinued after MY2023; findings describe the 2018-2023 generation, not a current product.
- AEB-optional claim rests on Consumer Reports' 2023 trim data; dealer-installed variation exists.

## Actionable takeaways
- Shopping a used subcompact (2018-2023 Rio, Versa, Mirage)? The Good crash ratings are within-class grades. On roads shared with Tahoes and F-150s, mass is a safety feature. A used Forte or Elantra with standard automatic emergency braking costs little more and brings the crash-avoidance tech the Rio charged extra for.
- If you already drive a Rio: the car's structure is sound — the risk is the mismatch. Drive like the smallest thing on the road, because it is. And check whether your trim actually has the optional AEB package; many don't.
- The general rule IIHS keeps proving: in a two-vehicle crash, the heavier vehicle wins. Buy as much mass and as much standard driver-assist as the budget allows, in that order.
