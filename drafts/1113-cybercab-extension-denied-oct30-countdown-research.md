## Angle (1-2 sentences)
Tesla asked NHTSA for eight weeks to answer 21 sworn questions about how it certified a car with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no mirrors. NHTSA gave it 30 days. The answers land October 30, under oath, with a $139M penalty ceiling — and Tesla keeps putting more Cybercabs on the road while it writes them.

## Kill test
- **Genuinely newsworthy?** Yes. New development reported Oct 8 (TeslaNorth, via public NHTSA Sept 29 memo to file). The Oct 30 deadline is 21 days out and will set the federal template for every wheel-less vehicle after it.
- **Novel angle?** Yes. Six prior Cybercab stories on this site covered the audit opening, the Special Order, the standards collision, and the Zoox contrast. None covers the extension denial — and the countdown framing (Tesla writing sworn answers while expanding the fleet to car #67 and filling the Dallas lot) is new.
- **Surprising?** The 8-weeks-asked vs 30-days-granted gap is the tell: NHTSA is not in a patient mood. And the sharpest question in the order — FMVSS 135's foot-control rule vs. a car with no pedals, with NHTSA's view that such a vehicle "could not certify" — is now 21 days from a sworn answer.

## Limitations (must state in article)
- NHTSA has not found that the Cybercab violates any standard; AQ26002 is an audit, the Special Order is a demand for information, not a verdict.
- Tesla's extension request itself is not public — only the single sentence NHTSA quoted from it. We don't know the full argument.
- The memo does not explain why NHTSA granted 30 days instead of 8 weeks; the "impatience" read is inference, stated as such.
- Tesla's answers are not due until Oct 30; this article reports the questions and the clock, not the answers.
- The Cybercab fleet's safety record ("no reported crash") is an absence-of-reporting claim over weeks, not a finding.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Self-certification is the process every car in America uses to reach the road; Tesla followed the law as written, and "inapplicable" determinations are routine engineering judgments. NHTSA audits manufacturers as a matter of course. Granting 30 days instead of 60 is ordinary case management, not a signal — agencies routinely split the difference on extension requests. Zoox's 4-year exemption odyssey is arguably the broken process here, and if the Cybercab is genuinely safe, the paperwork fight is theater that punishes the company moving fastest.

## Actionable insight (hard gate)
If you rode a Cybercab in Austin: the vehicle's legal right to the road is under sworn federal examination with answers due Oct 30. That doesn't make the ride unsafe, but the compliance story is unresolved — and whatever Tesla swears to will set the template for every driverless vehicle after it. Watch Oct 30, not the earnings call: the affidavit matters more than the guidance.
