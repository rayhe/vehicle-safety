The Cameras Were There. They Just Weren't Watching.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 17, 2026

On September 9, Ford told NHTSA that 223,472 F-150 pickups might be carrying their fuel tanks on improperly secured straps, and that the tanks could leak fuel or detach from the truck while driving. A fuel tank falling off America's best-selling vehicle at highway speed is about as noir as manufacturing defects get. So I did what any self-respecting crash correspondent does and asked the obvious question: who was supposed to be watching?

223,472
2023–2027 F-150s recalled under Ford campaign 26S69 (NHTSA 26V578)[5] for fuel tank straps that may not be secured to the frame

The answer, according to Ford's own recall report, is cameras. Ford built an assembly-line quality system called MAIVS, Mobile AI Vision System, out of consumer smartphones and 3D-printed camera holders, specifically so an eye would be on the body at every station. It can scan for 25 different quality problems. And yet the recall report explains the strap slots weren't being properly inserted into the frame rail because, and I am quoting the report, camera systems to verify proper fuel tank strap assembly "were not in continuous use" at the F-150 plants.[2] The watchers were installed; the watchers were off. Sixteen warranty claims worldwide later, here we are.

Context, because context is what separates a report from a panic: Ford says this affects less than 1% of the F-150 population, built across the Dearborn and Kansas City plants from January 2023 through August 2026. There have been no crashes and no injuries. The recalled trucks are either unsold or have less than three months in service, because a fuel tank strap that was never seated tends to announce itself early. That's also why the recall number sounds terrifying and the actual fix is a dealer visit: they inspect the straps, reseat or replace them, free, and the whole thing takes one appointment. Owner letters mail September 21; VINs have been searchable since September 14.[3]

What makes this week worth a column instead of a wire brief is the company it keeps. In the same batch, Ford recalled 37 F-150s whose rear axles got 8.8-inch bearings instead of the 9.75-inch units the Max Tow package specifies, because 37 trucks slipped past the Sterling Axle Plant with the wrong parts. Also recalled: 17 Raptors from December and January whose Panhard rod bolts may not have been tightened, because a torque-checking device was sitting in bypass mode.[4] Three recalls, one week, all three essentially reading "the verification step didn't verify." Ford has spent the last year telling anyone with a microphone about its quality overhaul, backed by a first-place finish in JD Power's 2026 Initial Quality Survey. The surveys measure the first 90 days of ownership. You know what else shows up in the first 90 days: a falling fuel tank.

The strongest defense Ford could offer is that the system worked, sort of: warranty claims surfaced the pattern, the company moved before anyone got hurt, and the recall is narrow by design, which is true and it matters. It is also true that a camera system built from smartphones and 3D-printed plastic was on the line specifically to prevent exactly this, and "not in continuous use" is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence. Somewhere in Dearborn there is a spreadsheet explaining when the cameras were off and why; I'd read it, and so would you.

If you own a 2023 or newer F-150, do the unglamorous thing: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls today, not when the letter arrives. There are no warning signs before a strap fails, per NHTSA, which means there is also no excuse for guessing; the dealer inspection is free. If you're shopping for a used one, note the population is unsold and sub-three-month trucks, so the 2023s and 2024s on lots are mostly outside the recall. Mostly is doing some work there too, so check the VIN anyway.
