74 Defects Under Federal Watch. Your Used Car Might Be One of Them.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 31, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better.

NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation is currently sitting on 74 open defect investigations that were opened since 2019, and 24 of them were opened in 2026 alone. Before a defect becomes a recall, it lives here, sometimes for years. ForCar published a table this week of the investigations covering common used cars, eleven rows of "maybe defective, maybe not."[1] So I did what I do: I cross-tabulated their table against a decade of FARS fatality data to find where the unresolved investigations overlap with the deadliest vehicles.

~43,800
FARS 2014-2023 deaths in the models covered by the 11 used-car investigation rows, all causes

Start with the headline finding. The deadliest vehicle in the investigation table is the Honda Accord: 7,102 deaths in FARS 2014-2023, a fatality rate of 3.07 per 100 million vehicle miles, the highest rate of any model on the list.[2] And what is the Accord under investigation for? Automatic emergency braking that fires with nothing ahead. Opened April 2024, still unresolved. Let that sit for a second: the car killing the most people per mile on this list is being investigated for brakes that slam on for ghosts.

Notice the bottom of the table. The Tesla Model 3 and Model Y are under investigation for front suspension links detaching, and they are the two safest-per-mile vehicles on the entire list, at 0.05 and 0.03 deaths per 100M VMT.[2] This is why the cross-tab matters and also why it can lie to you: a failure mode is not a crash outcome. A suspension link can be genuinely defective on a car whose drivers almost never die in it. Both facts fit in your head at once. Try.

Now the structural stats, because this is my beat and the shape of the docket is the real story. Fifteen of the 74 open investigations, about one in five, concern automatic braking, driver assistance, or self-driving systems: Tesla's Full Self-Driving, Ford's BlueCruise, robotaxi operators, and four separate files on automatic emergency braking firing at empty road.[1] The car industry spent a century learning to make metal not break. Now a fifth of the federal defect docket is software that imagines obstacles.

Twelve of the 74 are recall queries, which is the agency asking whether a recall repair actually fixed anything. The Jeep Grand Cherokee is in this box twice over: recalled for coil springs, then investigated because the springs detach after the recall repair, which means the fix itself failed. That category exists because this happens.[1]

And then there is the Nissan Rogue. Its phantom-braking file has been open since September 2019. Seven years and one month. Longer than the average car loan, longer than two presidential terms of regulatory attention, and the agency still has not decided. Since 2005, roughly half of NHTSA's investigations have ended in a recall and the rest closed with no action, so this one has a coin flip's chance of ending in silence.[1] If you drive a 2017-2019 Rogue, the government has been "looking into" your brakes since before the pandemic. Comforting.

What this article did not prove: that any of these 74 investigations will become a recall, that the FARS death rates are caused by the investigated defects (they are not; the rates cover all crash causes and broader model years than the investigations), or that a low-rate vehicle like the Model Y has no real defect (failure modes and crash outcomes measure different things). The 24 investigations opened in 2026 could mean more defects, a more active agency, or both. And ForCar's eleven rows are an editorial selection for used-car shoppers, not the full 74-file docket, so this cross-tab inherits their selection bias along with their homework.

The advice is arithmetic, and it starts before you buy. An open investigation shows up on no window sticker and in no listing, so check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and search NHTSA's investigations for your model, then do it again a few months after you buy: recalls routinely arrive years after the car was built.[3] Ask the seller about the specific fault, not "any recalls": on a 2016-2020 Pilot, an engine already replaced under warranty is good news, not bad news. On a 2015-2017 F-150, ask about harsh downshifts. On a Grand Cherokee, ask whether the coil-spring recall repair held. And remember the base rate: roughly half of these files end with nothing. Half end with a recall letter in your mailbox.
