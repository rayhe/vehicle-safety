The Gap

America’s Cheapest SUV Just Out-Crashed Its Best-Selling One. The Difference Is the Back Seat.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see what the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety found in its latest round of crash tests, because the result turns the usual price-safety logic upside down. On October 22, 2026, IIHS announced that the 2026–27 Nissan Kicks, a subcompact crossover that starts at about $22,730, earned a Good rating in the updated moderate overlap front test and a Top Safety Pick award, while the redesigned 2026 Toyota RAV4, America’s best-selling SUV at 479,288 units last year, managed only Acceptable, and the RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid scored Marginal.[1][4] Six vehicles went through the test. Only two earned Good. One of them costs nine thousand dollars less than the car it beat.

479,288
RAV4s sold in the US in 2025, making it the best-selling SUV in America and the third-best-selling vehicle of any kind.

Every one of these cars aced the tests that protect the driver. Small overlap front: Good. Side impact: Good. What separated them was the seat behind the driver, where IIHS now places a crash-test dummy roughly the size of a 12-year-old child, and where the belts in the RAV4, the Audi Q3, and the Chevrolet Trailblazer all did the same ugly thing: the shoulder belt slid up toward the dummy’s neck, and the lap belt rode off the pelvis and onto the soft abdomen, which is exactly how a survivable crash turns into an internal injury.[1][6] IIHS rated the RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid’s rear-seat restraints and dummy kinematics Poor. Audi’s redesigned Q3 earned Poor overall in the same test, and the Trailblazer joined it at the bottom, with weak headlights and front crash prevention on top. Only the Kicks and the 2027 Volkswagen Jetta earned Good.

Here is the part that should make Toyota uncomfortable, and it is also the part that makes this story worth your time. Nissan failed this exact test first. When the redesigned Kicks debuted for 2025, it scored Poor in the updated moderate overlap test, so Nissan changed the rear seat belts and load limiters on cars built after June 2026, sent the car back, and watched it earn Good.[1] That is a parts change, not a redesign, and it took months, not model years. Toyota, meanwhile, had the same test, the same dummy, and the same four years of warning since IIHS added the rear-seat dummy in 2022, and still shipped a clean-sheet RAV4 whose back seat cannot hold a child-sized passenger in place during a 40 mph crash. One company treated the new test as a bug report. One shipped the bug.

Fairness demands the other side, stated at full strength. A lab test is not a body count, and it will take years of real-world FARS data to show whether these rear-belt differences move the death toll at all, which means the RAV4’s Acceptable and Marginal ratings are a warning, not a verdict on how many people will die in one. Nissan’s win is also narrower than the headline suggests, because the Kicks scored only Marginal in vehicle-to-vehicle front crash prevention, the one metric where the RAV4 beat it, and that kept the Kicks out of the higher Top Safety Pick+ award.[6] Toyota can fix the RAV4 the same way Nissan fixed the Kicks, with a running change to the rear belts, and if it does, this article’s criticism goes stale overnight. All true. None of it changes the fact that right now, today, the best-selling family SUV in America protects the seat your kid sits in worse than the cheapest SUV in the test, and the fix is a part Nissan already proved works.

What is not proven here deserves saying plainly. IIHS ratings measure new cars in a lab, not crash outcomes on real roads, so nothing here predicts a specific family’s risk, and the price gap is approximate, since the Kicks’ $22,730 figure is the 2025 base and the RAV4’s $31,900 is the 2026 starting MSRP, both before destination and fees.[4][5] We also do not know whether Toyota has a rear-belt revision already in the pipeline, because the company has not announced one. If it does, buy the revised car, not the early one.

What do you do with this? If you are shopping for a Kicks, check the build date on the driver’s door-jamb sticker before you sign anything, because only 2026–27 models built after June 2026 carry the fix, and a pre-June car on the lot still carries the Poor rating. If you are shopping for a 2026 RAV4, ask the dealer, in writing, whether the car has revised rear seat belts, and if the answer is a shrug, wait for the running change or cross-shop the Kicks and the Jetta, both of which earned Good where the RAV4 did not. And whatever you buy, ask about the updated moderate overlap front test by name, because a Good small-overlap score tells you how the driver survives, and says nothing at all about the child in the back.
