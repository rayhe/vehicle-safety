Tesla Told Drivers the Suspension Failures Were Their Fault. NHTSA Just Opened a Probe Into 1.2 Million Cars.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 11, 2026

On July 29, NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Preliminary Evaluation PE26006: an estimated 1,198,300 Tesla Model 3 (2018-2020) and Model Y (2021-2023) vehicles, after 156 complaints that the front lower lateral link simply let go.[1][2]

3,213
Vehicles covered by Tesla's two prior US recalls for this component, out of 1,198,300 now under investigation

Read the federal resume slowly. The failure, it says, potentially causes "a loss of vehicle directional control." In most cases, the drivers got no advance warning. A few heard noises first. The car itself said nothing. No lights, no messages. Then the link that holds the wheel to the car ended, and the car went wherever physics took it.[1]

Tesla has recalled this part before. Twice. In 2021: 2,791 cars, attributed to a production issue. In 2023: 422 cars, for similar failures.[1] And yet here we are: a federal probe covering 1.2 million vehicles. NHTSA's own filing says the subject failures exceed the scope of those recalls and do not appear to be related to the production issue that prompted them. Two recalls, 3,213 cars, and the defect kept happening anyway.

None of this is new information to Tesla. A December 2023 Reuters investigation found the company had internally tracked chronic failures of suspension and steering components for years, fore and aft links, control arms, the whole unglamorous family of parts that keep wheels attached. Engineers ordered repeated redesigns. They discussed getting money back from suppliers. And in letters to customers and to NHTSA, the company blamed the failures on "driver abuse." In China, regulators pressured Tesla into recalling the front suspension aft link and rear upper link in 2020. In America, no such recall ever happened.[3] Two US senators wrote to Elon Musk in December 2023, saying they were "disturbed that you would blame your customers for these failures," and urged him to recall the components and correct the record with NHTSA.[4]

Fairness requires saying what PE26006 is not. It is the mildest stage of the federal defect process. The complaint set shows zero crashes, zero injuries, zero fatalities. A preliminary evaluation is an investigation, not a verdict. And the links Reuters documented are adjacent to, not identical with, the lateral link NHTSA is now examining. Same family, different defendant.

But the arithmetic will not sit still. The two prior recalls covered 3,213 vehicles, 0.27 percent of the population now under investigation. Every official action so far has treated this as a batch problem. A batch problem does not produce 156 no-warning failures across six model years. NHTSA's filing says the quiet part out loud: this is bigger than the batch.

If you drive a 2018-2020 Model 3 or a 2021-2023 Model Y, know this: your car will not warn you. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for 21V-835 and 23V-235, the two prior recalls. Listen for clunking from the front suspension; the handful of owners who got any warning heard noises first. And if a shop has ever replaced a front suspension link on your car, file the complaint at nhtsa.gov. The entire federal population estimate runs on 156 people who bothered.[5]

Tesla's engineers ordered repeated redesigns of these parts and discussed billing suppliers for the defects. Its letters to regulators called the failures driver abuse. PE26006 is the federal government's way of asking which of those stories was true.
