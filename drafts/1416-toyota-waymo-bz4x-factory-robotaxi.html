Toyota Handed Its Factory to the Safest Driver on the Road. It Isn't Human.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 6, 2026

I ran the numbers, then I ran them again, and they didn't get better. On October 30, Toyota announced it will build a custom bZ4X robotaxi for Waymo at its Takaoka plant, with Waymo's driving hardware integrated on the production line, and testing starts in Japan in 2027.[1] Waymo's latest published safety data, covering 271 million rider-only miles, shows 82 percent fewer injury-causing crashes and 95 percent fewer crashes involving serious injury or death than human drivers on the same roads.[2]

Start with the car: the 2025 bZ4X, built after December 2024, earned IIHS Top Safety Pick+, the Institute's highest award, with Good ratings in the small overlap, updated moderate overlap, and updated side tests, and NHTSA gave it five stars overall.[6] Only vehicles built after December 2024 qualify, because Toyota upgraded the XLE trim's headlights from Marginal to Good, and that single trim change was the difference between the award and no award. One caution: the robotaxi is a custom variant, with automatic doors, more rear legroom, and a power liftgate. Its crash-test ratings are unpublished, and the showroom car's Top Safety Pick+ transfers only to the extent the structure is shared.

Now the driver: Waymo's September 24 safety update covers 271 million rider-only miles through June 2026, across Phoenix, the San Francisco Bay Area, Los Angeles, Austin, and Atlanta. Against human-driver benchmarks on the same roads, counting crashes regardless of fault, the Waymo Driver recorded 82 percent fewer injury-causing crashes and 95 percent fewer crashes involving serious injury or death.[2] In absolute terms, that is 841 fewer injury crashes and 55 fewer serious crashes than humans would have had over the same distance. At the fleet's current pace of more than 4 million miles a week, the arithmetic works out to roughly one serious crash prevented every eight or nine days, which matches Waymo's own March claim of one every eight days, and that is division, not marketing.[3]

Skeptics should note the independent check. IIHS ran its own analysis in July 2026, over about 50 million Waymo driverless miles from 2021 through 2024, and found 68 percent fewer police-reportable crashes and 81 percent fewer injury crashes per mile than human drivers in the same cities and years.[4] IIHS President David Harkey put it carefully: on a limited scale, these driverless cars are safer than human drivers. Swiss Re, working from insurance claims rather than Waymo's own crash reports, found 92 percent fewer bodily injury claims and 88 percent fewer property damage claims over 25.3 million miles, and three different counters point the same direction.[5]

None of that makes the case airtight, because Waymo grades its own homework: the 271-million-mile figures are Waymo's analysis of Waymo's crashes against benchmarks Waymo selected. IIHS converged on the injury-crash number, 81 percent against Waymo's 82, but could not confirm the cyclist benefit, with only three Waymo cyclist crashes in its study period, and its Los Angeles results alone were not statistically significant. Its serious-injury estimate rests on two crashes. And the operating domain is the easiest driving in America: mapped urban grids, no freeways, fair-weather cities, city speeds. A 95 percent reduction on those miles says nothing about a snowstorm on I-80.

Waymo's record also includes real failures. In August 2026, a Waymo in Dallas slowed from 24 to 5 mph after detecting a pedestrian 150 feet out, and an SUV struck her at 35 in a 30. NHTSA's preliminary evaluation of Waymo's school-zone behavior is open, and Waymo filed its substantive answers fully redacted as confidential business information. Toyota, for its part, disclosed no fleet size, no price, no production target, and no launch date, and its language on personally owned self-driving Toyotas is "explore," a press-release verb, not a plan. Nobody can buy this safety record; they can only hail it, in five American metros and, from 2027, in Japan.

Two things are worth doing with this news. If you buy a bZ4X, check the build date: only vehicles built after December 2024 carry the Top Safety Pick+ award. And judge every robotaxi safety claim the same way: per-mile crash rates against matched human benchmarks, not raw crash counts or adjectives. Waymo publishes that comparison, while Tesla, whose robotaxi program is currently answering a federal defect petition over school-zone speeding, does not. Toyota just bet its factory on the company that shows its work. That is the whole story, and it fits in a spreadsheet.
