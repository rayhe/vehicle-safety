# Research: #1398 — Traffic Is Now the Leading Killer of American Police Officers

## Kill test: PROCEED

- **Genuinely newsworthy?** YES. Three independent federal tallies released within the last ~10 weeks converge on one reversal: traffic has overtaken gunfire as the leading cause of line-of-duty police deaths in 2026, and it is rising while overall officer deaths fall. NHTSA's own administrator announced it in a keynote to state highway safety officials; the National Law Enforcement Officers Memorial Fund's preliminary count through October 1 confirms it; the FBI's LEOKA collection confirms the shape (accidental deaths up, traffic dominating them).
- **Novel for this site?** YES. Queue/drafts filename + content grep for officer / law-enforcement / first-responder / struck-by / line-of-duty: zero prior Crash Report articles on officer fatalities in 1,397 articles. Nearest neighbors are different stories: #1179 (move-over compliance gap, driver behavior, Oct 17 peg) and #1201 (prehospital blood, post-crash care). Neither covers officer deaths as a trend. The October 2026 recall news cycle is fully exhausted (see #1299 kill notes); data stories are the fresh ground, and this one is data with a body count.
- **Original contribution:** (1) The enforcement paradox — NHTSA's Pathway to Safer Streets plan, unveiled April 2026, has as its cornerstone a return to vigorous traffic enforcement, putting more officers on the roadside in the same year traffic became their leading killer. (2) The definitional-gap analysis: NHTSA's +82% vs NLEOMF's +48% cannot both describe the same window; both are reported with windows labeled and the gap flagged, not reconciled. (3) FBI LEOKA arithmetic: 25 of 30 accidental officer deaths in Jan–Sep 2026 were traffic-related (15 motor-vehicle crashes + 10 struck-by) = 83%; accidental deaths up 50% year over year while felonious killings fell.

## Verified facts

- **NHTSA keynote (Morrison):** line-of-duty fatalities down 5% YoY; traffic-related officer fatalities up 82% "thus far this year"; traffic makes up more than half of officer fatalities. Keynote delivered at the Governors Highway Safety Association annual meeting; references the Labor Day "Drive Sober or Get Pulled Over" campaign as in progress, placing it in late summer 2026. No exact date is printed on the NHTSA page; do NOT assert one.
- **NLEOMF Jan 1–Oct 1, 2026:** total 68 (2025 same period: 84); traffic-related 31 (2025: 21). +48% traffic, −19% total. Traffic is the leading cause, ahead of gunfire.
- **FBI LEOKA Jan–Sep 2026:** 31 felonious killings; 30 accidental deaths (vs 20 in Jan–Sep 2025, +50%). Accidental causes: motor-vehicle crashes 15, struck-by 10, aircraft 2. Traffic-related accidental = 25 of 30.
- **NLEOMF 2025 full year:** 111 total deaths (lowest in 80+ years); traffic-related 34 (−23% vs 44 in 2024); struck-by 13 (−24% vs 17).
- **P2SS:** unveiled April 2026; eight pathways; cornerstone = active re-engagement with law enforcement and a return to vigorous traffic enforcement; $20M Innovative Traffic Safety Enforcement grant program accepting applications.
- **Context (same keynote):** about half of passenger-vehicle occupant fatalities chose not to buckle up; about one-third of fatalities involve an alcohol-impaired driver; about one-third involve speeding.
- **2025 national backdrop (NHTSA early estimate, Apr 1, 2026):** 36,640 traffic deaths in 2025, −6.7% vs 39,254 in 2024; rate 1.10 per 100M VMT, second-lowest ever. Civilian roads got safer while officer traffic deaths rose — the divergence is the story.

## Original analysis (computed — flagged as computed)

- **Pace arithmetic (NLEOMF):** 31 traffic deaths in the first 274 days of 2026 annualizes to roughly 41 for the full year (31 / 274 × 365), vs 34 in all of 2025 and 44 in 2024. This is a pace, not a prediction; Q4 is historically the deadliest quarter for traffic, so the final count could land higher. Label it as a pace.
- **FBI LEOKA share:** 15 crashes + 10 struck-by = 25 of 30 accidental officer deaths in Jan–Sep 2026 were traffic-related (83%). The remaining 5 include 2 aircraft crashes.
- **The +82% vs +48% gap:** NHTSA's keynote figure (+82%) and NLEOMF's Oct 1 figure (+48%, 21 → 31) describe different windows and possibly different definitions (NHTSA's exact window and counting rules are not stated in the keynote text). Both are reported with their windows; the gap is a limitation, not a contradiction to be smoothed over. A plausible partial explanation is timing: the keynote predates Oct 1, and early-2026 deaths may have been front-loaded. Do NOT assert this as fact.
- **Reversal magnitude:** traffic deaths fell 23% in 2025 (44 → 34) and are up ~48% through Oct 1, 2026 (21 → 31). A two-year swing from −23% to +48% in the same metric is the sharpest reversal in the NLEOMF series as reported here.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)

The rise may partly reflect exposure, not risk. NHTSA's own plan explicitly puts more officers back on traffic duty ("returning to vigorous, effective traffic enforcement"), and more officers doing more roadside stops mechanically means more officers exposed to traffic. If enforcement hours rose faster than officer traffic deaths, the risk per enforcement-hour could be flat or even falling while the body count rises. The data to split exposure from risk — enforcement hours by agency, stops per officer — does not exist in any public dataset cited here. The counterargument is real and the article must carry it, not bury it. What it does NOT explain: struck-by deaths at crash scenes and disabled-vehicle assists, where the officer is not choosing exposure but responding to someone else's crash.

## Limitations

- 2026 figures are preliminary (NLEOMF, FBI LEOKA) and will be revised; final 2026 counts arrive in early 2027.
- NHTSA's +82% figure: exact window and counting definition not stated in the keynote; it cannot be directly reconciled with NLEOMF's +48% through Oct 1.
- NLEOMF, ODMP, and FBI LEOKA count differently (NLEOMF 68 total through Oct 1 vs ODMP 73 as of Oct 11 vs FBI 61 through Sep 30); use each source's numbers only within its own tally.
- FARS does not code officer status, so no FARS cross-tab of officer deaths is possible; the site's own 2014–2023 dataset is blind to this story. That blindness is worth one sentence, not a paragraph.
- No causal attribution is proven for the rise (enforcement exposure, traffic volume, driver behavior, or a mix). Do not credit a single cause.
- Individual officers are not named in this article; the HSToday piece names Philadelphia Officer Shane Seiber (ambush, not traffic) — do not import non-traffic deaths into the traffic count.

## Actionable insights (required)

1. **Drivers — move over, all the way:** every state has a Move Over law. #1179 found 36% of drivers neither change lanes nor slow down for roadside emergency vehicles. A full lane change, not a polite lift, is the difference between a close call and a struck-by. If you cannot move over, slow down well below the limit.
2. **If you are the one stopped on the shoulder:** get out of the car and behind the guardrail if you can do so safely; a disabled vehicle on the shoulder is one of the activity categories in the struck-by tracker. Do not stand between your car and traffic.
3. **If you employ or manage officers:** the risk concentrates at traffic stops, crash scenes, disabled-vehicle assists, and directing traffic. Traffic incident management training and the Below 100 program exist for exactly this; NHTSA's $20M enforcement-innovation grants are accepting applications.
4. **Policy ask:** NHTSA's Pathway to Safer Streets should publish officer traffic deaths as a tracked metric of its own enforcement push. A plan that puts more officers on the roadside and does not count how many the roadside kills is flying blind.
