Tesla Detected That You Were Drowsy. It Politely Suggested You Keep Driving.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 22, 2026

According to the congressional mailbag, and this one is a doozy, Representative Raja Krishnamoorthi of Illinois wrote to the Secretary of Transportation on September 9 with a request that boils down to a single question: your car knows the driver is asleep, so why does it keep driving? The letter cites an NBC News investigation that counted 43 videos of drivers apparently asleep at the wheel of moving Teslas with Autopilot or Full Self-Driving engaged, 17 of them posted this year.[1][2]

Tesla vehicles covered by NHTSA engineering analysis EA26002, opened March 18, 2026

Here is the detail that made the congressman sit up. Starting with software update 2025.32.3, Tesla cars reportedly began flashing messages like "Drowsiness detected. Stay focused with FSD" and "Lane drift detected. Let FSD assist so you can stay focused."[1] Read that first one again slowly. The vehicle's impairment detector fires, identifies the driver as drowsy, and its recommended intervention is continued use of the system that requires a supervised, attentive driver. That's not a warning; it's a prescription for the problem it just found. Wired flagged this exact inversion a year ago.[3]

To appreciate the absurdity, consider what every other impaired-driving framework on Earth does. A breathalyzer interlock stops the car, and a drowsy trucker gets pulled off the road. Tesla's approach detects the impairment and says, roughly, "no worries, the robot's got this one," except the robot legally requires the impaired human to supervise it, and the loop closes on nothing.

Krishnamoorthi's letter draws the line carefully: advising an alert driver who drifts toward a lane marking to let the assistance system help is one thing, while encouraging a driver the car has already detected as drowsy to stay in the loop is something else entirely. The letter asks five questions, due in writing by September 30, and the fifth is the one with teeth: what findings would lead NHTSA to conclude this is a safety defect requiring a recall?[1]

Now zoom out, because the timeline is doing something hilarious and awful at the same time. On May 7 of this year, NHTSA put out a press release announcing the Tesla Model Y as the first vehicle to pass its brand-new Advanced Driver Assistance System tests.[5] Four months later, a member of Congress is asking the same agency whether the same company's driver-monitoring safeguards are so deficient they constitute a defect. Passing the agency's test suite in May and being asked about a potential recall in September is quite a semester.

Meanwhile there are two dockets on the same desk asking opposite questions. EA26002, opened in March, covers 3.2 million FSD-equipped vehicles and asks whether the system degrades gracefully when visibility drops: nine incidents, one fatality, one injury.[1] AQ26002, opened September 3, asks the reverse: how Tesla certified a car with no steering wheel or pedals as compliant with standards written around both. One docket asks what happens when the car stops supervising itself; the other asks what happens when the driver stops supervising the car. NHTSA is now officially examining both ends of the attention equation at once.

The camera, by the way, is not the backstop you think it is. Tesla's own owner's manual says the cabin camera monitors attentiveness, and the letter notes drivers have defeated it with sunglasses or, in one memorable video, a folded $20 bill taped over the lens.[1][6] A monitoring system that can be neutralized with pocket change is a suggestion, not a safeguard.

Fair is fair, so here is the steelmanned defense. Tesla's position would be that "stay focused with FSD" is encouragement to keep supervising, not permission to nap; that a drowsy driver with supervised assistance is strictly safer than a drowsy driver with nothing; and that 43 self-selected viral videos across a fleet of 3.2 million vehicles prove nothing about systemic failure. The misuse is the driver's, the camera exists, and the point is taken; none of those observations are nothing.

But here's what the defense can't wave away: the alert itself. A system sophisticated enough to detect drowsiness could just as easily disengage, slow the car, or demand a pullover. GM's Super Cruise does exactly that: its third inattention alert slows the vehicle to a stop in its lane, disengages the system, and has OnStar call into the cabin to check on the driver.[8] Tesla chose the one response that keeps the feature engaged at the precise moment the human is least qualified to use it. That is a design choice, and design choices are what recalls are made of.

The September 30 deadline was for answers to five questions, not a recall order, so calibrate accordingly. But the letter's last question is the one that keeps mattering after the deadline passes: if NHTSA answers what would constitute a defect, it has effectively published the test the agency will now grade Tesla against. For 3.2 million cars and everyone who shares a highway with them, that answer outlives any deadline.

If you drive a Tesla with FSD, treat a drowsiness alert as a pull-over signal, not a permission slip. The system asking you to stay focused is telling on itself: it knows you're not. Check your VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls, and if you've seen the drowsiness prompts or caught the camera being fooled, file a complaint at NHTSA's site. That complaint database is the fuel EA26002 runs on.[7]
