Dodge Built 15 Durangos With the Wrong Engine. The Fix Is a Whole New Engine.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

Somewhere in Detroit, or possibly in a dealer service bay by now, fifteen brand-new Dodge Durangos are waiting for new engines. Not rebuilt engines, not repaired engines, but entirely new ones, plus new catalytic converters, because Chrysler bolted the wrong powertrain into the cars at the factory and shipped them anyway. NHTSA campaign 26V760, filed November 19, 2026, covers 15 model-year 2026 Durangos built between July 1 and July 22, 2026. Every part in those cars was presumably fine. Only the combination was wrong.[1]

15
2026 Dodge Durangos built with the wrong powertrain. The remedy is a new engine and new catalytic converters, free of charge.

Chrysler’s own words, filed with NHTSA under 49 CFR Part 573, are admirably blunt: the vehicles “were manufactured with an incorrect powertrain that may cause a loss of drive power.” A loss of drive power increases the risk of a crash, which is the sentence that turns a factory mix-up into a federal safety recall. Dealers will replace the engine and catalytic converters free of charge. Owner notification letters go out December 15, 2026, and VINs become searchable on NHTSA’s site December 1. No crashes or injuries are cited in the filing, which is the good news, and the filing does not say which powertrain went in or which one should have, which is the part that will keep Durango forums arguing until spring.[1][4]

For scale, the Durango already had a 2026 recall before this one. Campaign 26V701, filed October 22, covers 13,330 Durangos whose tail-light wiring harness terminals can fail, leaving the tail lights dark. Add the two campaigns together and the 2026 Durango’s recall total is 13,345 vehicles, of which the wrong-engine cars are 0.11 percent. One model year, two recalls, two completely different failure modes: a harness that can go dark and an engine that should never have been installed. Neither one says anything about the Durango as a vehicle. Both say something about the year it had.[1]

Fifteen cars is a rounding error, and if the story ended there it would be a curiosity, not an article. It does not end there. Wrong-part-at-the-factory recalls form a distinct category, and once you start looking for them they show up at every manufacturer and every price point. I pulled every campaign I could verify against NHTSA’s API that fits the pattern, and the table below is the result.

Add the populations and the category totals 547,054 vehicles. Now read the table again, because the totals lie about the shape of the problem. One campaign, Subaru’s misprinted weight label, accounts for 541,237 of those vehicles, or 99 percent of the category, and its fix is a sticker in the mail. At the other end sits the Durango: 15 vehicles, 0.003 percent of the category, and the only remedy in the group that involves removing an engine. Volume and severity run in opposite directions here, which is exactly the kind of inversion that makes a census worth running. Two of the five campaigns were filed within 24 hours of each other, the Durango on November 19 and the Genesis ball joint on November 20, which proves nothing except that factory errors do not take turns.[2][3][9]

Think about what has to fail for a wrong engine to reach a customer. An engine is not a bolt. It arrives on a line sequenced to a build sheet, gets married to a chassis, gets wired, plumbed, flashed with software, and driven off the line by someone whose job includes noticing that the car runs. Somewhere in that chain, a verification step either did not exist or did not fire, and then every downstream check, end-of-line testing, dealer prep, delivery inspection, passed a car whose powertrain did not match its own paperwork. That is the finding that matters. Component testing cannot catch this defect, because every component tested fine. Only the combination was wrong, and combinations are verified by process, not by parts.

Now the strongest case against this article, stated at full strength. Fifteen cars. No crashes, no injuries, no complaints cited. Chrysler caught its own mistake within four months of the build window, filed the paperwork, and is paying for fifteen engine swaps out of its own pocket. That is the safety system working, not failing, and a whole article about fifteen SUVs is the Crash Report mistaking a curiosity for a crisis. Fair. Genuinely fair. But the counterargument answers a question this article is not asking. Nobody is claiming fifteen Durangos are a public health emergency. What those fifteen cars prove is that the one defect no component test can catch, the wrong combination of right parts, can walk out of a modern factory undetected, and the table above shows the same hole opening at Subaru, Genesis, and Jayco in the same year, with small numbers and the same hole every time.

What this article does not prove, stated plainly. It does not prove how the mix-up happened, because the filing does not say, and neither Chrysler nor NHTSA has published a root-cause report. It does not prove the error rate, because the total number of Durangos built in the July 1 to 22 window is not public, so 15 out of an unknown denominator is not a rate. Population figures for the comparison campaigns come from the Part 573 reports via mirrors, and the five-campaign table is my own grouping built by searching NHTSA’s API for “manufactured with an incorrect” language, which means other factory-error campaigns filed under different wording may exist and are not counted here. Finally, the filing cites no crashes or injuries, which is not the same as proving none occurred, only that none are in the record.

If you own a 2026 Durango built in July 2026, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls starting December 1, 2026, and do not wait for the December 15 letter, because the campaign is 26V760, Chrysler’s internal number B3D. Expect the remedy to take days, not hours: an engine swap is a multi-day job, so get the loaner in writing before you hand over the keys. And if you are shopping for any 2026 vehicle, run the VIN before you sign, because factory-error recalls live in the same database as design defects, and the database does not care whether the mistake was in the part or in the person who picked it up.
