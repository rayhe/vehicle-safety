13,593 School Buses Were Glued Together. The Glue Didn't Stick.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 2, 2026

Thomas Built Buses, the largest school bus builder in America, just failed the one federal standard written specifically for school bus bodies, for the fourth time. The latest campaign covers buses whose body panels were bonded together at the interior wheelhouse with structural adhesive, applied over surfaces that were never prepped right, so 13,593 buses may not meet the tensile strength requirements of FMVSS 221. Three years. Four recalls. Same standard.[1]

37,250
School buses Thomas Built has recalled for FMVSS 221 noncompliance across four campaigns since 2023

Read the chronology in the Part 573 filing and you can watch the whole thing unfold. On September 9, 2026, a routine plant audit flagged the surface preparation for structural adhesive bonding, which is the kind of thing that sounds boring until it holds a school bus together, and engineers spent the next month testing samples. On October 20, Daimler Truck North America, Thomas Built's parent company, determined the wheelhouse joints might not meet the standard and declared a recall. Every one of the 13,593 buses is estimated to carry the condition, because the recall population was drawn straight from manufacturing records: buses built from November 2024 through September 2026, spanning the C2, the electric C2 Jouley, the HDX2, and the EFX2. Of those, 12,039 are the C2, which tracks, since it is the best-selling school bus in the country. This is what a production-process defect looks like at scale.[1]

Now the part worth saying slowly. FMVSS 221 exists for exactly one reason: when a school bus crashes, the body is supposed to remain in one piece around the children inside it, which is why regulators wrote minimum tensile strength requirements for every body panel joint. The fix for this recall, per the filing, is "additional mechanical fasteners will be added to the joints," which is the federal way of saying screws. That adhesive was supposed to do the structural work; the adhesive failed; the repair is to drill in the fasteners the adhesive was apparently standing in for. And if the punchline sounds familiar, it should: every one of these four campaigns ends at the same place. 23,590 buses in 2023 for a rear interior joint built wrong.[2] Two buses this June for header material that flunked the tensile test.[3] Sixty-five in August for connector plates the factory never installed.[4] Now 13,593 for glue that never stuck. The remedy, every time, is more screws.

To be fair about it: nobody has been hurt. Zero injuries, zero deaths, zero claims across all four recalls, and Thomas Built caught this one itself in a routine audit, filed within 41 days, and owned a 100% defect estimate instead of burying the number. That is the system working the way the regulations intended. It is also the system working on a defect that should never have left the plant, on a standard this company has now tripped over four times in three years. Also worth noting: a recall decided on October 20 does not mail owner letters until December 21, which means 62 days of buses running morning routes before the school districts that own them are even told. The buses are on the road right now; the letters are not.[1]

So here is what to do with all this. Fleet managers and bus contractors: run your VINs at nhtsa.gov/recalls today, not in December. The NHTSA campaign is 26V-702, DTNA's internal number is F1044, and the fix is free at any authorized dealer. If your kid rides a Thomas Built C2, C2 Jouley, HDX2, or EFX2 built between November 2024 and September 2026, ask the district whether the wheelhouse joint recall has been done. School buses remain the safest way for a child to travel, and four compliance recalls do not overturn that. They do, however, mean that every new Thomas Built campaign deserves a closer look than the last one got.[5]
