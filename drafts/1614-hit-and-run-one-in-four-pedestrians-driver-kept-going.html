One in Four Pedestrians Killed in 2024 Was Hit by a Driver Who Kept Going.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 28, 2026

Somebody hit a pedestrian in America in 2024, looked at what they had done, and drove away. Then somebody else did it, and then somebody else after that. By the end of the year, federal crash records counted 1,749 pedestrians killed by drivers who fled the scene, nearly five a day, every day, for a year.[1] That is not a rounding error in the pedestrian death toll. It is one in four of all pedestrians killed.

25%
Share of pedestrian deaths in 2024 caused by hit-and-run drivers, up from about 20% in 2015–2019

Start with the totals, because the totals look like good news and they are hiding the story. NHTSA counted 7,080 pedestrians killed in 2024, down 3.9 percent from 7,367 in 2023, and total traffic deaths fell 4.3 percent, from 41,025 to 39,254.[2] Hit-and-run deaths fell too, by 153, or 5.3 percent. A reader scanning those numbers could be forgiven for thinking the problem is easing, and it is easing in volume, not in behavior. Pedestrian hit-and-run deaths fell by 95 in 2024, yet the share of pedestrian deaths caused by fleeing drivers stayed exactly where it has sat since 2020: one in four.[1]

That share is the finding that matters, and it has a hinge in it. Between 2015 and 2019, hit-and-run crashes accounted for about 20 percent of pedestrian fatalities. In 2020 the share jumped to 24 percent. It has inched up to 25 percent since then and never come back down.[1] Count the bodies behind the percentages: 1,123 pedestrians killed by fleeing drivers in 2015, 1,749 in 2024, a 55.7 percent increase in nine years while the overall pedestrian death toll rose 29 percent over the same span. Something changed in 2020. Whatever it was, it stuck.

Zoom out from pedestrians and the shape gets worse. An analysis of federal crash records put total hit-and-run deaths at 2,758 in 2024, about seven and a half people a day.[5] Pedestrians and cyclists together made up 71.8 percent of those deaths: 1,749 pedestrians, 230 cyclists, against 485 vehicle occupants. People outside cars are not a minority of hit-and-run victims. They are the overwhelming majority, more than four times the number of occupants killed. And 94 percent of the time, according to the Governors Highway Safety Association, the vehicle that fled was the vehicle that struck the pedestrian.[4] There is no mystery second car in these crashes. The driver who hit is the driver who left.

Darkness is the other half of the story, and it is the half engineers can actually fix. Seventy-six percent of pedestrian fatalities in 2024 happened in the dark, and the 6 p.m. to midnight window alone accounted for 47 percent of them.[1][3] Fatal pedestrian crashes at night rose 84 percent between 2010 and 2023, against a 28 percent rise in daytime.[4] November is the deadliest month on the calendar for people on foot, 732 deaths in 2024, with October second at 695 and December third at 685.[1] That is not a coincidence of weather. It is geometry: the sun sets before the evening commute ends, and the street lighting on most American arterials was designed for drivers looking through windshields, not for pedestrians crossing eight lanes of it.

Which brings us to the first 150 milliseconds, the part of a crash where engineering still gets a vote. Vehicles equipped with automatic braking that detects pedestrians had a 27 percent lower rate of pedestrian crashes than vehicles without it, according to IIHS research.[1] That is a real number, from a real study, and it is the single most effective countermeasure in the data. It is also not standard equipment on every trim of every car, and IIHS ratings for pedestrian detection vary widely by model, which means a buyer who assumes their new car sees pedestrians is making an assumption the crash data does not support. Lighting works too, in the bluntest possible way: the Portland Bureau of Transportation notes that a pedestrian in reflective clothing is visible to a driver from up to 500 feet away, against 55 feet for dark clothing with no reflective gear.[6] Nine times the distance. That is the difference between a driver having time to brake and a driver having time to swear.

Honesty requires the counterargument at full strength, because the share jump is not a clean behavioral signal. FARS hit-and-run coding depends on police reports, and classification practices vary by state and have improved over the decade, so some of the 20-to-25 percent move could be better counting rather than worse driving. The 2024 declines are also real: hit-and-run deaths down 5.3 percent, pedestrian hit-and-run deaths down 5.2 percent, total deaths down 4.3 percent, and none of that is a statistical artifact.[2] And FARS records no motive field at all. Panic, unlicensed driving, no insurance, outstanding warrants, impairment: all are plausible reasons a driver flees, and the data cannot apportion them. Anyone telling you they know why drivers run is selling you a theory, not a finding. One more caveat, on sourcing: the 2,758 total hit-and-run deaths and the city-level figures come from an analysis by a plaintiff personal-injury firm, not peer-reviewed research, so treat the national total as cross-checked (it reconciles exactly with NHTSA's reported 5.3 percent decline) and the city claims as directional.[5]

None of those caveats changes what a reader should do with this. If you walk after dark, wear something reflective and cross where the light is, because the data says 500 feet versus 55 feet and the deadliest hours are the ones right after dinner. If you are buying a car, check the IIHS rating for its pedestrian-detection system specifically, not just the overall crash score, because a 27 percent reduction is worth more than a trim package. If you drive, get a dashcam: a fleeing driver is caught when somebody records a plate, and the 94 percent figure means the plate that matters is usually the one that just hit someone. And if you are the driver in that first 150 milliseconds, understand the math you are actually doing when you consider leaving. A crash is a civil matter and an insurance claim. Leaving the scene is a felony in most states. Fleeing does not make the problem smaller. It makes the problem criminal, and it leaves a person bleeding on asphalt in the dark while you decide.
