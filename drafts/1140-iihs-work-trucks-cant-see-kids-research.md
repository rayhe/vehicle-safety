## Verified facts

- IIHS launched a commercial vehicle assessment program in June 2026 (driver-protection info first: airbags, advanced belts). Oct 8 ratings are the FIRST front crash prevention ratings for commercial vehicles.
- 11 vehicles tested: 7 cargo vans + 4 Class 3 pickups (GVWR 10,001-14,000 lbs).
- **Vehicle-to-vehicle:** only the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned Good. Chevrolet Express 2500, GMC Savana 2500 (both lack AEB entirely — optional forward collision warning only) rated Poor, along with the Silverado 3500HD, Ford F-350, GMC Sierra 3500. Ram 3500 Marginal. BrightDrop 400 Acceptable.
- **Pedestrian:** BrightDrop 400 and Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 earned Good. Sprinter 2500, F-350, Ram 3500 Acceptable. Express/Savana Poor (no AEB).
- **Motorcycles were the biggest challenge:** several systems did not warn or slow at all in most motorcycle trials. Others braked but were downgraded for late warnings.
- **Child dummy "disappeared":** tall front ends + sensors mounted near the rearview mirror meant the child dummy was detected at a distance, then vanished as the truck approached. Acceptable-rated F-350 and Ram 3500 struggled more with the child in daylight than the adult at night.
- F-350 could only avoid the passenger-car target at 31 mph; at 43 mph it failed to reduce speed at all. Silverado 3500HD failed to avoid the car in all six tests.
- 6,215 people killed in 2024 in crashes involving heavy-/medium-duty vehicles or light vans = 16% of all US roadway fatalities. Part of IIHS 30x30 vision (cut fatalities 30% by 2030).
- Cathy Chase (Advocates for Highway and Auto Safety, Oct 8 statement): federal heavy-vehicle AEB standard "essential"; cites NHTSA June 2023 estimate that such a rule could save 155 lives, prevent 8,814 injuries and 19,000+ crashes per year, save ~$2B/year.
- Coming next: headlight and rear-AEB ratings for Class 3, then Class 4-6 trucks phased in later.

## Story angle

Mia's opener format ("Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds") maps directly to the disappearing child dummy: this is a sensor-geometry story. Mirror-mounted sensors look over the hood; the kid is below the sightline. Then the actionable sting: the Express/Savana — the vans parked in every fleet lot and contractor driveway in America — don't have AEB at all, and they're still sold that way. Contrast with the Good-rated BrightDrop and Rivian delivery vans proving it is possible. Actionable: fleet buyers should demand AEB; parents should treat tall-hooded work trucks like they have no eyes at bumper level (because the sensor suite effectively doesn't); check whether your employer's vans predate AEB standardization.
