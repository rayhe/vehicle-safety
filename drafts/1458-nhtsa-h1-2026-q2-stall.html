The Safest Half-Year Since 2014 Has a Q2 Problem: Deaths Fell Just 0.2%

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. They didn't get better. NHTSA released its early estimate for the first half of 2026 this morning, and the headline is the best in a decade: 16,750 people died on American roads from January through June, down 2.5% from 17,175 a year earlier, at a fatality rate of 1.02 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, the lowest half-year rate since 2014 and the lowest death count since 2015.[1] Fifth straight year of declines, on pace. Champagne numbers.

Now read the second sentence. Deaths fell 5% in the first three months of the year, according to the agency's release, and just 0.2% in the second three.[1] Same half-year, same roads, same drivers. One quarter improved at a pace not seen since the post-pandemic comedown, and the next quarter essentially stood still.

0.2%
Q2 2026 decline in traffic deaths, vs 4.3% in Q1. The improvement rate fell roughly 95% in three months.

Start with the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is the whole story. Q1 2026 came in at 7,770 deaths, down 4.3% from 8,120 in Q1 2025, at a 0.99 rate that was the lowest first-quarter figure since 2014.[2] Subtract Q1 from the half-year total of 16,750 and Q2 works out to about 8,980 deaths, which NHTSA's release frames as a 0.2% year-over-year decline because the agency revised its Q2 2025 base upward, the kind of quiet statistical housekeeping that changes a headline without changing a single body. Either way, the direction is the same. The improvement rate fell by roughly 95% between the first quarter and the second. Q1's decline was more than twenty times Q2's.

Put that against the run it belongs to. Q1 2026 was the 16th consecutive quarterly decline in fatalities, a streak running unbroken since Q2 2022.[2] Q2 makes it 17. A streak that long deserves respect. It also deserves a look at its margin, because the streak now survives on two-tenths of a percent, which is the entire margin.

Had H1 2025's pace of improvement held, a decline of 8.2% instead of 2.5%, the first half of 2026 would have come in near 15,767 deaths, against an actual 16,750. That is about 983 more deaths than the old pace implied, and I am labeling that a rough counterfactual, not a forecast, because the 8.2% was measured against a different base year. Still, nearly a thousand people is a lot of rounding error.

Zoom out one more level and the picture gets stranger. Two different organizations estimated the same six months on the same roads and landed 1,710 deaths apart. NHTSA says 16,750, while the National Safety Council, using National Center for Health Statistics data, said 18,460 in September, a rate of 1.12 against NHTSA's 1.02.[3] Neither number is wrong. NSC counts deaths within 100 days of the crash and includes private roadways, parking lots and driveways. NHTSA's FARS counts deaths within 30 days on public roads only. That definitional gap is worth roughly 1,700 deaths every half-year, and it means the two series should never share a sentence without a label saying which is which. Our deceleration claim is built entirely inside the NHTSA series, Q1 against Q2, so the methodology gap does not touch it. But it does mean the next time someone quotes you a national death toll, ask which count they mean. The answer moves the total by ten percent.

One category went the wrong way while everything else improved, and it is the one that should be most sensitive to infrastructure and enforcement. Bicyclist deaths rose 4% to 1,148 in 2025, up from 1,103 in 2024.[1] Overall deaths fell 6.7% that year. Cyclists did not get the memo, or the memo did not reach the roads they ride on. Reuters notes 2023 was the highest cyclist figure in more than four decades, so this is not a one-year blip but a trend inside a trend, moving in the opposite direction while the national numbers around it keep improving year after year.

The counterargument, at full strength: this is still the best half-year in a decade by the only metric that matters, and a 0.2% Q2 decline after a 4.3% Q1 decline could be noise, seasonality, or regression to the mean. You cannot fall 8% every half-year forever, because the denominator keeps shrinking and the easy wins, post-pandemic normalization and fleet turnover, get used up. Q2 is the deadliest quarter of the year by exposure, summer driving, long trips, more miles, so a thin Q2 decline is not the same signal as a thin Q1 decline. The 17-quarter streak is intact, 2025 was the lowest death count since 2019, and the full-year 2025 rate of 1.10 is the second-lowest ever recorded.[4] Nothing here proves policy failure. It might just be arithmetic destiny.

What this does not prove: all H1 2026 numbers are preliminary statistical projections, subject to revision when the FARS Annual Reporting File for 2025 and the Final File for 2025 land, and again when the 2026 file arrives. NHTSA's own Q1 report says the counts will be slightly revised, and a 0.2% Q2 decline could revise to flat or slightly positive. Do not annualize Q2, and do not treat the 983-death counterfactual as a body count. It is a pace comparison, nothing more. FARS 2014-2023 cannot speak to 2026; every comparison in this article uses published early estimates, not model-level data. And the quarterly split, Q1 versus Q2, comes from NHTSA's release as reported by Reuters on publication day, because the CrashStats research note had not been indexed at the time of writing.

What This Means for You

Do not annualize the headline. A 1.02 half-year rate is not a 1.02 full-year rate, and Q2's near-flat result means full-year 2026 will land closer to 1.05 than to Q1's 0.99 unless the second half re-accelerates. If you ride a bike, the national improvement does not include you: cyclist deaths rose 4% while overall deaths fell 6.7%, so daylight visibility and separated infrastructure matter more than the national trend. Check your own state's numbers, not the national average, because the NSC series showed 11 jurisdictions getting worse by double digits in the first half of 2026 while the country as a whole improved. And remember the streak is 17 quarters long and 0.2% wide. Drive like it ends this quarter, because statistically it nearly did last quarter.
