The Fix Didn’t Fix It: BMW Recalls the Same 720,796 Cars a Second Time

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

BMW recalled 720,796 cars in August 2024 because the electrical connector on the water pump could short out and catch fire. Dealers inspected the connector, replaced it if it looked damaged, and bolted on a small shield to keep fluid off it, and that was supposed to be the fix.[4] On November 4, 2026, BMW recalled the same 720,796 cars again: same part, same fire risk, same number down to the last car.[1]

720,796
The 2024 recall and the 2026 recall cover the exact same cars, down to the last one.

Start with the 2024 diagnosis, because the whole story is the distance between it and the truth. BMW’s engineers concluded that blow-by fluid from the crankcase ventilation system was dripping from the intake hose onto the connector. A shield made sense, if the theory was right, and it was not. In April 2025 a 5 Series outside the US suffered a thermal overload at the connector after receiving the fix, and two months later a 3 Series in the US smoldered at the same spot, also post-repair. BMW spent a year blaming the repairs themselves, then lab work in March 2026 found something no shield was ever going to stop: the connector plastic, a material called PBT, had gone severely brittle, and CT scans found cracks in it. Moisture gets in through cracks, while a shield only diverts drips. Those are two different problems, and the first fix only ever addressed one of them.[1][5]

Now the number that should bother you more than the fire count. BMW’s own quarterly reports to NHTSA show 291,682 of the 720,796 cars had received the 2024 fix by the end of September 2026, while another 14,902 were scrapped, stolen, or exported, and that 40.5 percent completion rate came after more than two years. That leaves 414,212 cars, 57.5 percent of the recall, that never got the first fix at all. And every known US incident since, about 10 of them, happened in a car that had been repaired.[2][6] One incident per roughly 29,000 repaired cars. A recall system only discovers a failed fix from the owners who showed up for it, and barely four in ten did. The 414,212 who never came in could not have told BMW anything, because nobody ever looked at their connectors. The 3 Series sedan and the X3 alone account for 400,430 of the cars, 55.6 percent of the recall, and most of them are now on their second or third owner.[1]

Eighteen months, 552 days, passed between the first post-repair incident and the recall decision on October 28, 2026, and inside that span BMW ran DSC scans, microscopy, and chemical analysis, brought in an outside expert during May, and spent months coordinating with the pump supplier, Pierburg Pump Technology of Neuss, Germany, over whether water was breaking the plastic down from the inside. Hydrolysis is still only a possibility under study, not a conclusion. Here is the part BMW put in writing: the cause field in its own filing to NHTSA is blank. After all that work, the official answer is a shrug with a park-outside order attached. Meanwhile the remedy for the second recall does not exist yet. It is, in BMW’s words, under development, owner letters go out December 25, and VINs become searchable the same day. No dealer can book the repair until the remedy exists, and nobody has said when that will be.[1][3]

Give BMW its due, because the other side of this story is real. Ten incidents, zero crashes, zero injuries, across 720,796 cars. BMW’s own estimate is that fewer than 0.1 percent of these cars, under 721 of them, actually carry the defect. A 40.5 percent completion rate is ordinary for a recall covering 2012–2018 cars that have changed hands twice; second and third owners are the hardest people in America to reach by mail. Recalling every car a second time before knowing the root cause is the cautious move, not the cynical one. All of that is true, and it is also true that the cautious move is an admission, because the first diagnosis was wrong, the first fix was built on it, and the only people who found out were the ones who trusted the fix enough to drive the car home and park it in their garage.

What this story does not prove: the completion figures come from BMW’s quarterly reports as tabulated by Carveyance, not from a NHTSA document read directly for this article, and the underlying report was filed October 29, 2026. The incident count, about 10, is BMW’s own characterization of field reports that relate, or may relate, to the defect, and hydrolysis is unproven. And the fire risk, while real, has produced no crashes or injuries in the US. None of that changes the practical facts: the shield is not the fix, the fix does not exist yet, and the cars are supposed to sleep outside.

If you own one of these cars, a 2012–2018 BMW 2, 3, 4, or 5 Series, X1, X3, X4, X5 plug-in hybrid, or Z4 with a four-cylinder engine, park it outside, away from the house and away from other cars, until a remedy exists. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls starting December 25, 2026. If you already had the 2024 repair done, it does not count, and you go back. If you bought one used in the last two years, check anyway, because the odds are decent nobody ever fixed yours. BMW’s line is 1-800-525-7417, and NHTSA’s is 888-327-4236. A shield that diverted drips could not fix cracked plastic, and the only thing standing between your car and a fire right now is a parking spot in the open air.[2]
