NHTSA Ordered Tesla to Prove the Cybercab Is Legal. Under Oath. By September 30.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 19, 2026

On September 10, the federal government sent Tesla's lawyers homework with handcuffs attached. NHTSA issued a 21-request Special Order demanding the company prove, under oath, that the Cybercab — no steering wheel, no pedals, no mirrors — was legally certified as compliant with federal safety standards.[1] The answers are due September 30, and getting them wrong or leaving them blank carries civil penalties of up to $139 million.[1] Swear to something false and it becomes a criminal matter: a fine, or up to 15 years in prison.[1] The order is signed by NHTSA Chief Counsel Peter Simshauser and addressed directly to Tesla's head of litigation and its associate general counsel for regulatory affairs.[1]

$139 million
maximum civil penalty for failing to answer NHTSA's Special Order fully and truthfully; a sworn falsehood adds criminal exposure of a fine or up to 15 years in prison

Nobody claims the Cybercab is dangerous, and that is the part that should bother you more than any crash video.[8] This is not a recall, not a safety finding, and no injury has been publicly attributed to the car.[8] NHTSA is not asking whether the vehicle works. It is asking whether the certification was legal — whether Tesla swore to compliance it knew it could not claim. The car is on trial for its paperwork.

Start with Request 19, the sharpest of the 21. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 135 says it about as plainly as a regulation can: “The service brakes shall be activated by means of a foot control.”[1] The Cybercab has no foot control. NHTSA notes it has publicly said a manufacturer of a vehicle without a foot-activated service brake could not certify to that standard — then asks Tesla to explain, in detail, how it did exactly that.[1]

A cluster of requests goes after the temporary controls. Tesla is known to use bolt-on human controls during development, and the order wants to know whether the company leaned on them to certify compliance, then stripped them out before the cars carried paying passengers.[1] That runs straight into the Safety Act's “make inoperative” prohibition: you cannot certify a vehicle with the required safety equipment installed and remove it before delivery.[1] NHTSA defines the subject vehicles as those “lacking permanently attached, conventional manual controls” — brake pedal, accelerator, steering wheel, mirrors, telltales.[1]

Tesla does have a defense, and NHTSA clearly expects it. Request 10 presses the company on which standards it considers inapplicable to a car with no driver, on the theory that rules written for humans — mirror adjustment, turn signals that cancel by steering-wheel rotation — were never meant for a vehicle that drives itself.[1] The agency's answer is blunt. Until those rules are formally rewritten, they still apply, and “inapplicable” is not a box the manufacturer gets to check for itself.[1] Which is awkward, because NHTSA spent June proposing to delete the foot-brake rule for automated vehicles.[1] The agency is enforcing a standard it is simultaneously trying to erase. Wanting the rules to change is not the same as the rules having changed.

There was a legal way to do this, and a competitor just walked it. In July, NHTSA granted Amazon's Zoox a temporary Part 555 exemption covering eight federal standards, including the foot-brake and mirror rules Tesla is now being pressed on.[1] The exemption lets Zoox charge for rides in its no-controls pods, caps the fleet at 2,500 vehicles a year, and runs through 2028.[1] Zoox applied, made its safety case, and waited. Tesla certified the Cybercab as already compliant and started charging for rides on September 3 — no application, no exemption, no cap.[1] The arithmetic is simple: a robotaxi business cannot scale at 2,500 vehicles a year, so the exemption path was never going to work for Tesla's ambitions. Self-certification was the only route to scale, and it is now under sworn review. Then there is the velocity: Zoox's equivalent journey, from its own special order in 2022 to its exemption this July, took roughly four years,[9] while Tesla went from audit query to Special Order in seven days.

Meanwhile, a second file sits on the same desk asking the opposite question. On September 9, Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi of Illinois wrote to the Transportation Secretary citing 43 videos of drivers apparently asleep in moving Teslas with Autopilot or FSD engaged, and tied them to engineering analysis EA26002, which covers roughly 3.2 million FSD-equipped vehicles.[5] He asked five questions, due September 30 — the same deadline — including whether the agency's own data points to a defect requiring a recall.[5] So one docket asks how Tesla certified a car built on the premise that no driver is needed, while the other asks what happens when drivers in cars that require one stop driving. For the record, IIHS rated 14 partial-automation safeguard systems in 2024; eleven were poor, none were good, and Tesla's was the worst.[7]

Here is what to do with all of this. If you rode a Cybercab in Austin this month, the vehicle you were in was certified compliant on Tesla's own say-so — specifically, on determinations that certain federal standards simply did not apply.[2] Those determinations are now being tested under oath, with the company's answer due September 30. Three outcomes are on the table: NHTSA accepts the certification, which would validate the inapplicability argument as an industry-wide shortcut; NHTSA rejects it, which could mean a retroactive exemption application, a recall of deployed vehicles, or civil penalties; or NHTSA finalizes its rule changes first, which would dissolve the legal basis of the sharpest questions.[3] None of this says the car is unsafe; it says the legality is unsettled, the deadline is eleven days out, and the person swearing to the answers could go to prison for getting them wrong.

Strongest case against this article: this is paperwork theater over a rulebook everyone agrees is obsolete. NHTSA itself proposed deleting the foot-brake requirement in June, invited the rulemaking that would legalize exactly what Tesla did, and has said it wants to modernize the standards for these vehicles. Tesla's inapplicability argument — that mirror-adjustment rules cannot sensibly apply to a car with no mirrors — is the reasonable reading, and the agency's counter is procedural, not substantive. Fair. But the counterpoint stands: the rulebook may be obsolete, but it is still the law, and Tesla chose the one path — self-certification with no exemption, no application, no cap — that put the legality of a commercial taxi service in the hands of a sworn affidavit. Obsolete rules are changed by rulemaking, not by hoping nobody enforces them.
