The Camper Brand That Keeps Getting Recalled for Carbon Monoxide

Clara Rollover • Consumer Advocate Desk
September 8, 2026

This week, NHTSA logged recall 26V563. The 2027 Forest River Vibe may have an incorrectly installed furnace, one that can leak carbon monoxide into the living space.[1] That sentence alone should get your attention. What comes next should get more: this is the fourth time Forest River has recalled an RV for a carbon-monoxide hazard, and the second time the Vibe itself has been the vehicle in question.

An RV is the only motor vehicle you sleep in. Keep that in mind for everything that follows.

Americans who die each year from unintentional carbon-monoxide poisoning not linked to fires; more than 100,000 visit an emergency department, per the CDC

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless, which is why it kills without warning. Early symptoms read like the flu: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion.[5] If the whole family feels sick at the same time, that is not a stomach bug. People who are sleeping can die from CO poisoning before they ever wake up with symptoms.[5] Now put that gas inside a sealed box, at night, in the woods, with a furnace running.

Rewind six years.

In June 2020, Forest River recalled the 2020 and 2021 Vibe (models VBT16RB-OR and VBT17DB-OR) because the refrigerator's roof vent might be missing entirely, a defect the company's own letter said could lead to "fire, or carbon monoxide poisoning causing personal injury or property damage."[2] A month later, in July 2020, it recalled 2021 Columbus fifth wheels because the furnace exhaust vent sat underneath the rear slide-out, in a spot where exhaust fumes could enter through the slide seals; the letter warned of "Carbon Monoxide poisoning or possibly death."[3] Two recalls, two months, two different ways to pump combustion exhaust into a bedroom.

Then 2022. Forest River recalled its Surveyor travel trailers because the furnace's fresh-air intake tube was too short and could detach from the furnace, and because the burn chamber itself could crack. Either failure, the reporting at the time noted, could release carbon monoxide into the RV.[4] Three strikes across three model lines, and nobody at the company seemed to be asking the obvious question.

This week's recall answers what happened when nobody asked. The 2027 Vibe is back under NHTSA campaign 26V563 for a furnace that can leak carbon monoxide into the living space.[1] The detailed Part 573 report had not been posted publicly as of publication, so the unit count, production window, and exact installation error are not yet available, but the category of failure is unmistakable: it is the third furnace-exhaust-path defect and the Vibe's second carbon-monoxide recall in six years.

Why does this keep happening to the same company? Start with what an RV furnace actually is. It is a third-party appliance, usually a Suburban or Dometic-style unit, installed by the manufacturer into a thin wooden box that doubles as the master bedroom. Sealing that exhaust path through an exterior wall takes real precision, and every slide-out, duct, and cabinet is another chance for the seal to fail. Miss a vent, shorten a tube, tuck the exhaust under a moving wall, and combustion gas goes exactly where the people are.

Cars live under dense federal oversight: crash standards, emissions testing, defect reporting with teeth. Travel trailers do not. Their appliance installations ride on industry self-regulation, RVIA standards that the industry writes for itself, and spot-check oversight. And before anyone writes in: yes, Forest River is one of the largest RV makers in the country, which means its absolute recall counts run hot. Size explains volume. It does not explain the Vibe failing the same safety class twice, six years apart.

The strongest defense here deserves airtime, because it is partly true. Recalls are the safety system working: Forest River found each defect, reported it, and fixed the affected units for free. No injuries or deaths were cited in any of the four recalls. The furnaces themselves are supplier-built, so this is partly a supply-chain and installation problem that the whole industry shares. Four recalls in six years at the biggest manufacturer is not, by raw volume, obviously disproportionate.

But the defense thins with each repetition. Two of the four recalls are furnace exhaust paths, the single deadliest appliance failure in a sleeping space. The Vibe has now been recalled for carbon-monoxide hazards twice. A company can be praised for reporting a defect and still be asked why the same class of defect keeps shipping. At some point, "we keep catching it" stops being a defense and starts being a confession about the factory.

What should you do? If you own a 2027 Vibe, or a 2020-2021 Vibe VBT16RB-OR or VBT17DB-OR, a 2021 Columbus fifth wheel, or a 2022 Surveyor, run your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls once the owner letters post; the fix is free and it is hardware, not software. Confirm your CO detector exists, has power, and is not expired. They last about five to seven years, and a detector with a dead sensor is furniture.

Until the furnace is fixed, do not sleep with it running in a recalled unit. Learn the early symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, the flu-like cluster that fools everyone. If everyone in the camper feels sick at once, get out and call 911. And if you are shopping for a used RV, run the VIN through NHTSA before you buy, then look at the furnace vent on the outside wall: soot staining, gaps, or cracked housing are all reasons to walk away.

You can forgive a company one carbon-monoxide recall. You can almost forgive two. At four, across six years, in the vehicle people sleep in, the question is no longer whether the recall system works. It is why the factory keeps needing it.
