The Gap

IIHS Tested Work Trucks for the First Time. The Child Dummy Vanished.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 11, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds, except this time the vehicle isn’t doing anything at all. On October 8, IIHS released its first-ever front crash prevention ratings for commercial vehicles, and the headline finding isn’t a rating. It’s a disappearance: in the pedestrian tests, the child dummy was detected at a distance, then “disappeared” as the truck got closer. Tall front ends and sensors mounted up near the rearview mirror. A four-foot kid, below the sightline, inside the killing zone, invisible to the machine that was supposed to save them.[1]

16%
Of all U.S. roadway fatalities in 2024 happened in crashes involving heavy-duty vehicles, medium-duty vehicles, or light vans. 6,215 dead.

The rating table reads like a report card nobody wanted to bring home. Eleven vehicles went in: seven cargo vans, four Class 3 pickups. In the vehicle-to-vehicle test, only the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500 earned Good. A Ford F-350 could avoid the passenger-car target at 31 mph and, at 43 mph, failed to reduce its speed at all. Chevrolet’s Silverado 3500HD didn’t avoid the car in a single one of its six trials. Motorcycles were worse still: several systems never issued a warning or slowed in most of the motorcycle scenarios. IIHS President David Harkey called the results “a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers,” which is the polite way of saying the machines are blind.[1][3]

Now for the part that made me put down my coffee. Poor marks for the Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500, and the reason isn’t a bad algorithm. They don’t have automatic emergency braking, and it isn’t coming in a mid-cycle refresh. Not optional-in-a-package. They ship with an optional forward collision warning and nothing that touches the brake pedal, and they are, to this day, among the most common work vans on American roads.[1] Two of eleven tested vehicles, 18% of the sample, selling in 2026 without the one technology that decides the test. Meanwhile the pedestrian Good ratings went to the Chevrolet BrightDrop 400 and the Rivian RCV-Delivery 500, two electric delivery vans, which proves the problem is not physics. Physics is what lets a 14,000-pound pickup stop short. So the problem is a fleet buyer’s spreadsheet.

The honest counterargument deserves full volume, because it’s strong. These ratings are brand new, while passenger cars have had decades of IIHS pressure to get good at this; commercial manufacturers just got their first report card on Thursday, and vehicle development cycles run five years, not five weeks. Physics is also genuinely harder: a loaded Class 3 pickup needs far more distance to stop than a sedan, and the test protocols were built for passenger cars. A marginal rating on a 14,000-pound vehicle might represent more engineering than a Good on a Civic. And the 6,215 fatality figure counts crashes involving these vehicles, not crashes caused by them: a work van can be the vehicle that gets hit. None of that is wrong, and none of it changes the geometry of a child under a sightline, or the fact that two of the tested vans have no AEB to begin with. You cannot fix sensor placement with a software update, and you cannot fix absence with an excuse.[1][2]

What this piece does not prove, stated plainly: IIHS tested eleven models, one configuration each. Only the Express and Savana are confirmed to lack AEB; the rest of the field has systems of varying quality, and the ratings don’t tell you which failure mode (sensing, warning timing, braking authority) sank each marginal score. FARS’s fatality figure is from 2024 and these ratings are from 2026 model vehicles, so don’t draw a straight line between them. And these are controlled-track results with dummies, not chaos on I-95.

So here is what to do with this information. If you buy vehicles for a living, fleet managers especially: the Good-rated electric vans prove AEB is deployable on work vehicles right now, so write it into the purchase spec and stop accepting bids from vans that brake with nothing. If you’re a parent: treat every tall-hooded work truck near a school or crosswalk as if its eyes are mounted too high to see your kid, because IIHS just proved that, in the relevant sense, they are. And if you want the structural fix: Advocates for Highway and Auto Safety is pushing a federal AEB standard for heavy vehicles, citing NHTSA’s June 2023 estimate that it could save 155 lives and prevent 8,814 injuries a year.[2] The dummy disappeared on a test track. It doesn’t have to disappear on your street.
