The Gap

The Crash Already Happened. Now Your Car's Weight Casts the Deciding Vote.

MIA CRUMPLEZONE • Clautomotive Desk
October 22, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. Your airbags haven't finished deploying and your crumple zones are mid-sacrifice while momentum conservation is already deciding how much of the crash your body absorbs versus how much the other car absorbs, and it is not a negotiation.

Death rates per 100 million miles get all the attention, but they quietly blend two different risks into one number: how often a vehicle's occupants end up in a fatal crash, and whether they survive it. Split those apart and the data turns brutal. A Ford Mustang kills 0.70 of its own occupants every time it is in a fatal crash, while a Ram 1500 kills 0.34. Same dataset. Different question. Very different answer.

0.70 vs 0.34
Occupant deaths per fatal crash: Ford Mustang vs Ram 1500, FARS 2014–2023

Call it the post-crash fatality index: take every vehicle a nameplate put into a fatal crash from 2014 through 2023, count the dead occupants, divide. The Mustang tops the ranking at 0.696, with the Ford Ranger right behind it at 0.690, a pickup. At the bottom sit the Ram 1500 at 0.341 and the Ford F-250 at 0.364, also pickups, the same brand and the same vehicle class, separated by nearly a factor of two.

Class medians tell the same story at scale: sedans 0.641, SUVs 0.535, pickups 0.471. A Civic kills 0.68 of its occupants per fatal crash while a Silverado kills 0.49, because in those first 150 milliseconds the lighter vehicle changes velocity faster, its occupants ride down more energy with less structure in front of them, and physics does not offer partial credit. This is exactly what crash compatibility means, and it is the least advertised safety feature on the window sticker.

IIHS put numbers on the mechanism in February 2025: below the roughly 4,000-pound fleet average, every 500 pounds of added weight cut driver deaths by 17 per million registered vehicle-years, but above 4,000 pounds the benefit collapsed to one while the extra weight added seven deaths per million among the people in the other car. “For American drivers, the conventional wisdom is that if bigger is safer, even bigger must be safer still,” said IIHS president David Harkey. “These results show that isn't true today, not for people in other cars, and, this is important, not for the occupants of the large vehicles themselves.” A separate analysis of more than 30,000 two-vehicle fatal crashes found hatchback occupants dying at rates above 50 percent when paired against large SUVs.

One honest objection: this index cannot separate mass from role: heavy trucks are often the striking vehicle, so their low scores partly reflect the other car's occupants dying instead, deaths this metric does not count. Crash mix, occupancy, belt use, and rural exposure all bleed into the number, so read it for what it is, not a crash-test rating but a scoreboard of what happens to your occupants once the crash is already underway. The strongest case against the thesis is that the truck's number looks good precisely because the sedan's number looks bad, and that is not a flaw in the arithmetic. That is the point.

If you drive something light, the threat is the other lane, and IIHS keeps landing on the same sweet spot: midsize vehicles, roughly 3,500 to 4,200 pounds, heavy enough to survive the truck without becoming the truck. Belts on, obviously, and ease off on two-lane rural roads where head-on strikes happen. Next time a five-star barrier rating gets quoted at you, remember the barrier never swerves, because an F-250 does.
