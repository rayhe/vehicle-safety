Ford's Family Sedan Was America's Default Car. Its Death Rate Is Anything But Average

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 26, 2026

For a decade, if you pictured a normal American car, you pictured something shaped like a Ford Taurus. Federal crash data says that normal car was deadlier per mile than every mainstream rival in its class. Between 2014 and 2023, Tauruses were involved in 3,265 crashes where someone died, and 2,208 people died in them, at a rate of 2.74 deaths per 100 million miles traveled.[1] Run the same math on a Toyota Camry and you get 2.03. On a Toyota Corolla, 1.85. On a Chevrolet Malibu, 2.03.[2] In plain terms, the Taurus rate sits 35% above the Camry and Malibu and 48% above the Corolla.

2.74
Ford Taurus occupant deaths per 100 million miles, 2014-2023, vs 1.85 for the Toyota Corolla

First, kill the obvious excuse. Impaired drivers do not explain this gap, because Taurus drivers in fatal crashes were impaired at almost exactly the same rate as Toyota drivers. Among Taurus drivers with toxicology data, 18.9% tested positive for alcohol or drugs, against 19.2% for Camry drivers and 19.2% for Corolla drivers.[3] A three-tenths-of-a-point difference in impairment cannot carry a 35 to 48% difference in death rate. Whatever made the Taurus deadly was not who was driving it.

Next, run the control Ford itself provides. Crown Victoria was Ford's other big sedan of the same era, same maker, same sedan job, and it rates 1.46 deaths per 100 million miles, nearly half the Taurus.[2] So "being a Ford sedan in the 2000s" is not the answer either. Something specific to the Taurus, its age mix, or its crashworthiness is doing the work here, and the model-year pattern points straight at the old car. Taurus deaths concentrate in 1999 through 2007 cars, peaking at 266 deaths for model year 2003, then collapse after the 2009 redesign.[1] That plateau is exactly the fleet still circulating at the bottom of the used market today.

None of this makes the Taurus uniquely cursed. A Honda Accord rates 3.07 and a Chevrolet Impala rates 5.00 in the same dataset, so plenty of mainstream sedans look worse.[2] Nor does it prove the Taurus structure alone is at fault: old cheap cars are driven by younger, poorer, less-belted drivers on worse roads, and this dataset cannot fully separate the car from its driver and its roads. But a buyer does not get to separate them. Roads are what they are, other traffic is what it is, and in the world the Taurus actually drives in, it kills more often per mile than its rivals. Size and weight research from the insurance industry explains part of any sedan-versus-sedan gap in multi-vehicle crashes, but it cannot explain a gap between sedans of the same class.[4]

So if you drive an older Taurus, treat it like what the numbers say it is: a car from the plateau years, not a modern safety cage. Belt every seat on every trip, keep the tires and restraints maintained, and do not let familiarity pass for safety. If you are shopping at the bottom of the used market, let model year beat badge loyalty, since a post-2009 car with better crash structure is worth stretching for over a cheaper car from the 2003 peak. Before buying anything used, check its IIHS ratings and run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. A familiar nameplate is not a safety rating.
