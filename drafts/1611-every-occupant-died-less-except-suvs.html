The Gap

Every Vehicle Occupant Died Less in 2024. Except SUV Occupants.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

Somebody died less in 2024, almost everybody in fact, and NHTSA counted 39,254 traffic deaths for the year, down 1,771 from 2023, a 4.3 percent drop and the third straight year of improvement, with the fatality rate falling to 1.19 deaths per 100 million miles, the lowest since before the pandemic scrambled everything.[1] Car occupants died less, pickup occupants died less, van occupants died less, and so did motorcyclists, pedestrians, and cyclists. Read down NHTSA's own summary of what improved and it runs for a full page, category after category, every line pointing the same direction, and then the list of what got worse starts, and it has two entries: older drivers, up 115 deaths, and SUV occupants, up 112.[1]

+112
The increase in SUV occupant deaths in 2024, from 6,901 to 7,013, while every other occupant category fell. Passenger car occupant deaths fell 1,153 in the same year.

One hundred and twelve is not a large number next to 39,254. Say that first, because it matters, and because a reporter who hides the denominator is selling something, but the direction is the story. Passenger car occupant deaths fell 9.7 percent, from 11,828 to 10,675. Pickup occupant deaths fell 4.9 percent, from 4,321 to 4,111. Van occupant deaths fell 5.7 percent, from 965 to 910. Large truck occupants fell 4.4 percent, motorcyclists 2.2 percent, pedestrians 3.9 percent, cyclists 6.0 percent.[1] SUVs went the other way, from 6,901 dead occupants to 7,013, and they did it in a year when the country drove more miles, 3.29 trillion of them, up 1.5 percent, so the improvement everywhere else happened while exposure rose.[1]

Injuries tell the same story louder, because injuries are counted in the hundreds of thousands and the noise washes out. SUV occupants injured rose by 28,952, up 4.2 percent, to 723,240, a statistically significant increase, while total injuries fell 0.8 percent and passenger car occupants injured fell 4.6 percent.[1] Nearly three in ten people hurt on American roads in 2024 were inside an SUV. Pickup occupants injured fell 9.2 percent in the same year, which is the kind of split that makes you check whether somebody transposed two rows of a table. Nobody did. SUVs now account for 30.9 percent of all passenger vehicle occupant deaths, up from 28.7 percent a year earlier, and SUV occupants died in greater numbers than pickup and van occupants combined, 7,013 against 5,021, a gap of 1,992.[1]

Now the part that makes this a paradox instead of a press release. Our own FARS per-model data, ten years of fatal crashes from 2014 through 2023, says SUVs are the safest vehicles on the road per mile driven.[2] Eleven of the fifteen lowest death-rate models with more than 500 deaths are SUVs: the Toyota RAV4 at 0.19 deaths per 100 million miles, the Honda Pilot at 0.29, the Hyundai Tucson at 0.34, the Nissan Rogue at 0.35, the Chevrolet Equinox at 0.36, the Hyundai Santa Fe at 0.39, the Toyota Highlander at 0.42, the Subaru Outback at 0.45, the Ford Edge at 0.46, the Jeep Grand Cherokee at 0.51, the Honda CR-V at 0.53.[2] At the other end of the same table sit sedans and sports cars, the Hyundai Veloster at 8.54, the Ford Mustang at 6.02, the Chevrolet Impala at 5.00, the Nissan Maxima at 5.11. IIHS agrees from a different angle: its July 2026 driver death rate study put the Audi Q7, a large SUV, at the lowest driver death rate of any model it rated.[3] So the safest individual vehicles in America are SUVs, and the SUV class still killed more people. Both facts are true at the same time, and the tension between them is the whole story.

Exposure resolves the tension, or at least it is the best explanation the data supports, and it is worth spelling out because the alternative explanations fail on contact. SUVs did not suddenly get more dangerous per mile. Nothing in the crash test record says so, and the IIHS 2026 award list is thick with SUVs, 40 of its 53 Top Safety Pick+ winners. What changed is the denominator: America keeps buying SUVs and driving them more miles, and a small per-mile risk multiplied by a much larger number of miles produces more bodies even as the rate stays low. Passenger car occupant deaths fell 9.7 percent partly because there are fewer passenger cars left to die in, and SUV occupant deaths rose partly because the SUV is now the default American vehicle, the thing in the driveway and the thing in the next lane and the thing in the crash report. A rate and a count are different objects, and 2024 is the year they walked in opposite directions for the body style that defines the American road.

Fairness demands the counterargument at full strength, because one year is a thin reed and this column has watched one-year blips get mistaken for trends before. One hundred and twelve deaths on a base of 6,901 is 1.6 percent, and NHTSA itself notes that fatality counts are a census, not a significance test, so the rise could be noise. Our per-model rates end in 2023, which means no 2024 per-model number can confirm the exposure thesis yet, and the 2024 figures come from the Annual Report File, which NHTSA revises into a Final File about a year later. Meanwhile the 2025 early estimate puts total deaths at 36,640, down 6.7 percent,[5] and if SUV occupant deaths fall with the rest in 2025, this piece will read like a man shouting at a blip. That is a real possibility, and it belongs in the open. What keeps the finding worth printing is that the injury data moved the same way, in the same year, by a much larger margin, and injuries are the harder number to dismiss as noise.

Zoom out one more level and the SUV number sits inside a larger migration that NHTSA's own chart makes hard to miss. People killed inside a vehicle fell to 62 percent of all traffic deaths in 2024, the lowest share since the series began, down from 80 percent in 1996, while people killed outside a vehicle, motorcyclists, pedestrians, cyclists, and everyone else without a steel cage around them, rose to a record 38 percent.[1] Cars got safer faster than the fleet changed shape, and the deaths moved toward the people and vehicles with the least protection. SUVs sit on the boundary of that migration: safer than cars for their own occupants per mile, deadlier than cars for everyone they hit, and now numerous enough that their own rising body count shows up in the national totals even in a year when almost everything improved.

So what do you do with a number like this? If you are shopping, do not read it as a reason to buy a sedan. Individual SUV models remain among the safest vehicles per mile in the federal data, and the 2026 IIHS list is full of them. Read it as a reason to check one rating in particular, because the updated moderate overlap front test, the one with a small dummy in the back seat, is where SUVs and pickups keep failing, and the Forester, Palisade, CX-90, and Telluride all earned Top Safety Pick+ while the RAV4 and CR-V missed it on exactly that test.[3] If you already drive an SUV, the levers are the boring ones: the belt, the speed, the phone in the cupholder instead of the hand, because the behavioral factors that kill SUV occupants are the same three that kill everyone else, and NHTSA's Venn diagram for 2024 shows 57 percent of passenger vehicle drivers in fatal crashes had none of the three, drunk, speeding, or unbelted, which means the majority of fatal crashes start with an ordinary driver making an ordinary mistake.[1] And if you make policy, the question 2024 asks is whether a safety system built around protecting the people inside the vehicle has anything left to say about the year the people inside the safest vehicles started dying more anyway.

One year does not make a trend, it makes a question. Watch the 2025 numbers when the Final File lands, and watch whether the SUV line bends back down with everything else or keeps climbing while the sedans keep falling. If it bends back, this was noise, and if it does not, the safest year in a decade had a hole in it, and the hole was shaped exactly like the car in your driveway.
