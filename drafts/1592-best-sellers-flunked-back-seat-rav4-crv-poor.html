The Gap

America's Two Best-Selling SUVs Flunked the Back-Seat Test. The Cheapest Car in the Batch Fixed Its Belts and Passed.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 26, 2026

In the first 150 milliseconds of a 40 mph crash, the front seat of a redesigned 2026 Toyota RAV4 does everything the engineers promised. Seat belts hold, the airbag catches, the structure stays put, and the driver dummy walks away with Good marks across the board. Then look one row back, where a rear dummy, built to the size of a 12-year-old child, gets a shoulder belt sliding toward its neck and a lap belt slipping off its pelvis onto its abdomen, and the Institute calls the result Acceptable, which is the polite way of saying the back seat is not finished yet.[1]

883,056
Combined 2025 US sales of the Toyota RAV4 (479,288) and Honda CR-V (403,768). Neither earns a 2026 IIHS award.

That was the story of IIHS's October 22 ratings release, and the RAV4 was not even the worst news in it. Six vehicles went through the updated moderate overlap front test, which puts a fifth-percentile female dummy in the second row behind the driver. Two earned Good: the 2027 Volkswagen Jetta and the 2026-27 Nissan Kicks, and the Kicks only counts if it was built after June 2026. Audi's redesigned Q3 scored Poor, Chevrolet's Trailblazer scored Poor, and a RAV4 plug-in hybrid scored Marginal. Out of six, the two family SUVs America buys more than any others could not clear the bar in the seat where their kids ride.[1]

Honda's CR-V did worse, and it did worse across the board. IIHS rated the CR-V Poor in the same test, with a Poor score for rear passenger restraints and dummy kinematics and a Marginal score for chest injury measures, and the rating applies to every CR-V from model years 2023 through 2027, not one model year but an entire generation. A 2024 CR-V LX took the test, test ID CEF2343, and the numbers behind the letter grade are worth reading: structure Good, driver injury measures all Good, rear head and neck Good, rear chest Marginal, rear restraints Poor. Shoulder belt tension hit 4.3 kN, the driver is protected, and the passenger behind the driver is not, not to the standard IIHS set.[2]

Now the sales math, because scale is what turns a test result into a story. Toyota sold 479,288 RAV4s in the United States in 2025, making it the best-selling SUV in the country and the third best-selling vehicle of any kind, behind only the Ford F-Series and the Silverado. Honda sold 403,768 CR-Vs, the second best-selling SUV. Add them and you get 883,056 buyers in one year, and not one of them drove home in a vehicle that earns a 2026 IIHS award. Twelve small SUVs earned one, and the RAV4 and the CR-V are not among them. An award is not a trophy; it is the Institute's list of cars that passed every test it runs, and the two cars at the top of the sales chart failed the same one.[4]

What should embarrass the industry is the fix, because the cheapest car in the batch already did it. Nissan's Kicks scored Poor when the redesigned model launched for 2025. Nissan revised the rear seat belts and load limiters, and Kicks built after June 2026 score Good and carry a Top Safety Pick award. Four months of running changes, no redesign, no new platform. Audi reworked the Q3's rear seat belts for builds after May 2026 and still scored Poor. Chevrolet upgraded the Trailblazer's rear belt load limiters for builds after February 2026 and still scored Poor. Money did not solve this; geometry did, and only Nissan found it.[1]

Fairness demands the other side, stated at full strength. Both SUVs score Good in the small overlap front test and the updated side test, and their drivers are as protected as any car IIHS has tested. Neither car violates a federal standard, neither is under recall, and no defect finding exists against either one. IIHS keeps moving its own bar: the rear dummy arrived in 2022, the scoring criteria tightened in February 2024, and both of these vehicles were deep in development when the test changed. RAV4 scored Acceptable, not Poor, and an Acceptable rear seat in a car that aces everything else is a defensible place to be. All true, and none of it changes the shape of the failure, which is a belt that cannot stay on a small passenger's pelvis at 40 mph, in the seat where a family's smallest passengers sit.

What this test does not prove matters too. One crash scenario at 40 mph is not a fatality rate, and FARS death data for 2026 models will not exist for years. A Poor in the updated moderate overlap test says nothing about side impacts, rollovers, or the crash avoidance systems that prevent the crash entirely, and both SUVs carry standard automatic emergency braking that scores well. Ratings also attach to build dates and trims, so a 2025 RAV4 is a different car from the redesigned 2026, and a Kicks built in May 2026 is a different car from one built in July. An award is IIHS's bar, not the law, and missing it is not the same as being unsafe in any absolute sense.

What you do with this depends on which seat your kids ride in: if you are shopping a RAV4 or a CR-V, know what you are buying, a front seat that aces everything and a back seat that does not, and decide accordingly if your kids ride in row two. If you are shopping a Kicks, check the certification label on the driver door jamb and buy one built after June 2026; the same model year, built a month earlier, carries a Poor. If the back seat is the deciding factor, the 2026 Subaru Forester earned Good in this test and a Top Safety Pick+ award, and the Hyundai Kona, Mazda CX-30, and Honda HR-V did the same. Before you sign anything, open iihs.org/ratings, find the exact model year and build date, and read the moderate overlap line yourself, because the window sticker will not tell you any of this.[3]
