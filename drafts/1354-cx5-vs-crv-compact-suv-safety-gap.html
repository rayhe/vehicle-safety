The Gap

Same Segment, Same Drivers, Four Times the Deaths

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 1, 2026

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. Across a decade of federal crash data, the Mazda CX-5 records 0.12 deaths per 100 million miles driven, the lowest rate of any mainstream gasoline SUV in America. Sitting in the same showroom row, the same price bracket, the same suburban driveway archetype, the Honda CR-V records 0.53. That is a 4.4-to-1 gap between two vehicles your neighbors cross-shop on the same Saturday afternoon, and it is not a rounding error.

4.4x
The Honda CR-V's fatality rate (0.53) divided by the Mazda CX-5's (0.12), per 100M miles, FARS 2014–2023

The usual excuses die fast, because the toxicology tells tell no such story. In fatal crashes, impairment rates were 19.8 percent (CX-5), 17.6 percent (CR-V), 18.4 percent (RAV4), 19.4 percent (Equinox), and 20.5 percent (Forester). Across the whole segment, impairment sits inside a three-point band, so it is not the drivers.

Ask the test labs, and they agree with the morgue data, which is rarer than it should be. The 2026 CX-5 earned IIHS Top Safety Pick+, with Good ratings across crash tests and both crash-prevention evaluations.[3] Mazda has now crossed 100 total IIHS awards since the program began.[3] When the labs and the body counts agree, pay attention.

Now the part where I refuse to sell you a fairy tale. Fleet age is the strongest argument against this finding. Every CX-5 was born into the modern era: stability control, good small-overlap structures, standard automatic emergency braking. Honda has sold CR-Vs since the 1990s, and its rate carries pre-2012 models that lacked modern crash structures entirely. Part of the gap is just that: the average CR-V on the road is older.

But age cannot carry the whole 4.4x, so consider the RAV4, a nameplate as ancient as the CR-V's, which still scores 0.19, nearly three times better than the CR-V. If old nameplates were doomed, the RAV4 would sit up there with the CR-V. It does not. Design and standard safety equipment matter independently of nameplate age: Mazda made i-Activsense driver assistance standard across CX-5 trims years before Honda did the same with Honda Sensing on the CR-V. That choice shows up in the data.

What the numbers do not prove matters as much as what they do. FARS records fatal crashes only, a sliver of the roughly 6.7 million total crashes each year, so a low fatality rate does not guarantee a low injury rate. These rates use estimated fleet sizes and miles driven, not odometer readings, so meaningful uncertainty applies to lower-volume models. And none of this says your specific CR-V is a death trap: a well-maintained 2024 with Honda Sensing is a fundamentally different machine than a 2009 without it.

So here is the actionable version, since outrage without a to-do list is just noise. If you are cross-shopping compact SUVs, the CX-5 deserves a serious look on safety grounds alone. If you are buying a used CR-V, treat the model year as a safety feature: 2017 and newer brought Honda Sensing into the lineup, and the price premium buys real crash protection, not just a fresher touchscreen. Whatever you buy, run the VIN through nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands: ten minutes, no excuses.
