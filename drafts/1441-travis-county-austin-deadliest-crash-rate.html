Travis County Killed 146 People Last Year. Per Crash, It Was the Deadliest Big County in Texas.

Axle McScatter • Data Visualization Editor
November 8, 2026

Travis County, Texas killed 146 people on the road in 2025.[3] That is the fifth-largest body count of any county in the state, behind Harris, Dallas, Bexar, and Tarrant, and it is also the most misleading ranking in the dataset: Travis County holds only 1.36 million people against Harris County’s 5 million. Divide by population and Travis jumps to first: 10.71 deaths per 100,000 residents, the highest rate of any of Texas’s six biggest counties.[3]

8.53
Fatal crashes per 1,000 total crashes in Travis County, 2025, vs 4.44 in Harris County and 3.89 in Bexar County (TxDOT county tables)

Per-capita rankings can flatter a small county, so I ran the second denominator too, and Travis leads that one as well. TxDOT’s county table counts 136 fatal crashes out of 15,939 total crashes in Travis County, a rate of 8.53 per 1,000.[1] Tarrant County, the next most lethal of the Big Six, ran 6.11. Dallas ran 5.87, Harris 4.44, Bexar 3.89, El Paso 3.75. A crash in Travis County was 1.92 times as likely to be fatal as one in Harris County and 2.19 times as likely as one in Bexar County. Travis is the only big county in Texas that leads on both denominators at once: the most deaths per resident and the most deaths per crash.

Austin is not diluting the county. It is the county. TxDOT’s Cities and Towns report counts 94 fatal crashes and 98 deaths inside Austin city limits in 2025, out of 11,032 total crashes, a fatal-crash rate of 8.52 per 1,000, statistically identical to the county’s 8.53.[2] Austin accounts for 69 percent of the county’s fatal crashes and deaths, which means the lethality is not a rural-fringe artifact, it is downtown. Against the other big Texas cities the gap widens: Houston 4.53 per 1,000, Fort Worth 6.95, Dallas 6.69.[2] Austin logged 11,032 crashes against Houston’s 60,317, and kills at nearly double the rate per crash.

Now the twist. Austin’s own Vision Zero report for 2025 counts 99 traffic deaths and 301 serious injuries, and the two numbers moved in opposite directions: deaths rose about 2 percent from 2024 while serious injuries fell 28 percent, the lowest serious-injury count since the city adopted Vision Zero in 2015.[4] Pedestrian deaths fell from 39 to 30, motorcyclist serious injuries from 62 to 44, bicyclist serious injuries from 27 to 21, and combined fatalities plus serious injuries per capita fell from 49.3 to 37.9 per 100,000 residents.[4] On the streets Austin controls, the program is working. On the roads that kill, Austin is a bystander: an estimated 65 to 75 percent of the city’s traffic deaths happen on state-owned roads, interstates, highways, and frontage roads, where TxDOT sets the design and the speed.[4]

Speed is the variable that explains the split, and the statewide numbers say so plainly. Crashes on 75-mph roads killed 21.50 people per 1,000 in 2025, against 3.19 on 30-mph streets, a 6.7-times gap.[3] Rural roads posted 70 mph or higher ran 27.08 per 1,000, while urban streets at 35 mph or less ran 2.81, a 9.6-times gap.[3] Austin spent 2026 lowering speed limits and calming its high-injury roadways, and the city’s October speed management report counts a 17 percent decline in severe crashes on the treated streets.[4] That is real progress where the city has authority, and it leaves the deadliest roads untouched: as of November 6, Austin had recorded 79 fatal crashes and 81 deaths in 2026, against 81 and 85 on the same date in 2025.[4]

Give the ranking its proper discount before believing it. Travis County logged only 15,939 total crashes against Harris County’s 107,472, so its rate is more sensitive to a bad year, and reporting differences could move the numbers: under-reported rural minor crashes inflate rural rates, over-reported Austin ones deflate its own. But the lead is large, 8.53 against the next-highest 6.11, a 40 percent gap, and the per-capita lead uses a completely different denominator, population rather than crashes, so a paperwork quirk would have to move both the same way to manufacture it. Two independent counts of Austin’s deaths also agree, TxDOT’s Cities table at 98 and the city’s own Vision Zero report at 99. Austin’s serious-injury decline could still mean the city is genuinely getting safer and 2025’s death count is noise, but the 2026 numbers so far do not support a turnaround, and the fatal crashes live where the city’s tools do not reach.

What to do with this depends on where you drive. In Austin, the risk is not getting into a crash, it is how lethal the crash is, so the fatal risk concentrates on the high-speed, state-owned roads, I-35, US 183, SH 130, the MoPac frontage roads, not on the calmed neighborhood streets. Slow down on the interstates: the statewide speed ladder says a crash at 75 mph kills at 6.7 times the rate of a crash at 30 mph, and Travis County’s lethality is a speed story wearing a geography costume. On foot, cross at intersections and wear something reflective after dark, because statewide 84 percent of pedestrian deaths happened away from intersections and 76 percent happened in the dark. And before assuming the big city is the dangerous place, check your own county in the TxDOT tables: in Texas, the deadliest crashes per mile happen where the fewest people live, except in Travis County, where they happen right downtown.
