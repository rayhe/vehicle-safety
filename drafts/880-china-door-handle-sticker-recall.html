The Gap

China Recalled 4.3 Million Cars Because Nobody Could Find the Door Handle. The Fix Is a Sticker.

Mia Crumplezone • Safety Engineering Editor
September 11, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds after a severe crash, when the power dies and the doors stay shut. Your exit plan is a mechanical emergency release, a small lever most owners have never touched. On August 21, China decided that if you cannot find that lever in the dark with the power off, the car is defective. About 4.3 million vehicles are being recalled over it, the largest auto recall in Chinese history.[1]

4.3 million
vehicles recalled in China over door handles that hide the emergency exit — the country's largest auto recall on record

Tesla filed the biggest share: 2,975,910 vehicles, including 973,156 China-built Model 3s and 1,956,713 China-built Model Ys, plus imported 3s, Ss and Xs, effective September 25. Xiaomi, Leapmotor, Xpeng and Geely each filed their own largest-ever recalls on top of it.[2] The defect is not mechanical. It is cartographic. China's market regulator says the interior emergency release may be difficult to identify after a crash that disables the electrical system, because the release closely matches the surrounding trim color. In plain terms: the map to the exit was erased by the interior designer.[1]

Here is the design review failure, stated plainly. The flush electronic handle is the primary. The mechanical release is the backup for the primary. And the backup is camouflaged. It is a backup plan that needed its own warning label. This is not a hypothetical failure mode. Chinese state media reported that the driver of a Xiaomi SU7 died in an October crash-and-fire after passersby could not open the doors to pull him out. In the United States, lawsuits have alleged that Tesla's handle designs trapped drivers or passengers in burning vehicles, and NHTSA has received complaints from drivers who could not get children out of cars.[3] JD Power flagged this back in 2023, noting that seven of the ten most problematic door-handle models were EVs. This is an industry-wide fashion, not one company's quirk.[1]

Now the remedy, which deserves its own moment. For 4.3 million cars, the official fix is warning labels plus an over-the-air software update that automatically lowers the windows after a collision. No hardware change. The powered handle still dies with the power; now there is a sticker pointing at the thing that doesn't need power.[2] China is also doing the real thing: in February it became the first country to ban "hidden" door handles outright, effective 2027.[1]

And here is the gap this column is named for. In July, NHTSA reviewed a petition seeking a recall of roughly 180,000 Tesla Model 3s over emergency door releases that may not be clear in an emergency, and denied it — deciding the issue belongs in a rulemaking for a potential new federal standard instead of a recall. By late August, NHTSA said automakers had not notified it of any plans to file a similar recall in the US market.[1] Europe's regulators are drafting standards requiring electronic handles to open after power is cut.[3] A proposed US bill would require a fail-safe manual release on cars with hidden-style handles, but it is only a proposal. Same hardware, same defect, two continents: one gets a 4.3-million-car recall and a 2027 ban. The other gets a rulemaking and a promise.[1]

What to do: find your mechanical emergency release today, in the driveway, with the power on — before you ever need it in the dark with the power off. In a Model 3 or Y it is the small lever in front of the window switches, the one the Chinese regulator says blends into the trim. Pull it. Make sure it works. Then show every passenger who rides in your back seat, because in a crash they are the ones who need it and you may not be conscious enough to give directions. If you are shopping for a car with flush handles, ask the salesperson to show you the manual release before you sign anything.[1]
