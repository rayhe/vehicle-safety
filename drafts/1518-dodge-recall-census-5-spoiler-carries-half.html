Dodge Filed Five Recalls in 2026. One Spoiler Was Nearly Half of Them.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 15, 2026

Five times in 2026, Dodge walked into NHTSA with a new defect. Add up the five campaign populations and you get 153,660 vehicles.[1] Now look at where that number lives, because it is not spread evenly. A rear spoiler on the Durango that does not sit flush with the roof accounts for 74,578 of them. Nearly half of Dodge’s entire recall year is one body panel, fitted wrong, on a truck that has been in production since 2011.

153,660
Campaign-vehicles across Dodge’s five 2026 NHTSA recalls, counting only Dodge’s share of multi-brand campaigns. Populations overlap in principle, so this counts recall actions, not unique vehicles.

Let’s talk about what happens when the parts do not fit. April brought the first campaign, 26V262: 8,528 Charger Daytona EVs from model years 2024 and 2025, with instrument panel software that can fail and take the gear indicator, brake warnings, stability control warnings and tire pressure warnings with it.[2] August brought three in nineteen days. Campaign 26V510: 40,386 Hornets from 2023 through 2025, with rear outboard seat belts that twist inside the retractor and stop pulling tight.[4] Campaign 26V528: 74,578 Durangos from 2021 through 2023, with a rear spoiler built over-flush to the roof, scraping the roof every time the liftgate cycles until the spoiler works loose and detaches at speed.[5] Campaign 26V531: 16,838 Chargers from 2026 and 2027, with radio software that can keep the rearview camera image from appearing when the driver shifts into reverse.[1] October brought the last one, 26V701: 13,330 brand-new 2026 Durangos, with terminals in the tail light wiring harness that may be damaged, so the tail lights may simply not work.[7]

Shares first, because shares are the story: spoiler, 48.5 percent of the year’s total; Hornet seat belts, 26.3 percent; Charger camera, 11.0 percent; Durango tail lights, 8.7 percent; Charger Daytona cluster, 5.5 percent. One recall is bigger than the Hornet and Charger camera campaigns combined, 74,578 against 57,224, and it is bigger than the other two Durango and Charger campaigns put together. If you want to understand Dodge’s 2026 in one sentence, it is this: a spoiler that does not fit became the year.

Here is the number that made me set down the calipers. Two of these five recalls estimate that 100 percent of the vehicles carry the defect. Every single Charger Daytona in 26V262 has the bad cluster software; every single Charger in 26V531 has the bad radio software.[1][6] That is what a software defect looks like when it is filed honestly: the code is in every car, so the population is the defect, and the recall count is the production count. Now compare the other end of the scale. FCA estimates that 0.8 percent of the 74,578 spoiler Durangos actually carry the defect, about 597 trucks, and 1.6 percent of the Hornet population, about 646 of the 40,386 Hornets.[4][5] Same brand, same year, two opposite recall shapes. Recall everyone because the code is in every car, or recall 74,578 trucks to find 597. Both shapes get the same letter, the same free fix, the same tally line. That is why the raw total misleads in both directions at once.

Now the Charger, which went blind twice. April took the instrument cluster: the screen behind the steering wheel, the one that tells the driver what gear the car is in and whether the brakes or the tires need attention, can go dark because of a Valeo software error.[6] August took the rearview camera: the screen in the center stack, the one that shows what is behind the car when the driver shifts into reverse, can stay black because of a Harman Becker radio software error.[1] Different suppliers, different screens, different campaigns, four months apart, same failure mode. A display that is supposed to tell the driver what is happening stops telling them, and the driver finds out by noticing the absence. Dodge’s new Charger has two ways to go blind, and 2026 exercised both of them.

Two corrections to the tally, because a census that does not correct itself is just a press release with arithmetic. First, one of the five “2026 recalls” is not a new defect at all. Campaign 26V528 is the third recall for the same spoiler on the same trucks: 21V842 in October 2021, 23V115 in February 2023, and now 26V528, after spoilers kept detaching on vehicles that had already been through the first two repairs.[5] Add the three campaigns together and one body panel has generated 218,434 recall notices across five years. So the count of genuinely new 2026 campaigns is four, not five, and the year’s biggest number is a five-year-old problem that outlived two fixes. Second, the headline totals you see in press coverage are multi-brand, and the Dodge-only numbers are smaller. Campaign 26V262 covers 20,271 vehicles, but only 8,528 are Dodges; the rest are Jeep Wagoneer S.[6] Campaign 26V510 covers 48,777, but only 40,386 are Hornets; the rest are Alfa Romeo Tonales.[4] Campaign 26V531 covers 844,027, but only 16,838 are Chargers; the rest are Jeeps, Rams and Chryslers.[1] Dodge is 2 percent of that last campaign and 100 percent of the headline that matters to a Charger owner. Both numbers are true, so use the right one for the question you are asking.

Step back and look at the five defects as a set, and the pattern is not a single bad system. It is a brand with three nameplates and no margin for error. Dodge sells the Durango, the Hornet and the Charger, and all three appear in this census. A spoiler that does not fit, a seat belt that twists, a wiring harness with damaged terminals: three physical fit-and-finish failures, the kind of thing a design review is supposed to catch before the first car ships, and two software failures where the code shipped with the defect already in it. Nobody designed a bad car five times. Somebody’s panel gap was wrong, somebody’s routing missed a clearance, somebody’s belt webbing twisted in a retractor that was designed that way, and two different suppliers shipped code that blanks a screen. A regression line fit to five points is a joke, and I know it, but the slope here points at the factory floor and the software bench in equal measure.

Fairness requires the other side of the ledger, stated at full strength. Five recalls is a normal year for a major manufacturer, and the actual danger per vehicle is small: two of the five are software updates with no parts to replace, the spoiler defect affects about 597 of 74,578 trucks, the seat-belt defect about 646 of 40,386 Hornets, and no deaths or injuries are tied to any of the five in the filings. Recalls are the safety system working, not failing. FCA found these defects, filed them, and pays for the repairs, which is precisely what the law asks a manufacturer to do. A reader who walks away thinking Dodge builds dangerous cars has misread the data; the tally is big, the danger per vehicle is small, and every fix is free at the dealer. That said, a spoiler recalled three times for the same defect is not the system working. It is the system issuing the same fix twice, watching it fail, and mailing owners a letter saying the real fix is coming later. Both of those sentences are true at the same time. That is usually the case with the numbers, which is why I keep measuring them.

If you own a 2021 through 2023 Durango, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for 26V528, FCA recall 61D. If your spoiler was already fixed under the 2021 or 2023 recall, you are in this one too, and the warning sign is the spoiler scraping the roof when the liftgate cycles.[5] If you own a 2026 Durango, check for 26V701, FCA A2D, now, and do not wait for the owner letter, which is expected December 3. You cannot see your own tail lights fail from the driver’s seat, so have someone stand behind the car while you hit the brakes, or just run the VIN.[7] If you own a 2024 or 2025 Charger Daytona, the cluster software update under 26V262, FCA 39D, is free and quick, and if your dash has ever gone blank, even once, get it done.[2] If you own a 2026 or 2027 Charger, the rearview camera fix under 26V531, FCA 78D, is a radio software update, and until it is done, treat the camera as advisory and turn your head.[1] If you own a 2023 through 2025 Hornet, check for 26V510, FCA 84D: pull each rear belt fully out and let it retract, and if it twists or sticks, the retractor is the defect.[4] Buying a used Dodge? Confirm every open recall was completed, ask for the service record instead of taking the seller’s word, and remember that the Durango spoiler has already been fixed twice before. The third fix is the one that counts.
