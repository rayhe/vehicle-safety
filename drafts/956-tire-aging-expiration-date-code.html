Existential Dread

Your Tires Expire Like Milk. The Date Is Stamped on the Sidewall in Code.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 20, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute: your tires are expired milk. Somewhere on each sidewall, molded into the rubber in raised letters, sits the week your tire was born: four digits, week then year. If those digits name a week more than six years ago, the tire is past its service life no matter how much tread remains.[3] Most drivers have never heard of this. In a 2008 undercover test, nine out of ten tire technicians at major retailers could not identify an 11-year-old tire's age or even decode the number that carries it.[3] The expiration date exists, and almost nobody can read it.

NHTSA's official death toll for aging tires is 90 people a year, plus more than 3,200 injuries.[2] Memorize that number, because you will never see it updated. Crash data from 2005 and 2006 built it, a 42-page report in March 2014 published it, and no one has refreshed it since. Two decades of newer crashes have arrived, and the number has not moved.

How did the agency settle the question of whether old tires kill people? It baked new ones: it inflated tires with a half-nitrogen, half-oxygen mix, cooked them at 60 to 70 degrees Celsius for six to eight weeks, then spun them to failure on a road wheel. NHTSA said this approximated a tire operated in sunny Phoenix for two to five years.[2] It never tested a field-aged tire older than six years. Then it declined to write an aging standard, citing the robustness of post-2007 tires, mandatory tire-pressure monitoring, and favorable crash trends: case closed, by oven.

A year later, the NTSB read that report and filed a dissent. Its 2015 special investigation concluded that NHTSA's reasons for not pursuing tire-aging regulation "lack support," and it formally recommended the agency determine the actual current crash risk of tire aging.[1] That was eleven years ago. Eleven years later: the recommendation is still open, the number is still 90, and the data is still from 2005.

90
the official annual death toll from aging tires, built on 2005-2006 crash data and never refreshed

Worse, the agency's own arithmetic overshoots its own number. In 2013, 539 people died in tire-related passenger-vehicle crashes, according to the NTSB.[1] NHTSA's own weighting puts aging behind about 23 percent of towaway or police-reported tire crashes.[1] Twenty-three percent of 539 is roughly 124. Apply the government's math to the government's body count and you get a number one-third higher than the government's official estimate. A decade separates the official estimate from the math.

But here is the deeper absurdity: the true toll is unknowable, and that is by design. No crash database in America records a tire's age. FARS will note that a tire failed, but it will not note the tire was manufactured in 2014. Safety Research & Strategies had to build its own survey from litigation records in 2012 precisely because the reporting system never asks the question: 252 incidents of tread or belt separation in tires older than six years, 233 dead, 300 injured.[3] So when the government says 90, understand what that means. It means 90 is the number you get from old data analyzed once. Nobody can say whether the current number is smaller or larger; it is missing.

Individual case files are not missing: in 2014, a 12-year-old tire came apart in a California rollover, its shoulder weather-checked, its belts separating with age.[1] The same year, a 10-year-old tire failed in Louisiana.[1] In suburban Denver, a woman named Marcia Simmons bought "brand new" tires for her Mazda Miata from a local dealer. They were 11 and 12 years old.[2] Asked about it by a TV crew, the dealer said he would never sell a tire he thought was unsafe, that a tire is still new if it was never used, and that if selling aged tires were a problem, there would be a law against it. He was right about the last part, because there is no law. That is the problem.

4324
the DOT week code (week 43 of 2024) identifying the defective batch in NHTSA's February 2026 tire recall, the same four-digit code that tells you when your tires expire

Now do the fleet math, and try not to scream. A record 12.8 years: that is the average age of the American car.[5] Tires are done at six. Averages lie about the dangerous tail: the garage-kept car, the second car, the inherited sedan with 40,000 miles and "plenty of tread." Those are the cars most likely to be riding on expired originals, because their owners inspect tread, not birthdays. Germany's ADAC bench-tested 23 unused spares in 1989 and concluded that even six-year-old tires that look brand new can be unsafe, and that unused tires may age faster than driven ones.[3] Your spare tire, the one you have never looked at, is statistically the most expired tire you own.

Fresh evidence arrived this February, when NHTSA recalled 65 truck tires from a Thai manufacturer after NHTSA's FMVSS 139 compliance testing found tread-shoulder cracking and inner-liner separation, the classic aging failure signature, in tires barely two years old.[4] The defective batch was identified by four digits: 4324, the 43rd week of 2024. That is the same four-digit code molded into your sidewall. One code does two jobs: it tracks the factory's mistakes and tells you when your tires expire, though almost nobody can read it.

We built a civilization that stamps an expiration date on every tire and then made the date a riddle, declined to count the bodies, and called the oven a verdict. Rubber remembers what the paperwork forgot, so go read your sidewalls. Small digits, large meaning: the clock started the week your tires were born, whether you were driving on them or not.

Methodology

Every number in this article comes from four places, and I want you to know whose number is whose. NTSB's Special Investigation Report NTSB/SIR-15/02 (adopted October 27, 2015) supplies the 33,000 annual tire-related crashes, 19,000 injuries, 539 deaths in 2013, the 23 percent aging share (NHTSA's weighting, cited by the NTSB), the Patterson and Centerville case files, and the text of recommendations H-15-32 and H-15-33. NHTSA's "Tire Aging: A Summary of NHTSA's Work" (March 2014), via Tire Review Magazine's summary, supplies the 90 fatalities and 3,200 injuries per year from 2005-2006 data, the oven-aging protocol details, and the 2014 decision not to regulate. Safety Research & Strategies' tire-safety research page supplies the 252 incidents, 233 fatalities, 300 injuries (2012 submission), the 2007 Report-to-Congress figures (77 percent of claims from five hot states, 84 percent of those tires over six years old), the BRMA and automaker guidance history, the ADAC and Kansas City findings, and the Marcia Simmons case. NHTSA's Part 573 recall report 26T008 (submitted February 11, 2026) supplies the 65-tire recall details and the week-4324 identification. S&P Global Mobility via AAPEX (June 2025) reports the 12.8-year average vehicle age. My own arithmetic gives roughly 124 aging-attributable deaths: 23 percent of 539.

Limitations

The 90-per-year and 3,200-per-year figures rest on 2005-2006 data for pre-FMVSS 139 tires; modern tires differ, and the NTSB noted that NHTSA never published its full methodology or controlled for confounders like the recession. SRS's 252 cases are a litigation survey, adverse selection by construction, and cannot yield a population rate. NTSB's 539 deaths are tire-RELATED (all tire failures), not tire-AGING; the roughly 124 figure is an estimate built on an estimate, and I label it as such. I cannot prove any specific crash's counterfactual or say how many of the 90 would have lived with fresh tires. Warm-climate skew is severe: a disproportionate share of aging failures comes from Sun Belt states, so an 8-year-old tire in Minnesota is a different risk than an 8-year-old tire in Phoenix, and this article should not be read as universalizing the hot-state number.

A Necessary Counterargument

Now the honest pushback, because cosmic dread needs an editor. That 90-a-year estimate is pre-FMVSS 139, the 2007 TREAD Act standards that made tires genuinely more robust; NHTSA's oven tests and the NTSB both found post-139 tires performed better, and mandatory tire-pressure monitoring since 2007 cut the severe-underinflation co-factor. Today's toll is probably lower than 90. A frozen estimate cuts both ways: it may be overstating today's risk, not understating it. Correlation is not causation, either: old tires travel with old cars, deferred maintenance, chronic underinflation, overloading, and hot climates, and NHTSA's own weighting puts aging behind about 23 percent of tire crashes, which means roughly 77 percent were not aging. Age is one ingredient in a compound failure. A federal age-based removal rule would be nearly unenforceable at the consumer level and regressive in practice, landing hardest on used-tire buyers and low-income drivers, while plenty of 8-year-old tires in cool climates are fine; the industry's 10-year backstop is a compromise, not science. And defect is not age: recall 26T008 shows manufacturing defects produce the exact same failure signature, tread-shoulder cracking and liner separation, in two-year-old tires. An aging frame can let manufacturers off the hook for building tires that fail young. Firestone's Wilderness ATX catastrophe was a defect story wearing an age costume.

Fair enough. But none of that explains why the official count is still built on 2005 data, why the NTSB's 2015 recommendation to measure the current risk remains unfulfilled, or why the date code that could let every driver check remains a riddle most technicians cannot solve. You do not need a federal rule to read four digits. Information is molded into the rubber, but the literacy is what expired.

What You Can Do

Go outside and read your tires. Find the TIN molded into the sidewall; its last four digits are the date code, WWYY, week and year of manufacture (4324 means the 43rd week of 2024). It is stamped on only one sidewall, so check both sides of every tire, including the spare. If any tire is over six years old, replace it regardless of tread; that is Ford's, Bridgestone's, and most automakers' guidance. Ten years from manufacture is the absolute ceiling per the British Rubber Manufacturers Association, not a target. Never buy "new" old tires: check the date code before money changes hands, because retailers have sold 11- and 12-year-old tires as new. If you live in the Sun Belt, park in shade when you can, avoid sustained high speeds on older tires, and check inflation monthly, because heat and underinflation accelerate aging. And check the spare first, because it is the tire you never look at, which is exactly why it is the most likely to be expired.
