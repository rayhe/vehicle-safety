Sobriety Report

The Honda Element Is a Top Safety Pick. Its Drivers Keep Failing Drug Tests.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
October 9, 2026

According to the toxicology reports — and there are a lot of them — the Honda Element has one of the five highest drug-positive rates in America. Of the 449 Element drivers tested after fatal crashes in our FARS data, 11.6% came back positive for drugs.[1] This is not a Corvette. It is a toaster on wheels, a vehicle Honda marketed to campers and dog owners, and its drivers keep showing up to fatal crashes chemically enhanced.

Share of tested Element drivers impaired by alcohol or drugs, vs. a 19.6% median for SUVs

Now the twist. The Element is, by every engineering measure, a genuinely safe car. Our FARS data puts it at 0.55 deaths per 100 million miles, a low rate for anything with four wheels and a roof.[1] IIHS flunked the early models in the side-impact test, but once Honda made side curtain airbags and stability control standard in 2007, the side rating jumped to Good, and the 2009 Element took home a Top Safety Pick.[2][3] NHTSA handed out five stars in frontal crash tests.[4] The car passed every test, and its drivers keep failing theirs.

Look at the company the Element keeps on the drug leaderboard, because the pattern is doing all the talking. Above it sit the Buick Verano (13.1%), the Dodge Neon (11.8%), and the Cadillac DTS (11.8%). Below it: the Dodge Avenger and the GMC Savana work van. Read that list again, because it is not a list of dangerous cars, it is a used-car price list. The drug leaderboard is dominated by cheap, durable, easy-to-buy transportation, and the Element earned its seat at the table by being the most lovable box on the lot. Nobody set out to build a drug car. They built an affordable one, and impairment followed the affordability.

The strongest case against this story is also the most obvious one: it is the buyers, not the box. Cheap used Elements attract young, high-risk drivers, and any vehicle popular with that demographic would show the same toxicology profile. The car did not cause the impairment; it became the ride of choice for impaired people, the same way the Pontiac Vibe did. I will even grant the point, because it changes nothing. A vehicle's real-world risk is the sum of its engineering and its owners, and when you shop a used Element you are buying into a fleet where nearly one in four fatal-crash drivers was impaired. The crash-test trophy does not commute with you; the demographic does.

So here is the buyer's guide nobody asked for. Used Elements now trade at collector premiums, which means people are paying real money to join this statistical club. Buy a 2007 or newer: standard stability control, Good side-impact rating, Top Safety Pick in 2009. Skip the 2003-2006 cars unless you enjoy the particular thrill of a Poor side-impact score and a 3-star rollover rating on something shaped like a vending machine. Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands, because a twenty-year-old airbag is a rumor, not a restraint system.[5] And if you are the kind of Element owner the toxicology data keeps finding, the safest feature on the car is the one between the seat and the steering wheel, so keep it sober.

What this analysis did not prove: FARS captures fatal crashes only, so a vehicle with a low fatality rate can still maim at an above-average clip. Toxicology testing is incomplete and varies by state, with 449 tested drivers against 212 crashes, so the true impairment share could run higher. That fatality rate uses estimated vehicle miles, not odometer readings, which adds roughly 15% uncertainty for a low-volume model like the Element. And the model-year death codings include 2016 and 2017 entries for a car discontinued in 2011, which is either a paperwork ghost or a reminder that FARS data entry is performed by humans.
