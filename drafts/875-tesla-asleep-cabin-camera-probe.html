Tesla's Cabin Camera Is Supposed to Catch You Sleeping. The Videos Say It Can't.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 10, 2026

Somebody fell asleep in a moving Tesla this week, and that is not even the interesting part. The interesting part is that the car was supposed to notice. Tesla's own description of Full Self-Driving (Supervised) says the cabin camera monitors driver attentiveness while the system is engaged.[1] The videos circulating this week suggest the attentiveness in question can include full unconsciousness. Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi (D-Ill.) is now urging NHTSA to open an investigation, arguing the agency has not determined whether Tesla fixed the driver-disengagement lessons regulators already identified.[1]

IIHS safeguard categories where Tesla's FSD rated "poor" in 2024, the worst of 14 systems tested

Here is the uncomfortable bit of history nobody at Tesla wants attached to this news cycle. In March 2024, the IIHS rated 14 partial-automation systems on their safeguards, meaning their ability to keep drivers engaged and prevent misuse.[2] Eleven scored poor. One earned acceptable. Tesla's FSD (Beta) scored worst of all: poor in six of eight categories.[2] IIHS researchers tested the driver monitoring with a cheesecloth over the driver's face and weights on the steering wheel, and too many systems passed anyway.[3] "Many can be used despite occupants being unbelted or when other vital safety features are switched off," said IIHS senior research scientist Alexandra Mueller.[3] That was two years and many software updates ago. The videos suggest the core weakness survived the updates.

Do not confuse this with the other open Tesla probe, though the file drawer is getting crowded. NHTSA opened its FSD investigation in October 2024 after four reports of Teslas crashing in reduced visibility, sun glare, fog, airborne dust.[1] One of those crashes killed a pedestrian; another injured someone. The agency escalated to a full engineering analysis in March 2026, and it is still open.[1] Krishnamoorthi's push is narrower: given that NHTSA has, in his words, "already identified the dangers that can arise when a highly capable driver-assistance system is paired with inadequate safeguards against driver disengagement," the asleep-at-the-wheel videos warrant a fresh look at the monitoring itself.[1] Six Tesla-related probes are open at NHTSA right now. Six.[1]

Sleep is the original impairment, and my desk treats it that way. A drowsy driver with both hands off the wheel is not supervising anything, no matter what the product page calls the mode. So here is the checklist. Treat the cabin camera like a smoke detector with a dead battery: hands on the wheel, eyes on the road, especially when FSD is engaged. If you are shopping partial-automation systems, check the IIHS safeguard ratings before the marketing page; eleven of fourteen systems rated poor is not a lineup, it is a warning label.[2] And if you see this kind of misuse on the road, report it at nhtsa.gov, because the October 2024 probe started with four crash reports from regular people.[4]

What this article did not prove. The IIHS safeguard ratings date to March 2024, and Tesla has shipped many FSD updates since; the ratings describe the system tested, not the one in your driveway today. Viral videos are anecdotes, not a defect finding, and asleep drivers are misusing the system as labeled. Krishnamoorthi's letter urges an investigation; no new probe has been opened. NHTSA's FSD engineering analysis concerns crashes in reduced roadway visibility, not sleeping drivers specifically, though disengagement safeguards are within its scope. Stated plainly.
