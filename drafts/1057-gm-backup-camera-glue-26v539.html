GM Closed Its Backup-Camera Investigation. 33,365 Complaints Later, It's Recalling 306,511 SUVs.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026

Shift into reverse, check the screen, and trust what it shows, because federal law has required a working rear camera on every new car since 2018, after too many people died in backover crashes. For 306,511 GM SUVs, that screen can lie: the image distorts or goes completely blank while you back up, and the failure is baked into the assembly line itself.

33,365
Owner complaints tied to the suspect build window, filed between July 2024 and May 2026

Per GM's own chronology filed with NHTSA, the company opened an investigation into inoperative rearview cameras on May 20, 2025, then closed it on September 4, 2025, having found "no defect trend," and returned to monitoring field data twice a year, even as the cameras kept dying. On March 25, 2026, with claim rates climbing anyway, GM opened a new investigation and found the culprit: a plasma-cleaning treatment meant to prepare the camera housing for bonding was weakening the adhesive bond in some assemblies. A process meant to make the camera cleaner made it leak. Moisture and grime seep in over time until the image warps or dies.

Almost poetic, the root cause, except the poetry lands hardest on the cheapest trims. Only standard rearview cameras are affected; buyers who paid for Surround Vision on the premium trims have cameras from a different, unaffected supply chain. This defect lives on the cheaper end of GM's four best-selling small SUVs: the 2025 Chevrolet Trax (159,821 units), the 2024-2026 Chevrolet Trailblazer (74,515), the 2024-2025 Buick Envista (41,323), and the 2024-2026 Buick Encore GX (30,852), all built between April and December 2024. That window ends exactly when the supplier modified its plasma-cleaning fixture, which is the kind of coincidence that writes its own punchline. GM estimates 10 percent of the recalled population carries the defect, roughly 30,651 vehicles driving around with cameras on borrowed time.[1][2][3]

GM has a counterargument, and fairness requires airing it at full strength: the company knows of no accidents or injuries tied to this condition, and a dead backup camera is not a detaching steering wheel, because the mirrors still exist and most drivers survived decades of reversing without a screen. Ninety percent of recalled vehicles are probably fine, which means most owners will burn a dealer visit to be told their camera passes inspection, since the remedy is inspect-and-replace as necessary rather than a blanket replacement, with owner letters going out in phases.

That defense has a time-shaped hole. Moisture intrusion gets worse, not better, so a camera that passes inspection this fall can fail next spring, which makes "as necessary" a snapshot of a slow leak. If you own one of these vehicles, the inspection is not a one-time all-clear. It is a status check on a seal that was weakened at birth.

What to do. Search your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls right now, because GM's campaign numbers 26V539 and A262554900 have been VIN-searchable since August 20, 2026, and the dealer visit costs you nothing. Until a dealer confirms a good camera, treat reverse like it is 2017 again: mirrors, turn your head, and never trust a blank screen to mean a clear driveway. Shopping used in this segment? These are among the most common compact SUVs on lots today, and an inspect-and-replace remedy means a recall can be "completed" without a new camera going in, so ask for the service record, not just the recall-closed stamp.[4]
