By The Numbers

A Cracked Capacitor Defeated the Most Effective Safety Mandate in American Cars

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 8, 2026

I ran the numbers, ran them again, and they didn't get better. Last week Chrysler told NHTSA that certain 2025-2026 Voyager and Pacifica minivans, 2,017 of them including the Voyager PHEV, may carry a cracked capacitor inside the brake pedal position sensor. When that capacitor fails, the car can lose electronic stability control entirely, which puts every one of those minivans in noncompliance with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 126.[1] With no warning light and no telltale, the mandate's crown jewel, silently switched off by a component that costs less than the envelope the recall letter will arrive in.

55%
Reduction in fatal single-vehicle crashes attributed to ESC in passenger cars (NHTSA, Sivinski DOT HS 811 486)

Some context on what just got switched off. FMVSS 126 became final on April 6, 2007, and every light vehicle built after September 1, 2011 carries compliant ESC by law.[2] When the rule was proposed, NHTSA estimated the technology would save 5,300 to 10,300 lives and prevent 168,000 to 252,000 injuries per year at full fleet penetration.[3] That estimate held up. A 2011 NHTSA study found ESC cuts fatal single-vehicle crashes by 55% for cars and 50% for light trucks and vans.[4] Seat belts are the only mandated technology with a bigger body count prevented. This is not a backup camera; it is the single most effective crash-avoidance system ever legislated.

Which makes the census I ran genuinely unsettling. I pulled every FMVSS-126 noncompliance recall I could find in NHTSA's records. Four campaigns:

22E-017 (2022): Chrysler/Mopar ABS control modules whose software kept the ESC telltale lamp from illuminating.[5] 24E-047 (2024): ABS modules with a software malfunction that could disable ESC outright.[6] 24V-838 (2024): FCA hydraulic control units leaving ABS and ESC inoperative, with brake lights stuck on for extra menace.[7] And now 26V561: a cracked ceramic capacitor.

The pattern: zero of the four involve a car that shipped without ESC hardware. Four of four are electronics ghosts: software bugs, a telltale that wouldn't light, and now a passive component the size of a grain of rice. The 2007 mandate made stability control universal, but it did not make the electronics around it robust. We legislated the brain and left the nervous system to the lowest bidder's ceramic supply chain.

One more number worth sitting with: the recall report reached NHTSA on January 9, 2026. Owner notification letters are expected to be mailed September 29. That is roughly 8.7 months between the government learning your minivan might be noncompliant and you learning it.[1] VINs become searchable September 9, which at least lets the impatient check for themselves. The remedy is an inspection and sensor replacement, free, no over-the-air fix available. Two thousand seventeen minivans driving around for most of 2026 with stability control that might be a fiction.

If you drive a 2025 or 2026 Voyager or Pacifica, or the 2026 Voyager PHEV, plug your VIN into nhtsa.gov/recalls starting September 9, and don't wait for the letter. When you visit the dealer, ask them to confirm the replacement sensor is the revised part, not the same ceramic lottery. An ESC warning light is the only honest signal in this story, because a cracked capacitor produces none; if the telltale never lights at startup, that's its own warning.

Now for the honest accounting: I could not locate the Part 573 chronology for 26V561, so I have no defect rate, no discovery date, and no field-incident count; I am not claiming 2,017 cracked capacitors, only 2,017 recalled vehicles. My four-campaign census is everything I could find through NHTSA's API and published 573 reports, not an exhaustive flat-file census, and I say so here. No injuries or deaths are tied to this recall. The harm is probabilistic: the possible loss of a system that prevents more than half of fatal single-vehicle crashes.

The strongest case against this story writes itself. Two thousand seventeen minivans is a rounding error against 280 million registered vehicles, and the recall system worked exactly as designed: Chrysler found it, filed it, and the fix costs owners nothing. A cracked capacitor defeating ESC on paper does not mean ESC failed in the field; by every measure, the 2007 mandate is one of the great regulatory triumphs in transportation history. All true. But the counterargument's limit is the census itself. Four of four recent FMVSS-126 recalls share the same shape, electronics ghosts, not missing hardware, and that shape is structural to how modern ESC is built. Any single campaign is small, but the pattern is the story.
