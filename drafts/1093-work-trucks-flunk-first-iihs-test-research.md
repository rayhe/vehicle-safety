# Research Notes — Article #1093: Work Trucks Flunk First IIHS Test

**Working title:** "IIHS Finally Crash-Tested the Work Truck. It Flunked."
**Journalist:** Mia Crumplezone (Safety Engineering Editor — design analysis, safety tech beat)
**Slug:** 1093-work-trucks-flunk-first-iihs-test

## Kill test verdict: PROCEED
- Newsworthy? Yes. IIHS published its first-ever crash-avoidance ratings for commercial work vehicles this week (Detroit Free Press coverage, Oct 2026). First-of-its-kind testing series, begun June 2026, 11 vehicles at Ruckersville, VA.
- Novel angle? Yes. Three layers: (1) the two vans that failed BOTH vehicle-to-vehicle and pedestrian tests are built on a 30-year-old platform GM still sells new; (2) the mandate-exemption math: FMVSS 127 promises ~360 lives/yr saved for light vehicles while the work-vehicle category it doesn't touch kills 6,215/yr (~17x); (3) child-size dummies "disappear" near tall truck front ends even in daylight.
- Overlap check (queue grep 2026-10-07): no article covers IIHS work-truck/van crash-avoidance testing. #862/#867 are motorcycle impairment stories (different thesis), #883 is delivery-van drug prevalence (different thesis). No overlap.

## Verified facts (all sourced, no fabrication)

1. **First-of-its-kind test.** IIHS began work-vehicle assessments June 2026: cargo vans and Class 3 pickups (GVWR 10,001–14,000 lbs). Parameters mirror retail-vehicle checks: standard front/side airbags, advanced seat belt tech, seat belt reminders, then crash-avoidance tests. 11 vehicles tested. (Source: freep.com, Oct 2026; IIHS strategic-plan announcement Mar 2026 via thedrive.com, jalopnik.com, automotiveworld.com)
2. **Death toll.** Crashes involving heavy/medium-duty vehicles or light vans killed 6,215 people in 2024 — 16% of all roadway fatalities per NHTSA FARS. (Source: freep.com citing IIHS/NHTSA FARS). Sanity check: NHTSA 2024 estimate = 39,345 deaths (aashtojournal/Reuters, Apr 2025). 6,215/39,345 = 15.79% ≈ 16%. Consistent. 2023 figure: 6,535 (16%).
3. **Poor in vehicle-to-vehicle:** 2025–2027 Chevrolet Express 2500, 2025 Ford Transit T250, 2025–2027 GMC Savana 2500, 2025–2027 Ram ProMaster 2500, 2025–2026 Chevrolet Silverado 3500 HD, 2025 Ford F-350, 2025–2026 GMC Sierra 3500. (Source: freep.com)
4. **No AEB at all:** Express 2500 and Savana 2500 earned Poor in pedestrian avoidance too because they ship WITHOUT automatic emergency braking — optional forward-collision warning only. (Source: freep.com, attributed to IIHS)
5. **Motorcycle worst case:** motorcycle target presented the biggest challenge; several systems "did not issue warnings or slow at all" in motorcycle scenarios; others braked but warned too late and lost points. (Source: freep.com)
6. **Children disappear:** F-350 and Ram 3500, even rated Acceptable in pedestrian tests, struggled with child-size dummies in daylight vs adult dummies at night. At close range the child dummy "disappears" from driver sight lines and from sensors mounted near the rearview mirror due to tall front ends. (Source: freep.com)
7. **Bright spots:** Rivian RCV-Delivery 500 (Amazon van platform) — Marginal vehicle-to-vehicle, Good pedestrian. Mercedes Sprinter 2500 — Good vehicle-to-vehicle. Discontinued Chevy BrightDrop 400 EV — Good pedestrian. (Source: freep.com)
8. **The 30-year platform:** Express/Savana launched as 1996 model year; facelift 2003; "remained largely unchanged throughout their production life" (gmauthority.com, Dec 2024). 2026 − 1996 = 30 years. IIHS tested 2025–2027 model years — same basic platform. ~40,000 units sold 2024 (gmauthority.com). (Sources: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevrolet_Express, gmauthority.com)
9. **Regulatory gap (PRIMARY — NHTSA):** FMVSS 127 (final rule Apr 2024, NHTSA-2023-0021) requires AEB + pedestrian AEB on light vehicles ≤10,000 lbs GVWR by Sept 1, 2029. Class 3 pickups (10,001–14,000) are outside the mandate. IIHS VP David Kidd: "you have to have [AEB] that detects vehicles as well as pedestrians, but that's not coming for commercial vehicles." NHTSA expects the rule to save ≥360 lives and prevent ≥24,000 injuries per year. (Sources: FMVSS 127 final rule text via safetywissenapp.de mirror; aashtojournal.transportation.org; hlc.com)
10. **Original calculation:** 6,215 (work-vehicle deaths/yr) ÷ 360 (FMVSS 127 projected lives saved/yr) ≈ 17.3x. The mandate's headline benefit is roughly one-seventeenth of the annual toll in the category it doesn't cover. AEB cuts rear-end crash rates ~50% (Kidd, freep.com).

## Story spine (Mia Crumplezone voice)
- Lede: the first 150 milliseconds of a crash in a vehicle engineered in the Clinton administration.
- The 30-year-old van as protagonist: Express/Savana double-Poor, no AEB, platform old enough to vote (and rent a car).
- The regulatory cliff: 10,000 lbs GVWR is a regulatory bright line; above it, Kidd says the AEB requirement "is not coming."
- The disappearing child + invisible motorcycle: engineering details that make the failure concrete.
- Actionable: fleets and small businesses buy these vans new; IIHS ratings now exist for the first time; buyers can check ratings before ordering; the mandate won't save them until 2029 and only below 10k lbs.

## Limitations (for the limitations section)
- FARS 2024 figure (6,215) comes via IIHS citing FARS; final 2024 FARS data release status not independently confirmed in this research — treat as IIHS-reported.
- IIHS tested 11 vehicles; results "varied widely" — this is a first-round sample, not a fleet census. Class 4–6 trucks not yet tested.
- Express 2500 GVWR (~8,600 lbs) falls UNDER the 10,000-lb FMVSS 127 threshold, so it WILL require AEB by Sept 2029; GM's current offering predates compliance. Class 3 HD pickups (10,001–14,000) genuinely sit outside the mandate.
- freep.com is secondary reporting; the IIHS news release itself was not directly retrieved (link to iihs.org topics pages instead of guessing a release URL).
- 30-year claim refers to the platform generation (1996 MY launch, 2003 facelift), not literally zero changes — powertrains and some safety equipment evolved (e.g., dual airbags standardized 1997).

## Strongest counterargument
Fleet operators keep buying Express/Savana because they're cheap, simple, and repairable (~40k units in 2024 with minimal incentives), and the vehicles' crash-involvement toll partly reflects exposure: work vehicles are driven far more miles per year than passenger cars. A per-mile analysis might narrow the gap. Also, adding AEB to cab-chassis work trucks is genuinely harder (upfitters mount arbitrary bodies that sensors must see past), which is a real engineering constraint, not just manufacturer laziness.
