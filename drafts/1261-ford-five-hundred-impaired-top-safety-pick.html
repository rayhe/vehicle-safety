Ford's Forgotten Sedan Out-Drank the Corvette and Still Won Top Safety Pick

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
December 25, 2027

According to the toxicology reports, and there are a lot of them, the most impaired driver pool in the big-sedan class did not belong to a sports car or a luxury flagship. It belonged to a car most buyers have already forgotten. In FARS toxicology data covering 2014 through 2023, 26.4% of Ford Five Hundred drivers in fatal crashes tested positive for alcohol or drugs, 57 of 216 tested.[1] That ranks 5th of 290 models with 150 or more tested drivers, and it edges the Chevrolet Corvette, which sits at 26.2%. A Volvo-boned family sedan, out-drinking America's sports car.

26.4%
Share of Five Hundred drivers in fatal crashes testing positive for alcohol or drugs (57 of 216)

Split the number and the drug side is even more striking. Five Hundred drivers tested drug-positive at 13.0%, the 3rd-highest rate of all 290 models, behind only the Buick Park Avenue (16.6%) and the Buick Verano (13.1%).[1] Alcohol-positive came in at 19.9%. Compare that with the rest of the big-sedan class and the gap opens up: Crown Victoria 22.3%, Buick Lucerne 22.0%, Toyota Avalon 21.7%, Chevrolet Impala 21.4%, Chrysler 300 20.5%. Every one of them sits below the Five Hundred, and every one of them sits above the fleet-wide average of 20.0%. Only the Five Hundred clears the Corvette.

Honesty requires a footnote on that Corvette edge. It is 0.2 percentage points, built on 216 Five Hundred drivers against 1,147 Corvette drivers, and a handful of cases either way would flip it. Call it essentially tied with the Corvette, not a rout. What is not within noise is the class gap: 4.1 points over the next big sedan, 6.4 points over the fleet average, on a sample of more than 200 drivers. Nobody else in the sensible-shoes segment is close.

0.57
Five Hundred death rate per 100M VMT (estimated), near the dataset median of 0.54, despite the impairment rank

Now the award history, because it makes the paradox stranger. Ford built the Five Hundred on Volvo's P2-derived platform, with SPACE side-protection architecture adapted from Volvo's Side Impact Protection System and a hydroformed beam between the B-pillars.[5] With optional side airbags fitted, it earned Good ratings in IIHS front, side, and rear tests and took Top Safety Pick Gold for 2006, plus five stars from NHTSA for front- and side-impact performance.[3] Tested without those optional airbags in June 2006, the same car rated Poor for side impact, and Ford made side airbags standard on cars built from September 2006.[3] Then IIHS tightened the award criteria for 2007 to require electronic stability control, which the Five Hundred did not offer at any price, so it lost the designation without failing a single crash test.[4] Ford renamed the car Taurus for 2008, added ESC as an option and standard side airbags, and the renamed car regained Top Safety Pick.[4]

Put the two halves together and something odd falls out. A car whose drivers tested impaired at sports-car rates still posted a death rate of 0.57 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled (estimated), essentially at the dataset median of 0.54.[1] Impairment and lethality decouple here. One reading is generous: Volvo engineering absorbed a driver pool that should have made this car a killer. Another reading is darker: Five Hundred fatal crashes may skew toward late-night, single-car crashes where the driver dies whatever the structure does, and a low fleet death rate mostly reflects how few of these cars are left on the road. FARS cannot settle that argument. It can only say the trophy winner and the impairment outlier are the same car, and that should bother anyone who thinks safety ratings describe the driver.

Three caveats belong in any honest version of this story. Alcohol-positive in FARS toxicology means any detected alcohol, BAC above zero, not the 0.08 legal threshold behind NHTSA's headline impaired-driving counts, so these rates run hotter than the official fatality share.[2] Testing is incomplete and varies by state, so a state that tests aggressively and happens to hold many surviving Five Hundreds could inflate the rate without any behavioral difference. And the data covers fatal crashes only: 26.4% describes drivers in fatal crashes with test results, not Five Hundred drivers in general, and it cannot say why, whether age, used-car price, or late-night rural miles did the work.

What to actually do with this

Shopping a cheap big sedan like this one? Check the build date before you trust the trophy: Five Hundreds built before September 2006 may lack the side airbags the Top Safety Pick depended on, so verify the equipment on the actual car rather than the award year. And for every driver, the countermeasure with real evidence behind it is not starting the car impaired at all: IIHS estimates that blocking BAC 0.08 starts would have averted roughly 9,400 deaths a year.[6] If you drive a car this old, the safety feature that matters most is the decision made before the key turns.
