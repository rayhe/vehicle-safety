# Research — #1450: The Drunk Driver Killed the Kid in His Own Back Seat

**Slug:** `1450-117-kids-died-riding-with-drunk-driver`

**Kicker:** Sobriety Report
**Date researched:** November 9, 2026

## Angle
In 2024, 221 children aged 14 and younger died in alcohol-impaired-driving crashes. More than half of them, 117, were not hit by a drunk driver. They were riding with one. They were passengers in the drunk driver's own car. The adult who was supposed to get them home safely was the impaired driver, and the child had no say in the matter, no license to revoke, no keys to take. The national conversation about drunk driving is about the driver and the stranger in the other lane. The data says the most defenseless victim is in the back seat.

## Verified facts (3+ primary sources)

1. **NHTSA, Alcohol-Impaired Driving: 2024 Data, DOT HS 813 815 (June 2026)** — primary source, read in full November 9, 2026 via crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov:
   - 11,904 fatalities in alcohol-impaired-driving crashes in 2024, 30% of all traffic fatalities; down 3.9% from 12,382 in 2023. One alcohol-impaired-driving fatality every 44 minutes.
   - Role breakdown of the 11,904: 7,247 alcohol-impaired drivers (61%); 1,483 passengers riding with alcohol-impaired drivers (12%); 1,858 occupants of other vehicles (16%); 1,316 nonoccupants (11%).
   - Children section (p. 7): of 1,032 children 14 and younger killed in 2024, an estimated 221 (21%) died in alcohol-impaired-driving crashes. Of those 221: **117 (53%) were passengers of vehicles with alcohol-impaired drivers**; 74 (33%) were occupants of other vehicles; 28 (13%) were nonoccupants; 2 (1%) were child drivers.
   - 68% (8,097) of alcohol-impaired fatalities were in crashes where at least one driver had BAC of .15 g/dL or higher.
   - Night vs day: alcohol impairment among drivers in fatal crashes was 31% at night vs 10% during the day. Weekend 28% vs weekday 15%.
   - 21- to 24-year-olds had the highest percentage of alcohol-impaired drivers in fatal crashes (28%), followed by 25- to 34-year-olds (27%).
   - States: alcohol-impaired fatalities highest in Texas (1,676), California (1,311), Florida (769). Share of state fatalities ranged from 41% (Vermont) to 19% (Alaska).
2. **NHTSA, Children: 2024 Data, DOT HS 813 811 (June 2026)** — primary source, via crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov:
   - 1,032 children 14 and younger killed in 2024, 3% of the 39,254 total traffic fatalities. Up 1% from 1,023 in 2023 while total traffic deaths fell 4.3%.
   - An estimated 160,850 children injured; an average of 3 children killed and 439 injured every day.
   - 76% of killed children were occupants, 24% nonoccupants. 17% of children killed were pedestrians.
   - Child safety seats reduce fatal injury by 71% for infants under 1 and 54% for toddlers ages 1-4 in passenger cars.
3. **NHTSA, Overview of Motor Vehicle Traffic Crashes, 2024 (DOT HS 813 791)** — 39,254 total traffic deaths in 2024, down 4.3% from 41,025 in 2023; fatality rate 1.19 per 100M VMT.
4. **NHTSA FARS database** — all counts are FARS census counts (2024 Annual Report File), with BAC imputed where test results were unknown. https://www.nhtsa.gov/research-data/fatality-analysis-reporting-system-fars

## Original contribution: original math (show work)

All calculations from the two fact sheets above:

- **Cadence:** 117 child passengers / 365 days = 0.32 per day, i.e. **one child every 3.1 days** died riding with a drunk driver in 2024. 221 children in alcohol crashes / 365 = one every 1.65 days.
- **Overrepresentation:** children are 1,032 / 39,254 = **2.6%** of all traffic deaths, but 117 / 1,483 = **7.9%** of all passengers who died riding with an impaired driver. Children are overrepresented among dead passengers of drunk drivers by **7.9 / 2.6 = 3.0x**. A child in a drunk driver's car dies at three times the rate their share of traffic deaths would predict, because children cannot choose their driver.
- **The back-seat majority:** 117 of 221 = **53%** of children killed in alcohol crashes were passengers of the impaired driver. For all ages, passengers of impaired drivers are only 12% of alcohol deaths. For children, the passenger share is 4.4x higher (53% vs 12%).
- **The innocent share:** only 2 of the 221 child deaths (1%) were child drivers. 219 of 221 children (99%) were killed by someone else's drinking.
- **The .15 factor:** 68% of all alcohol-impaired fatalities involved a driver at BAC .15 or higher, nearly twice the legal limit. These are not "one glass of wine" crashes.
- **Divergence:** total traffic deaths fell 4.3% in 2024 and alcohol-impaired deaths fell 3.9%, but child traffic deaths ROSE 1% (1,023 to 1,032). Children went the wrong way while the country improved.

## Limitations
- BAC values are imputed by NHTSA when test results are unknown, so the 221 figure is an estimate, not a counted census. The fact sheet labels it "estimated."
- "Alcohol-impaired-driving crash" means an impaired driver was involved, not that alcohol caused the crash. NHTSA states this explicitly.
- The 117-passenger figure does not say the impaired driver was a parent or guardian. FARS does not record the relationship between driver and child passenger. "The adult who was supposed to get them home" is a fair characterization of the role, not a verified relationship in each case.
- 2024 figures come from the Annual Report File, which NHTSA revises when finalized the following year; small counts may move.
- FARS counts deaths within 30 days of the crash; children who died later are not counted.
- The overrepresentation ratio compares two different denominators (share of all deaths vs share of passenger deaths). It is a descriptive ratio, not a causal risk estimate, because exposure (how often children ride with drinking drivers) is unknown.

## Strongest counterargument
The 117 number is small next to 11,904 alcohol deaths and next to 1,032 child deaths, and it fell inside a year when alcohol deaths overall declined 3.9%. A skeptic can fairly say this is a rounding error in a national improvement story, and that the drunk driver who kills his own passenger is already the most punished, most stigmatized driver on the road. There is no policy lever aimed specifically at "drunk drivers with children in the car" that is not already covered by DUI law, child endangerment statutes (which exist in most states), and ignition interlocks. The piece cannot claim a new law would fix this; it can only claim the public's mental image of the drunk-driving victim is wrong. That is a framing claim, not a policy claim, and it should be stated as one.

## Actionable insights
- If you are hosting and someone has been drinking: do not let them drive home with kids in the car. Take the keys, call a ride, offer a couch. The child in the back seat cannot veto the driver.
- If you are a passenger and the driver has been drinking: get out, take the kids with you. NHTSA's own reminder: if someone you know has been drinking, don't let them drive.
- If you see a car weaving at night with kids visible: call 911. Alcohol impairment among drivers in fatal crashes is 31% at night vs 10% during the day, and 28% on weekends vs 15% on weekdays.
- Check your state: most states have child endangerment enhancements for DUI with a child passenger. Know whether yours does before you need it.
- Parents: a car seat reduces fatal injury by 71% for infants and 54% for toddlers, but no restraint undoes a drunk driver. The restraint numbers are in the fact sheet; the driver is the variable.
