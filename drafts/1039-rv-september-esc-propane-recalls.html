September Brought 13 RV Recalls. One Shipped Motorhomes With No Working Stability Control.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 1, 2026

In September 2026, NHTSA logged 13 RV safety recalls covering 1,288 motorhomes, trailers, and camper vans.[1] Read them in sequence and they stop reading like automotive recalls and start reading like a home inspection by an inspector losing the will to live. A rooftop fan board that can catch fire. A furnace venting carbon monoxide into the bedroom. A sofa blocking the emergency exit. A motorhome rated to go faster than its tires are rated to survive. And one coach builder that shipped flagship motorhomes with the stability control programmed wrong.

47
REV motorhomes (2025–2027 American Coach, Fleetwood, Holiday Rambler) whose RoadWatch safety system was programmed with the wrong vehicle parameters, leaving electronic stability control diminished or lost entirely.

Start with the flagship, because it is genuinely astonishing. REV Recreation Group recalled 47 of its flagship coaches after discovering the RoadWatch Safety System, which manages electronic stability control among other functions, had been programmed with incorrect vehicle parameters.[2] The software that decides when your 30,000-pound coach starts to tip over was calibrated for some other vehicle, and ESC functions may be diminished or lost, with remedy letters going out November 16.[2] Here is the part that makes it structural rather than anecdotal: Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 126 requires ESC only on vehicles at or under 10,000 pounds GVWR.[7] Your Honda Civic cannot be sold without it. A Class A motorhome three times heavier and shaped like a sail carries ESC only when the builder volunteers. REV volunteered, then entered the wrong numbers.

From there, September’s docket descends into the domestic. Tiffin recalled 88 Wayfarer motorhomes whose liquid propane tanks may not be secured in their brackets and were built without a remote shutoff.[3] Transport Canada, which runs the same recall, puts the sequence plainly: a loose tank detaches, becomes a road hazard, and if there is a leak you cannot shut off the gas.[4] Forest River recalled five 2027 Vibe travel trailers because the furnace exhaust may not be attached to the outside flue, which is the regulatory way of saying the heater can fill your sleeping quarters with carbon monoxide — five units, five bedrooms.[5] And two separate recalls, Storyteller’s 26V566 and Kibbi’s 26V574, cite the same failure in the same supplier component: the MaxxAir N-Series Maxxfan rooftop ventilation fan, whose printed circuit board can overheat and start a fire. One fan maker, two coach builders, two NHTSA campaign numbers, nobody connecting the dots on the paperwork.[1] Meanwhile Grand Design recalled its Lineage motorhomes because, with the optional 43 tire package, the vehicle’s top speed may exceed the tires’ speed rating.[1] Read that twice.

So here is what to do with all of this. If you own a 2025–2027 American Coach American Dream, Fleetwood Discovery LXE or Palisade, or Holiday Rambler Armada: do not wait for the November 16 letter. Call REV at 800-509-3417 and get the free RoadWatch reprogram scheduled now.[2] Tiffin Wayfarer owners (2026–2027): the dealer installs the remote propane shutoff and a new bracket free; call 256-356-8661.[4] Shopping used RVs? The three label recalls in this batch (Winnebago’s wrong tire pressures on the federal placard, Jayco’s wrong tire sizes and axle ratings) mean the sticker on the door may be lying. Verify pressures against the tire sidewall and run every VIN through nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands.

In fairness, and the fairness here is real: motorhomes are a statistical afterthought in crash data for a reason. They are driven few miles by experienced drivers at moderate highway speeds and spend most of the year parked. IIHS has spent fifteen years documenting that ESC roughly halves fatal single-vehicle crash risk in passenger vehicles,[6] which is why the mandate gap matters more than the incident count, and the incident count across all 13 recalls is zero. But zero injuries is not the same as zero risk. Nobody crash-tests a Class A motorhome. In September, thirteen recalls explained why someone should.
