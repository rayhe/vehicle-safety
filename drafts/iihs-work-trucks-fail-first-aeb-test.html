IIHS Gave Work Trucks Their First Crash-Avoidance Test. Every Heavy-Duty Pickup Flunked.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 17, 2026

On October 8, the IIHS finally ran commercial vehicles through the same front crash prevention tests it gives family sedans. Of 11 cargo vans and Class 3 pickups tested, not one heavy-duty pickup earned a Good rating in either test. Three rated Poor at avoiding a collision with another vehicle outright.[1]

6,215
people died in 2024 in U.S. crashes involving heavy- or medium-duty vehicles or light vans — 16% of all traffic deaths (IIHS, citing NHTSA data)

Consider the lab numbers. Chevrolet's Silverado 3500HD failed to avoid the passenger-car target in all six test runs. Ford's F-350 could only avoid the target at 31 mph; at 43 mph, 8,000 pounds of truck reduced its speed not at all. Ram's 3500 managed only a Marginal.[2] Only the Mercedes-Benz Sprinter 2500, a van, managed a Good in the vehicle-to-vehicle test.

But the pedestrian side held a sharper surprise. The Ford Transit's pre-collision assist failed to produce a timely warning in motorcycle scenarios regardless of speed, and its pedestrian warnings arrived 0.6 to 1.6 seconds before impact. That's not a warning; it's a narration.

Then there's the bottom of the table: the Chevrolet Express 2500 and GMC Savana 2500, Poor in both tests. Not because their sensors are bad — because there aren't any. Neither van offers automatic emergency braking, period.[3] In 2026, you can still buy a brand-new work van with a safety spec sheet from the Clinton era.

6.6×
the Chevrolet Express's occupant fatality rate (0.92/100M VMT) versus the Ford Transit's (0.14) in FARS 2014–2023

And here's where the lab meets the morgue ledger. Cross-referencing the tested vans against FARS per-model fatality data, the two AEB-less vans are also the two deadliest vans by estimated VMT rate: the Express at 0.92 deaths per 100 million miles, the Savana at 0.54, the Transit at 0.14. That's 6.6 times the Transit's fatality rate for the Express, 3.9 times for the Savana.[4] America ran this experiment for a decade. The AEB-less platform lost.

Now the regulatory punchline: NHTSA's FMVSS 127, the automatic emergency braking mandate finalized in May 2024, applies to passenger cars and light trucks up to 10,000 pounds GVWR, effective September 2029, while Class 3 pickups start at 10,001 pounds. Every single vehicle in this test is legally exempt from the mandate.[5] NHTSA estimates the rule saves at least 360 lives a year, none of which accrue to this segment. IIHS president David Harkey called the results "a wake-up call for commercial vehicle makers," which is polite executive-speak for: your product line is a decade behind your own test protocol.

Fairness demands the counterargument, and it's a strong one. A Poor rating measures lab performance under a specific protocol, not crash outcomes. The F-350 carries a FARS fatality rate of 0.15 per 100M VMT, which makes it one of the safest vehicles in the entire database. Lab tests and morgue ledgers answer different questions, and conflating them is the actuary's original sin. Heavy-duty pickups also skew toward low-mileage work cycles where high-speed AEB matters less. Ford and GM could reasonably argue the test measures a scenario their customers rarely encounter.

What the FARS data cannot tell you is how many of those 6,215 deaths the other vehicle caused. That's the uncomfortable arithmetic: the victims of these crashes are mostly not in the truck.[6] So if you manage a fleet, read the spec sheet before the next order cycle and make AEB a line item, not an assumption. If you drive a Transit, don't trust the pre-collision assist around motorcycles until Ford retunes it. And if you're buying a used full-size work van, know that an Express or Savana has no AEB to check for, not even as an option. Check any VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls.
