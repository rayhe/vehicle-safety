By The Numbers

Lincoln Recalled 11,623 Hybrids Because the Anti-Rust Oil Caused the Rust

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 1, 2026

I ran the numbers. Then I ran them again. The anti-rust oil is what caused the rust. Ford filed NHTSA campaign 26V688 on October 20, recalling 11,623 Lincoln Nautilus and Corsair hybrids because a supplier's anti-rust oil is corroding the circuit board it was sprayed next to.[1] A chemical whose entire job is preventing corrosion is causing corrosion, inside the transmission's park module, and the result is a luxury crossover that can shift itself into neutral at highway speed.

100%
Ford’s estimated share of the recalled population carrying the defect: not some of them, every single one.

Here is the failure chain, and it reads like a Rube Goldberg machine built by a saboteur. EFI Automotive, a Tier 1 supplier in Wuhan, swapped the anti-rust oil inside the Integrated Park Module without telling Ford and without checking whether the new oil gets along with the conformal coating on the module’s circuit board.[1] It does not get along. Oil degrades coating, corrosion creeps across the rotary sensor circuit, sensor voltage sags, and the powertrain controller reads the sag as a fault. An update to the Hybrid Powertrain Control Module’s software had already decided what a fault means: shift to neutral, even while you are driving.

Now the part that impressed me, which does not happen often. Two warranty claims for loss of motive power on 2026 Ford Edge hybrids triggered a global escalation in August. Ford traced suspect-oil modules through supplier records to transmissions to VINs, confirmed the chemistry in September, and filed federally by October 13.[1] Seventeen US data points, zero crashes, zero injuries, and 11,623 cars recalled anyway. That is the recall apparatus working somewhere near its design spec.

Two numbers will not leave me alone, starting with 100%. When a manufacturer files “estimated percentage with defect: 100%,” it is not hedging. The defect is not random; a supplier’s unvalidated chemical swap went into every one of these modules, and Ford traced all of them. Compare Ford’s own tailgate recall from the same month: 30,123 trucks, 2% estimated defective, which means 98% of those owners got a letter about somebody else’s cable.[5] A 100% estimate is paradoxically the good kind, because it means they found every one.

Second: zero. Zero Ford Edges appear in the recall population, though Edge hybrids running the same HF55 transmission filed the loss-of-power claims that launched the investigation. Ford’s trace says the suspect oil went to Lincoln modules. Probably. The filing never explains the exclusion, and “probably” is doing heavy lifting in that sentence. Somebody at Ford should address it on the record.

The fair counterargument: 106 warranty claims worldwide is 0.9% of the recalled population. Nobody has crashed, nobody is hurt, and a wrench light with a “Service Vehicle Soon” popup warns you before the neutral shift.[1] A cynic could call this expensive paperwork for a lab problem. Half right. Observed failures are rare, but exposure is total, corrosion is progressive, and the failure mode is disengagement at speed. You do not wait for the body count on that one.

Drive a 2026–2027 Nautilus Hybrid or a 2027 Corsair Hybrid? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now; the lookup has worked since October 26.[4] Interim letters start November 2, which is tomorrow, but the mail can wait; reference Ford campaign 26S75 at 1-866-436-7332. If the wrench light appears with a shifting-system message, treat it as real and get off the road, because the next thing the car may do is put itself in neutral. And one free lesson: when a recall says 100%, the manufacturer is telling you it knows exactly where the bodies are buried. Believe them.
