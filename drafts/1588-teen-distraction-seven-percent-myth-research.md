## Kill test: PASS

**Proposed angle:** NHTSA's one-page data sheet *Teens and Distracted Driving in 2024* (DOT HS 813 792, April 2026) has never been a standalone article on this site — full-content grep for "813 792" returns exactly one hit, a secondary citation inside #1454's research notes. The sheet's numbers demolish two stereotypes at once. First, the distracted teen is not overrepresented: 15-to-20-year-olds were 9% of drivers in fatal crashes and 10% of distracted drivers in fatal crashes, and the distracted share is a flat 6% for every age group from 15 to 44. Second, the phone is not the story: cellphones appear in only 14% of distraction-affected fatal crashes, and only 13% of distracted 15-to-20 drivers were on a phone. The number that repeats is 7: 7% of teens killed were in distraction-affected crashes, 7% of teen drivers in fatal crashes were distracted, 7% of people killed in teen-driver crashes involved a distracted teen. And the honest caveat that makes the piece: all of it is a floor. Police coded 5% of fatal-crash drivers as distracted; NHTSA's own naturalistic-observation work puts distraction in 29% of crashes.

**Challenge — is this genuinely new after 1,587 articles?** Yes, three ways. (1) No prior draft, story, or queue entry uses DOT HS 813 792 as its primary source (grep: one secondary citation only). (2) The nearest neighbors are different documents: the all-ages distracted-driving sheet DOT HS 813 790 was covered by `distracted-driving-undercount-3x` and `distracted-86-percent-not-phones` (both published), and #1454 used the Young Drivers sheet DOT HS 813 814. None ran the teen-distraction sheet's cross-tabs or the flat-6%-across-ages finding. (3) Novel contribution: the 7%-everywhere coincidence, the age-flatness computation (teens 6% vs 21-24 6% vs 25-34 6% vs 35-44 6%), the teen self-kill share (47% of people killed in distracted-teen crashes were teens, vs the #1454 pattern where young-driver crashes mostly kill others), and the floor-vs-count framing from the 29% naturalistic figure.

## Verified facts

### Primary: NHTSA, Teens and Distracted Driving in 2024, DOT HS 813 792, April 2026
https://crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov/Api/Public/ViewPublication/813792
1. **186 teens (15-19) killed in distraction-affected crashes in 2024** — 6% of all 3,208 distraction-affected deaths; 7% of the 2,662 teens killed in all traffic crashes.
2. **241 distracted teen drivers in fatal crashes in 2024** — 8% of all 3,026 distracted drivers in fatal crashes; 7% of the 3,667 teen drivers in fatal crashes.
3. **264 people killed in crashes involving distracted teen drivers in 2024** — 7% of the 3,996 people killed in all teen-driver crashes. **123 of the 264 (47%) were teens themselves.**
4. Definitions: teens = ages 15-19 in this sheet (note: the Young Drivers sheet DOT HS 813 814 uses 15-20; do not mix the two denominators).

### Cross-check: NHTSA, Distracted Driving in 2024, DOT HS 813 790, April 2026
https://crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov/Api/Public/ViewPublication/813790
5. **3,208 people killed and an estimated 315,167 injured** in distraction-affected crashes in 2024. 2,955 fatal crashes (8% of 36,297); 3,026 distracted drivers (5% of 55,620 drivers in fatal crashes).
6. **Cellphones in only 404 fatal crashes (14% of distraction-affected fatal crashes); 437 people died in them.** Phones are the minority of distraction. (The site's published `distracted-86-percent-not-phones` covers this; cite as cross-reference, not repeat.)
7. **Age-flatness:** 6% of drivers aged 15-20, 21-24, 25-34, and 35-44 in fatal crashes were distracted — identical across all four groups. Teens are not the outliers. Ages 15-20 were 9% of all drivers in fatal crashes but 10% of distracted drivers and 10% of cellphone-distracted drivers — barely above share.
8. **The undercount (the floor):** NHTSA's naturalistic-observation cost study found distraction in **29% of all crashes**, 10,546 deaths and $98.2 billion in 2019 — vs 8% of fatal crashes police-coded as distraction-affected in 2024. The police-coded 3,208 is a floor; the true toll is roughly three times higher. (Site's `distracted-driving-undercount-3x` covers the all-ages version; here it frames the teen numbers.)
9. **639 nonoccupants** (pedestrians, pedalcyclists) killed in distraction-affected crashes in 2024.

### Cross-check: NHTSA, Young Drivers: 2024 Data, DOT HS 813 814, July 2026
https://crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov/Api/Public/Publication/813814
10. Young drivers (15-20) killed: 2,162; total killed in young-driver crashes: 5,247. Teen sheet (15-19) total is 3,996 — the 20-year-olds account for the 1,251 gap. Do not conflate.
11. 29% of young drivers killed had BAC .01+; 25% were at .08+. Distraction and alcohol overlap in the same age group but the sheets measure different risks.

### Cross-check: NHTSA, Overview of Motor Vehicle Traffic Crashes in 2024, DOT HS 813 791, April 2026
https://crashstats.nhtsa.dot.gov/Api/Public/ViewPublication/813791
12. 39,254 total traffic deaths in 2024; 36,297 fatal crashes; 55,620 drivers in fatal crashes.

## Computations (original to this piece)
- **The 7% triple:** 186/2,662 = 7.0% of teen deaths distraction-affected; 241/3,667 = 6.6% of teen drivers in fatal crashes distracted; 264/3,996 = 6.6% of teen-crash deaths involved a distracted teen. Three different denominators, same answer.
- **Teens are 8% of the distracted-driver file, not the face of it:** 241/3,026 = 8.0%. Drivers 25-34 alone were 22% of distracted drivers (667). The modal distracted driver in a fatal crash is 25-34, not 15-19.
- **Self-kill share:** 123/264 = 46.6% of people killed in distracted-teen crashes were teens. Compare #1454: young drivers were 41.2% of deaths in their own crashes. Distraction concentrates the dying on the kids themselves.
- **The floor multiplier:** 29% naturalistic vs 8% police-coded = 3.6x. Applied to the teen sheet: the 186 police-counted teen distraction deaths imply roughly 670 true distraction-involved teen deaths. State as an estimate with the methodology caveat, not as a count.
- **Phones vs everything else:** 404/2,955 = 13.7% of distraction-affected fatal crashes involved a phone. For teens specifically, 40 of 315 distracted 15-20 drivers were on phones (13%) — the same share as all ages. The phone panic is misaimed twice: wrong device share, wrong age group.

## Strongest counterargument
Police-coded distraction is the weakest number in FARS — it depends on what a dead or surviving driver admits, what a phone record shows, and what an officer writes down. The 29% naturalistic figure comes from 2019, a different year and a different method (in-car video), and cannot be multiplied cleanly onto 2024 teen counts. A skeptic can fairly say the "7% everywhere" is an artifact of small numbers rounding to the same integer, and that the teen sheet's single page gives no trend, no state split, and no distraction-type split for teens. All true — which is why the article frames 241 and 186 as floors and the 7% as a pattern worth noticing, not a law of nature.

## Limitations
- 2024 is the FARS Annual Report File (ARF), near-final but subject to revision.
- Distraction is underreported by design: FARS codes what police reports record; the 29% figure is the best available correction and is from 2019.
- The teen sheet (813 792) is a single-page data sheet with no year-over-year table; no trend claim is made from it alone.
- "Teens" here means 15-19; the Young Drivers sheet means 15-20. Denominators differ; the article keeps them separate.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- Parents: the passenger is the distraction too — the sheet's "distraction" includes talking to passengers and adjusting controls, not just phones. GDL passenger limits exist for this reason; enforce them past the legal minimum.
- Teens: the crash that kills you is likelier to kill you than anyone else (47% self-kill share). The person at risk from your distraction is sitting in your seat.
- Buyers: a phone mount and a do-not-disturb-while-driving setting cost nothing and address the 14% phone slice; the other 86% is attention discipline, which no app sells.
- Policymakers: if enforcement targets only phones, it misses 86% of distraction-affected fatal crashes. Hands-free laws are necessary and insufficient.
