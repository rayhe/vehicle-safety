The Crash Test Didn't Get Harder. The Trucks Did.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 25, 2026

In 2021, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety looked at its side crash test, looked at the average American SUV, and decided the test equipment was too skinny. Out went the 3,300-pound barrier traveling at 31 mph; in came a 4,200-pound barrier at 37 mph. IIHS's own engineers did the math: the new test configuration carries 82 percent more crash energy than the old one[1]. That jump did not come from cars getting more dangerous. It came from the trucks they share the road with.

17% → 5%
Share of rated models scoring Poor or Marginal in the updated side test, 2023 to 2026 model years

When the tougher test arrived, the ratings fell apart: a census of IIHS ratings found 17 percent of 2023 models scored Poor or Marginal in side impact, a share that dropped to 10 percent, then 6 percent, then 5 percent by the 2026 model year[2]. Engineers answered with stronger B-pillars, wider curtain airbags, and better door beams, fixing in three model years what an industry that needs five to redesign a headlight usually manages in a decade. Victory seems premature, because the research that forced the test change found real-world side crashes, which account for nearly a quarter of passenger vehicle occupant fatalities, were already more severe than anything the test measured[1]. Nobody in the lab raised the bar; traffic did, and the old test simply was not tall enough to see it.

I ran the fleet numbers from NHTSA's fatality database to see which side of that equation is dying. Sedans post a median fatality rate of 0.68 deaths per 100 million miles traveled and account for 46.6 percent of all model-attributed deaths from 2014 to 2023, while SUVs die at a median rate of 0.35, exactly half[3]. Honda's Accord alone accounts for 7,102 deaths at a rate of 3.07; the Chevy Cobalt sits at 5.10. Meanwhile the vehicles most likely to be doing the striking, the SUVs and pickups averaging 4,200 pounds and climbing, are the class least likely to die per mile. That is the geometry the heavier barrier finally admits: the struck survive the test or they don't, but the strikers were never in danger to begin with. IIHS president David Harkey said the test reflects what happens when sedans are struck by higher-riding pickups or SUVs, which means the calibration target was never an abstract barrier. It was the parking lot outside your office.

Full credit where it is due, because the fix worked, and it worked fast: automakers redesigned doors and airbag coverage in three model years, and the heavier barrier protects big-truck occupants too, not just sedans. But one part of this cannot be engineered around. Barriers get heavier because the striking vehicles got heavier, and no B-pillar repeals physics. A test can measure how well a small car survives being hit by a large one; it cannot make the large one smaller. Ratings will keep recovering, the fleet will keep growing, and eventually someone will put a heavier barrier in the test again while we all pretend the test got harder.

If you are shopping used, a 2020 side-impact rating and a 2024 side-impact rating are not on the same scale, even when they look identical in the listing. Verify that the rating is the updated-test version before comparing model years[4]. On older vehicles, curtain airbag coverage is the single specification most worth confirming, because it is the component doing most of the work in exactly the crash this test recreates. And if you drive a Civic into a world of 4,200-pound everything, know that the old test imagined a 3,300-pound world you no longer live in.
