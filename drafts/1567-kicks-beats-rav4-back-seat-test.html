The $23,000 Nissan Kicks Just Out-Crashed America's Best-Selling SUV

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 22, 2026

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds. In IIHS's updated moderate overlap test, a car hits a deformable barrier at 40 mph with 40 percent of its nose, and two dummies ride along: a 50th-percentile man up front, and a dummy the size of a 5th-percentile woman, or a 12-year-old child, belted in behind the driver.[1] That rear dummy is the whole test. And in the November batch, it just humiliated the best-selling SUV in America. The redesigned 2026 Toyota RAV4 scored Acceptable in hybrid form and Marginal as a plug-in hybrid, while the $22,730 Nissan Kicks scored Good and took home a Top Safety Pick award.[1][2]

1 in 5
SUVs in IIHS's November 2026 moderate-overlap batch that earned a Good rating for rear-seat protection. The one that passed costs $22,730.

Six vehicles went through the updated test this round. Four failed to earn Good. The RAV4 hybrid, the RAV4 plug-in hybrid, the Audi Q3, and the Chevrolet Trailblazer all fell short in the back seat, and the only SUV that passed was the cheapest car in the group.[1][2] The 2027 Volkswagen Jetta earned Good too, but it is a sedan, and sedans were never the ones with the rear-seat problem. Every one of these SUVs earned Good in the small overlap front test, Good in the side test, and Good or Acceptable everywhere else. The back seat was the only place any of them failed. That is not a coincidence. That is a design priority, written in sheet metal.

The failure mode is identical in the Q3 and the RAV4 plug-in, and it is worth picturing precisely, because it is a belt doing the wrong job at the worst moment. On impact, the shoulder belt rides up toward the dummy's neck. The lap belt slides off the pelvis, where it belongs, and onto the soft abdomen, where it does not.[3] A belt on the abdomen loads organs instead of bone. The Q3 earned a Poor rating for rear-seat restraints and dummy kinematics, with a moderate risk of chest injury on top. The RAV4 plug-in got Marginal, with the same belt migration. Same physics, same outcome, two different price points, two different badges.

Now the part that should make every engineer in this industry slightly embarrassed. The Kicks failed this exact test first. When the redesigned Kicks debuted for 2025, it scored Poor. Nissan responded by revising the rear seat belts and load limiters, the hardware that controls how much force the belt lets through before it yields, and the cars built after June 2026 retested Good.[2] A belt and a load limiter. Not a new platform, not a structural redesign, not a billion-dollar program. A parts-bin fix, inside a single model year, on a car that starts at $22,730 while the average new car now sells for more than $50,000.[5]

That fix creates a trap nobody warns shoppers about. Two 2026 Kicks with the same nameplate, same model year, same window sticker can carry different crash ratings: Poor if built before June 2026, Good if built after.[2] Hyundai did the same thing with the Palisade, whose Top Safety Pick+ applies only to cars built after November 2025, when it changed the rear seatbelt buckles.[4] The safety rating is now a function of the build month, not the model year, and the door-jamb sticker is the only place that tells you which car you are buying.

To be fair to Toyota, and fairness matters here, the RAV4 is not a dangerous car. It earned Good in every other IIHS category. No federal crash data yet shows elevated rear-seat deaths in the redesign, because a 2026 model has barely a year of real-world exposure, and one Acceptable in one test configuration does not overturn a safety record. Toyota could reasonably call this a belt-geometry edge case in a scenario most families will never replicate.

That defense collapses the moment you compare it with the Kicks. Nissan found the identical failure mode, fixed it with a belt and a load limiter, and did it on the cheapest car in the segment. If the fix is that cheap, "one test scenario" stops being a defense and starts being an excuse. IIHS added the rear dummy to this test in 2022 and tightened the scoring in 2024.[2] The RAV4 and the Q3 are clean-sheet 2026 redesigns. They had four years to design for a test they knew was coming, and they still shipped a back seat that lets the belt slide onto a child's abdomen. The front seat has had thirty years of optimization. The back seat is still where manufacturers cut corners, because until 2022, nobody was watching.

Here is what to do with this. Shopping for a 2026 Kicks: check the build date on the door jamb. Only cars built after June 2026 carry the Good rating and the award. Shopping for a 2026 RAV4 or RAV4 plug-in: know the rear seat is the weak spot, and if you regularly carry kids or smaller adults back there, the plug-in's Marginal means the lap belt can ride up onto the abdomen in a frontal crash. Shopping for a 2027 Q3: Poor in the updated moderate overlap test, Good everywhere else, same belt problem. And as a general rule, check the IIHS rating for the exact model year and build date at iihs.org/ratings, not just the nameplate. Awards now turn on build months, and the cheapest car on the lot just proved the back seat is not a luxury feature. It is a belt, a load limiter, and a decision.
