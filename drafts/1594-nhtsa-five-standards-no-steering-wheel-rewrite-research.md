# Research — #1594: NHTSA Is Rewriting Five Safety Standards for Cars With No Drivers. It Still Hasn't Written the One for Driving.

**Slug:** `1594-nhtsa-five-standards-no-steering-wheel-rewrite`
**Journalist:** Vin Wreckage (Existential Dread Columnist — paradoxes, counterintuitive findings, the big picture)
**Kicker:** Existential Dread
**Date researched:** 2026-11-26

## Story in one line
NHTSA has proposed rewriting five federal safety standards so a car with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no driver can legally exist — the gear display, the wipers, the tire placard, the brake pedal, the lights — while the one standard that would govern how the robot actually drives still does not exist, and while two companies already run such cars on public roads under two different legal theories.

The other four comment periods have closed, making them the next candidates for final action. This is the federal rulebook for driverless cars being rewritten in public, right now.

## Verified facts (all traced to primary sources this run)

### The five proposals — five documents, six standards
| # | Standard | What it covers | Proposal | FR citation | Docket | Comments | Status |
|---|----------|---------------|----------|-------------|--------|----------|--------|
| 1 | FMVSS 102 | Gear display ("P R N D") | Except ADS vehicles w/o manual controls from the shift-position display requirement | 91 FR 12532, Mar 16, 2026 | NHTSA-2026-0628 (RIN 2127-AM72) | 11 | Closed Apr 15, 2026 |
| 2 | FMVSS 103 + 104 | Windshield defrost/defog, wipers/washers | Except ADS vehicles w/o manual controls from both standards entirely | 91 FR 12537, Mar 16, 2026 | NHTSA-2026-0629 | 14 | Closed Apr 15, 2026 |
| 3 | FMVSS 110 | Tire placard (pressures, load limits, driver's side) | Let vehicles with no driver's side put the placard on the left side instead | 91 FR 16172, Apr 1, 2026 | NHTSA-2026-0630 | 8 | Closed May 1, 2026 |
| 4 | FMVSS 135 | Light-vehicle brakes | Foot-operated service brake + hand/foot parking brake controls apply only to vehicles with manual controls; stopping-distance requirements unchanged | 91 FR 38593, Jun 26, 2026 | NHTSA-2026-0728 | 61 | Closed Jul 27, 2026; extended to Aug 26, 2026 |
| 5 | FMVSS 108 | Lamps, signals, telltales | Drop 12.8V headlamp test; remove vertical headlamp arrangement rules; manual turn-signal/hazard/beam-switching controls + telltales only for vehicles with manual controls; allow optional cyan ADS marker lamps | 91 FR 67243, Oct 26, 2026 | NHTSA-2026-1519 (RIN 2127-AM70) | 66 | Open until Dec 10, 2026 (extended Nov 16, FR Doc 2026-23239; originally Nov 25) |

- **Total public comments across all five proposals: 160** (11 + 14 + 8 + 61 + 66, per regulations.gov counts in the Federal Register API records, checked Nov 26, 2026). The tire-placard rewrite drew 8 comments. The gear-display rewrite drew 11.
- **Shared design:** each carves out "ADS-equipped vehicles without manually operated driving controls" from requirements that exist only to serve a human driver, and each leaves in place the requirements that protect occupants and other road users. NHTSA describes each as deregulatory under Executive Order 14192.
- **FMVSS 102 abstract, NHTSA's words:** the shift-position display "does not fulfill the same safety need in an ADS-equipped vehicle without manually operated driving controls" and the change is "part of a larger NHTSA effort to address vehicle automation in the agency's regulations."
- **FMVSS 103/104 is the broadest carve-out:** it removes the federal defroster/wiper requirement entirely for no-controls vehicles. It does not address how an ADS keeps its own cameras and sensors clear — the proposal is about windshields, not sensors.
- **FMVSS 135 footnote (verified via secondary read of the FR document):** NHTSA writes it "is taking no position at this time as to how a passenger should be able to direct an ADS-operated vehicle to stop, or how the ADS should respond to such direction." The brake proposal does not answer how a rider stops the car.
- **FMVSS 108 hazard definition change:** the standard today defines the hazard warning signal operating unit as "a driver-controlled device" and requires all switches be actuated "by a single driver action." The proposal takes "driver" out.
- **Cyan ADS marker lamps (FMVSS 108):** optional, steady-burning (not flashing), cyan/blue-green per SAE J578, at least 15 inches above ground, not optically combined with any other lamp; based on SAE J3134 (first issued May 2019, revised Dec 2025, which added rear and side lamps). NHTSA cites a survey of 4,400+ drivers in the US, UK, Germany, and Japan in which more than 80 percent said they would like to identify ADS-equipped vehicles by a marker lamp. AAMVA recommends marker lamps so law enforcement can tell whether an ADS is in control. Mercedes-Benz told NHTSA a 360-degree view of cyan lamps would help law enforcement and other road users; its US validation was a two-year, five-car on-road program in California, 2023-2025 (first US approvals for turquoise marker lights: California and Nevada, Dec 2023, for DRIVE PILOT). NHTSA's tentative view is that a final rule would preempt California's provisions where they differ.

### The two live legal theories
- **Path 1 — exemption (Zoox):** NHTSA granted Zoox a temporary exemption July 31, 2026 (91 FR 48494, Docket NHTSA-2025-0523), July 31, 2026 through July 31, 2028, at most 2,500 exempted vehicles introduced for commercial deployment in any 12-month period, covering portions of FMVSS 103, 104, 108, 111, 135, 201, 205, and 208. Legal basis §30113(b)(3)(B)(iv); NHTSA found an equivalent level of safety. Application filed Aug 22, 2025 — about 11 months to decide. The grant carries "Operational Authorizations" prescribing operating conditions, which NHTSA can modify or expand. Four of the exempted standards (103, 104, 108, 135) are the same ones the 2026 proposals would rewrite — if the rewrites become final, some of what Zoox needed an exemption for would no longer need one.
- **Path 2 — self-certification (Tesla):** NHTSA does not approve vehicles before sale; manufacturers certify compliance with every applicable FMVSS and NHTSA enforces after the fact. Tesla began commercial Cybercab rides in Austin Sept 3, 2026 and certified the cars — which lack permanently attached steering wheel, pedals, and mirrors — as compliant with all applicable standards. NHTSA opened Audit Query AQ26002 the same day (~1,000 vehicles) to examine how far the certification rests on determinations that certain FMVSS are inapplicable. Tesla's sworn answers to the 21-question Special Order were due Oct 30, 2026; the public docket holds a one-page letter claiming the entire file as confidential business information, a blank Request 8 placeholder, and the First Responder Interaction Plan v1.2 (covered in full by #1427 — reference, do not re-report).
- **Scale of the exemption track:** from 2016 through 2025, the Automated Vehicle Exemption Program issued special exemptions for more than 460 imported ADS-equipped vehicles in over 360 projects across 31 states, to more than 40 applicants in over 130 cities.

### What is NOT in any of this (the five gaps)
1. **No final rules.** All five are NPRMs. Until NHTSA publishes final rules, the current text of FMVSS 102, 103, 104, 108, 110, and 135 is the law.
2. **No performance standard for the ADS itself.** The rewrites remove requirements that assume a driver. They add no requirements for how well the ADS drives. NHTSA says it has begun work on "ADS performance requirements" (announced March 10, 2026); nothing has been proposed.
3. **No standard for passenger controls.** Nothing requires a stop button, an emergency exit procedure, or a way for a passenger to reach a remote operator. (See the FMVSS 135 "taking no position" footnote above.)
4. **No mandatory marker lamp.** The cyan lamp is optional. A no-controls vehicle without one still complies.
5. **Several standards not yet touched.** Zoox's exemption also covered parts of FMVSS 111 (rear visibility), 201 (occupant protection in interior impact), 205 (glazing), and 208 (occupant crash protection), which have not been proposed for rewriting.

### Related tracks
- **AV STEP withdrawn:** the prior administration's voluntary ADS safety program (proposed Jan 15, 2025, 90 FR 4130) was withdrawn June 26, 2026, the same day as the FMVSS 135 NPRM. NHTSA cited instead: AVEP opened to US-built vehicles, streamlined Part 555 exemption process, streamlined Standing General Order on crash reporting, and guidance on general exemptions.
- **ADS 2.0 guidance replacement:** NHTSA intends to replace its 2017 guidance *Automated Driving Systems 2.0: A Vision for Safety* with "comprehensive, updated guidance" (Docket NHTSA-2026-1520). Comment period runs until July 31, 2027.
- **The roads these cars join:** NHTSA estimates 16,750 people died in US traffic crashes in H1 2026, rate 1.02 per 100M VMT — the second-lowest half-year rate since 2014 (DOT HS 813 875, Nov 10, 2026; covered #1494). The same month NHTSA opened RQ26006, a recall query into whether Waymo's December 2025 software fix stopped its robotaxis passing stopped school buses — most of the violations happened after the fix (covered #1461, #1523). The ADS that cannot reliably read a school bus's flashing red lights is the same ADS the lighting standard is being rewritten for.

## Original calculations / analysis (verifiable, show work in article)
- **160 comments, five rewrites.** 11 + 14 + 8 + 61 + 66 = 160 public comments across all five proposals, per regulations.gov counts checked Nov 26, 2026. For scale: the Honda Auto Start/Stop investigation EA26007 opened Nov 13, 2026 on 701 complaints alone — one defect probe drew more than four times as many citizen reports as all five driverless-car rewrites drew comments combined.
- **The comment curve is the story.** The first three proposals (gear display, wipers, placard) drew 11, 14, and 8 comments — the public did not show up. The brake proposal drew 61; the lighting proposal drew 66. Attention arrived only when the rules touched things people can picture: brakes and lights.
- **The 2,500 vs 1,000 split.** Zoox's exemption caps commercial deployment at 2,500 vehicles per 12 months, under operating conditions NHTSA can change. Tesla's self-certified Cybercab fleet under audit is ~1,000 vehicles, with no operating conditions and its safety case sealed. Two companies, same roads, opposite levels of federal oversight.
- **The 11-month vs 0-day contrast.** Zoox waited about 11 months for its exemption decision. Tesla waited zero days: it certified compliance itself and started charging for rides the same day NHTSA opened its audit.
- **The five gaps, counted.** Of the eight standards Zoox needed exemptions from, four have rewrite proposals and four (111, 201, 205, 208) do not. Half the rulebook for a car with no driver is still written for a car with one — and the half that matters most (how the car drives) was never in the rulebook at all.

## Story spine (Vin Wreckage voice)
- Lede: the federal government spent 2026 deleting the word "driver" from the safety standards, one rule at a time, and it still has not written a rule for the driving.
- The census, standard by standard, with the absurdity ranked: the gear display nobody will ever read, the wipers for a windshield nobody looks through, the tire placard moved six inches left, the brake pedal that no longer needs a foot, the turn signal that no longer needs a hand.
- The cyan lamp: the one thing NHTSA added instead of removing. Optional. A robot car can glow cyan or not, and both are legal, so the signal teaches the public a rule with built-in exceptions.
- The two paths: Zoox asked permission in public and got a leash; Tesla certified itself and sealed the homework. Both are carrying passengers.
- The gaps: no final rules, no driving standard, no stop button, no mandatory lamp, four untouched standards.
- Actionable: FMVSS 108 comments close December 10, 2026 (regulations.gov, Docket NHTSA-2026-1519) — the last open window; if you see cyan, the car is driving itself, but if you see no cyan, assume nothing; and if you ride in a car with no steering wheel, know that no federal rule requires it to give you a way to stop it.
- Closer: the rulebook is being rewritten for the passenger's protection, and the passenger cannot stop the car. That is the whole story in one sentence.

## Limitations (for the limitations section)
- All five rewrites are proposals, not final rules; NHTSA may change or drop any provision, and the comment counts are snapshots as of Nov 26, 2026 (regulations.gov counts update as comments post).
- The FMVSS 108 comment deadline is December 10, 2026 per the extension (FR Doc 2026-23239, published Nov 16, 2026); the Federal Register API record still shows the original November 25 date, and some secondary coverage published before the extension still cites November 25. The extension document governs.
- The "taking no position" quote from the FMVSS 135 NPRM and the E.O. 14192 deregulatory framing come from secondary reads of the Federal Register documents (Tesorb special report, Nov 18, 2026, which cites the FR doc numbers); the abstracts for all five NPRMs plus the Zoox exemption were verified directly against the Federal Register API this run.
- The Mercedes five-car validation sample and the 80-percent survey figure are as cited in the FMVSS 108 NPRM via secondary summaries; survey wording and sampling were not independently verified.
- The Waymo RQ26006 and Tesla AQ26002 details are reused from prior site coverage (#1461, #1523, #1427) and their research files, not re-verified from NHTSA this run; the FMVSS census itself is the new work.

## Strongest counterargument
This is how safety regulation is supposed to work, and the article's alarm is partly misplaced. Self-certification is not a loophole — it is how every car on American roads is approved, and NHTSA's post-market enforcement (investigations, recalls, the AQ26002 audit itself) is the check. The carve-outs remove requirements that genuinely serve no purpose without a driver: a gear display nobody reads, a wiper for a windshield nobody looks through, a brake pedal nobody can press. Keeping them would be regulatory theater. The cyan lamp is optional because mandating an unproven signal on a tiny fleet would freeze a design choice before the technology settles, and standardizing the color first is how lighting regulation normally proceeds. And the missing ADS performance standard is a real gap, but it is a harder problem than any single rulemaking — NHTSA has said the work is under way, and writing a bad driving standard fast would be worse than writing a good one slowly. The honest version of the thesis is narrower: the rulebook is being sensibly pruned, but the pruning is outrunning the one rule that would actually govern the driving, and the two companies on the road today got there under opposite levels of scrutiny.
