The Federal Watchdog Counted 9,200 Dead. DOT Agreed With Half the Ask

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 16, 2026

Let me talk about what the driver cannot see, because the federal watchdog finally did. The Government Accountability Office's July report on pedestrian and cyclist safety put the number out loud: about 9,200 pedestrians and cyclists were killed on American roads in 2024, roughly 65 percent more than in 2010,[1] or about twenty-five every day of the year. Congress asked a narrower question: does what the driver can see have something to do with the pile of bodies? GAO's answer was yes, with receipts.

Nobody keeps a database of blind-zone sizes, so the federal analysts had to use a proxy. GAO studied turning maneuvers in fatal pedestrian crashes instead: a vehicle mid-turn is exactly where a blind corner, a thick A-pillar, or a loftier cab matters most. Larger vehicles had higher odds of turning than going straight than cars did, and heavy-duty trucks like semitrailers had at least 12 times the odds of cars.[1] That is the geometry of "I never saw them," quantified by the government's own accountants.

Then statistics become choices. GAO reviewed 18 studies and interviewed five automakers and five truck manufacturers, and found the fixes already exist: side bicyclist alerts, pedestrian automatic emergency braking, internal visibility benchmarking that lets a manufacturer measure the blind zones the regulator cannot.[2] The EU has adopted driver visibility standards that manufacturers must meet; London runs a working Direct Vision Standard requiring some truck operators to fit extra safety equipment.[1] Two recommendations followed: DOT should determine a method to measure driver visibility, then fully analyze the risks and develop responses. DOT concurred with the first and declined the second. The regulator agreed to figure out how to measure the problem, then said no to studying it. Measurement without analysis is a hobby, not a safety program.

The counterargument, because this column does not grade on a curve: turning as a proxy is noisy. Semitrucks make far more urban turns than cars because their job is deliveries, so some of that 12x could be exposure, not blindness. Pedestrian AEB, already deployed, may save more lives per dollar than redesigning every cab. And the 2024 snapshot sits at the top of a curve that may be bending down: GHSA estimated 6,732 pedestrian deaths in 2025, the third straight decline.[3] None of that excuses the non-concurrence. A regulator that cannot measure blind zones, and declines to fully analyze them when asked, is not regulating that risk; it is filing it.

If you drive anything tall: your front and right-side blind zones are the kill zones. Slow your right turns, check twice before pulling away from a stop, and assume the driver cannot see a person standing directly ahead of the front bumper on many tall pickups.[4] If you buy for a fleet: London's Direct Vision Standard is a working procurement template, so require direct-vision ratings or camera retrofits before your trucks hit a city. If you shop for yourself: check IIHS's new commercial-vehicle crash-prevention ratings, and insist on pedestrian AEB. DOT's five-star program still does not test pedestrian safety. Nobody is coming to measure this for you. Measure it yourself.
