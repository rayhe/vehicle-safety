**Working headline:** "The $462 Million Verdict That Wasn't"

## Angle (1–2 sentences)
A St. Louis jury put a $462 million price on a trailer rear-impact guard in September 2024. By October 2025 the case was closed: a judge had cut the punitive award from $450 million to $108 million on constitutional grounds, and the parties settled on confidential terms with the insurer paying everything except a $30 million company contribution. The verdict everyone still quotes was never the ending, and the guard at the center of it complied with federal law when it was built.

## Verified facts (all sourced, no fabrication)
1. **The verdict (SEC Form 8-K, Wabash National Corp., March 20, 2025; corroborated by plaintiffs' counsel Simon Law and defense-side Amundsen Davis).** Case: *Eileen Williams, Elizabeth Perkins, et al. v. Wabash National Corporation, et al.*, Circuit Court of the City of St. Louis, Missouri, No. 2022-CC00495. Filed October 6, 2020. On September 5, 2024, a jury awarded $12.0 million compensatory ($6M to each family) and $450 million punitive against Wabash — $462 million total. On November 22, 2024, applying an offset for the plaintiffs' settlement with a separate defendant, the court entered judgment of $11.5 million compensatory and $450 million punitive. Wabash took a $461.5 million charge in Q3 2024.
2. **The crash (SEC 8-Ks; Amundsen Davis; Legal500).** 2019 accident: a passenger vehicle (Volkswagen sedan) with an unobstructed view struck the back of a nearly stopped 2004 Wabash trailer operated by co-defendant GDS Express Inc. Two men died: Taron Tailor and Nicholas Perkins, both young fathers. The rear impact guard detached/snapped on impact and the car slid underneath. The driver had a blood alcohol level over the legal limit and neither occupant wore a seat belt — evidence the jury was prevented from hearing. Impact speed reported at 40–55 mph (Amundsen Davis) / 55 mph (Legal500).
3. **The remittitur (SEC Form 8-K, March 20, 2025; Wabash press release March 24, 2025).** On March 20, 2025, the Circuit Court determined the $450 million punitive award "did not comport with the Company's constitutional rights" and ordered it reduced to $108 million, compensatory remaining at $11.5 million (the "Adjusted Award," $119.5 million total). In Q1 2025 Wabash recognized a $342 million reduction to the $461.5 million charge. Wabash continued to believe the damages "remain abnormally high and the verdict is not supported by the facts or the law."
4. **The settlement (SEC Form 8-K, October 9, 2025).** On October 9, 2025, Wabash finalized a settlement with the plaintiffs. Covered by insurance other than a $30 million contribution by the Company. The Company recognized an $81.2 million reduction to the charge taken in Q3 2024. During Q2 2025 it accrued $3.2 million contingent liability for penalty costs; as of June 30, 2025, total bond and contingent penalty interest expenses were $4.6 million. The settlement does not constitute an admission of liability or wrongdoing. The matter is now closed.
5. **The guard and the design theory (Simon Law plaintiffs' account; Amundsen Davis defense-side analysis; Legal500).** The trailer's rear impact guard ("U.S. RIG") was a two-post design dating to 1985, largely unchanged until 2007. Plaintiffs' evidence: Wabash performed no crash testing on the U.S. RIG from 1985 to 1996 despite similar accidents, and other manufacturers had moved to a four-post design. Plaintiffs also alleged decades of industry lobbying against stricter guard standards and a joint defense agreement among manufacturers. Wabash's position: the guard complied with all applicable federal standards, and no guard could have prevented the deaths at the impact speed; the human body cannot survive a 55-to-zero deceleration. The 1998 federal standard (FMVSS 223/224 as then in force) was the compliance benchmark at trial.
6. **The Missouri-law quirks (Amundsen Davis).** Missouri has no statute of repose on product liability claims, so the suit could proceed even though the trailer was sold 15 years before the crash. The court found that in the product-liability context, comparative fault does not include driver error that results in unintentional misuse, where the misuse was foreseeable — so the driver's intoxication and the occupants' unbelted status were excluded from evidence.
7. **The standard moved after the trailer was built (NHTSA final rule, July 2022, effective January 11, 2023; FMVSS 223/224).** NHTSA's 2022 rule upgraded rear impact guard requirements to protect occupants of compact and subcompact cars impacting at 56 km/h (35 mph), center and 50% overlap — adopting requirements similar to Transport Canada's standard. The rulemaking began from petitions by IIHS and by Marianne Karth and the Truck Safety Coalition, and fulfilled a mandate in the November 2021 Bipartisan Infrastructure Law. The 2004 trailer in the Missouri case was built to the pre-2022 standard. Separately, FMCSA's November 2021 final rule added rear impact guards to the annual-inspection checklist for commercial motor vehicles.
8. **Same-month parallel (site context, #1564).** On November 19, 2026, a Los Angeles jury awarded $21.9 million in *Gallegos v. Consolidated Disposal* over a garbage truck's non-compliant rear underride guard. Eight days earlier, NHTSA recalled MAC liquid-tank trailers for shipping with no rear impact guards at all (26V723). The Wabash case is the third leg of the same story, told from the opposite direction: the guard complied, and the jury still found liability.

## Original calculations (mine, shown methodology)
1. **Punitive-to-compensatory ratio, before and after the cut:** $450M / $12M = **37.5x** at verdict; $108M / $11.5M = **9.4x** after remittitur. The U.S. Supreme Court's guideposts in *BMW v. Gore* (1996) and *State Farm v. Campbell* (2003) treat a single-digit ratio as the presumptive outer limit for punitive damages; the St. Louis court's cut landed the ratio just above that line.
2. **The verdict's collapse before settlement:** $462M → $119.5M Adjusted Award = **-74.1%** of the headline number, erased by one judicial order before any settlement talks concluded.
3. **The charge unwind (SEC 8-K figures):** $461.5M charge (Q3 2024) minus $342M reduction (Q1 2025) minus $81.2M reduction (settlement) = **$38.3M** remaining charge. The company's own cash costs were the $30M settlement contribution plus $4.6M in bond and penalty-interest expenses = **$34.6M**, or **7.5%** of the $462M verdict figure. (The $3.7M gap between $38.3M and $34.6M is not itemized in the filings and is not allocated here.)
4. **Timeline:** trailer sold 2004 → crash 2019 (15 years later) → suit filed October 2020 → verdict September 2024 → judgment November 2024 → remittitur March 2025 → settlement October 2025 → closed. Six years from crash to closure; five years from filing to settlement.

## Strongest counterargument (must state at full strength)
The driver was drunk, unbelted, and hit a nearly stopped trailer he could see clearly at 40–55 mph. Wabash's experts testified that no rear impact guard on the market — not even the modern four-post design the plaintiffs held up as the alternative — would have saved two men in a car decelerating from highway speed to zero in the length of a trailer bed. The jury never heard about the alcohol or the seat belts, because Missouri law kept that evidence out. The $450 million punitive award was 37.5 times the compensatory damages, a ratio the trial court itself found unconstitutional and cut by three-quarters. The confidential settlement means nobody outside the parties knows what the families actually received, and the insurer paid most of it. Read this way, the case is not a safety story at all: it is a story about a runaway jury, a sympathetic pair of young fathers, and a manufacturer that followed the federal rule to the letter and got punished for the rule being old. The compliance defense has real force — if meeting the government's own standard is not enough, no manufacturer can know what is enough.

## Limitations (must state honestly)
- Settlement terms are confidential beyond what the 8-K discloses ($30M company contribution, insurance coverage, no admission of liability). The families' actual recovery is unknown; do not present any figure as the amount paid.
- The guard-failure mechanics ("snapped like a toothpick," decapitation) come from plaintiffs' counsel's account, not from the SEC filings, which describe only a rear-end collision with two fatalities. The article must attribute color to its source and not state disputed trial evidence as established fact.
- Impact speed is disputed (40–55 mph vs 55 mph); the 8-Ks do not state a speed. Do not pick one number.
- The $418.6M "gain" figure cited in a November 2026 legal summary of Wabash's 2025 annual report is not used here; the article uses only the 8-K figures ($342M + $81.2M reductions), which reconcile to a $38.3M remaining charge without relying on the secondary summary.
- Wabash maintains the verdict is not supported by the facts or the law; the settlement is not an admission. The article must carry the company's position.
- FARS underride counts are not used for this crash: underride fatalities are undercounted in FARS (GAO-19-264), and this crash's classification is not stated in the sources. Do not import side-underride statistics as if they describe this rear impact.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- If you drive a car, you cannot crash-test a trailer's guard from your lane — but you can refuse to tailgate a truck whose rear guard is visibly bent, damaged, or missing, and you can report it: a missing or damaged rear guard on a commercial truck is a reportable defect to the carrier and to FMCSA's National Consumer Complaint Database.
- If you run a fleet, the 2022 NHTSA rule covers new trailers, not the 2004 trailer still hauling freight. FMCSA's 2021 rule put rear guards on the annual-inspection checklist: a missing inspection record is evidence, not paperwork. Audit guards against FMVSS 223/224 as written, and against the 35-mph compact-car test, not the 1998 standard.
- If you buy a trailer or spec a fleet, ask for the four-post guard design and the crash-test data behind it. Wabash's own trial record included the fact that it had not crash-tested its two-post design for over a decade.
