Sobriety Report

Two Crashes Made 2024 the Deadliest Year Inside a School Bus.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

2024 was an average year for school transportation, and the deadliest year in a decade for the people riding inside the bus.[1] Both numbers come from the same federal report, and both are true, yet one of them is lying to you anyway.

NHTSA counts 110 people killed in school-transportation-related crashes in 2024, down from 128 the year before.[1] Inside the bus, the count went the other way. Seventeen school-vehicle occupants died in 2024, up from 13, the highest annual total from 2015 through 2024.[1] Twelve of those 17 died in two crashes.

12 of 17
School-vehicle occupant deaths in 2024 that came from two crashes, on two mornings, 64 days apart.

March 11, 2024, 11:29 a.m., U.S. 24 near Rushville, Illinois. A 25-passenger school bus carrying three small children crossed the centerline on a curve at about 55 mph and hit a sand-hauling semi head-on, and both vehicles burned. Five people died, four of them aboard the bus.[2] In March 2026 the NTSB closed its investigation with a probable cause that reads like a toxicology report: the bus driver, 57-year-old Angela Spiker, was impaired and fatigued from multiple prescription medications.[2] Her district had no safety policies at all: no fitness-for-duty rules, no cell-phone policy, nothing. It wrote its first transportation handbook in December 2025.[7]

May 14, 2024, 6:35 a.m., State Road 40 near Ocala, Florida. A retired school bus carrying 53 farmworkers to a watermelon harvest sideswiped a Ford Ranger that drifted across the centerline, ran off the road, hit a tree, and rolled. Eight workers died and forty more were hurt.[5] Pickup driver Bryan Maclean Howard, 41, told investigators he had smoked marijuana oil and taken prescription medication hours earlier. He was charged with eight counts of DUI-manslaughter.[6] His bus had no seat belts. None of the eight were children, and no school was involved, but the crash still counts in the federal school-bus column, because the vehicle was a school-bus body.

Do the subtraction and the scariest number in the report falls apart. Twelve of 17 inside-bus deaths came from those two crashes. Take them out and 2024 drops to five, second only to the pandemic year 2020, when schools were closed and the count was three, and not the highest by any measure. Eight of the 17 were adult farmworkers, which leaves three confirmed children among the dead inside school vehicles all year, plus one passenger whose age the federal table does not break out.[1] A headline claiming 2024 killed the most kids inside school buses would be wrong by a factor of four, and the federal table would still back it up, because the table counts bodies by vehicle, not by who was riding to school.

None of that makes the buses safer, because a system whose annual toll can triple on two bad mornings has no margin, and both mornings were impairment: a bus driver on prescription meds, a pickup driver high on marijuana oil, and a district running buses with no safety policies whatsoever. School buses are the safest vehicles on American roads because the system around them usually works. Rushville and Ocala are what the system looks like when it does not.

Meanwhile the actual killing zone never moved. Seventy percent of the decade's 1,069 deaths were occupants of other vehicles. Fifteen percent were pedestrians, and 77 of the 204 school-age children killed over the decade died on foot, nearly double the 39 who died riding.[1] Illegal passing is down, 38.4 million projected passes this school year against 45.2 million two years ago, and that is still more than 200,000 illegal passes every school day.[8] Some 20.3 million children ride a school bus every school day, and the bus remains the safest way for them to get there.[3] The curb at 7 a.m. is where children die. The bus is where they are safest.

The counterargument, at full strength: the concentration argument cuts both ways. Rarity is not the same as safety, and a death toll that two crashes can triple is a toll with no cushion. Both crashes were preventable in the most ordinary way: a driver who should not have been behind the wheel was behind the wheel, and in Rushville the district that employed her had never written down a single rule about it. The illegal-passing decline is real, but 38.4 million passes a year is not a number anyone should celebrate, and 63 people have been killed by illegal passing since 2000, 27 of them children on foot.[1] By that account the "decade high" is not a statistical artifact to explain away. It is a warning that the whole system rests on individual drivers and districts doing things right, and when they do not, the numbers spike. That reading has real weight. But it does not change the arithmetic: the children inside the bus were never the ones in the most danger, and 2024 did not change that.

What this does not prove: 2024 figures come from the FARS Annual Report File, not the final file, so late-reported deaths can revise totals slightly upward.[9] The "one passenger whose age the table does not break out" is arithmetic from published table totals, 12 passengers minus 3 Rushville children minus 8 Ocala farmworkers, not a separately sourced case. An independent analysis of the same data counts 13 of 17 from the two crashes by including the Rushville truck driver, who was not a bus occupant; this article uses 12, the bus-occupant count.[4] NHTSA's "school-transportation-related" definition includes crashes involving a school-bus-body vehicle in any capacity, including retired buses in non-school service, which is why the Ocala deaths sit in the school-vehicle column. Illegal-passing counts are survey extrapolations, a one-day count scaled to a 180-day school year, not observed totals.

What This Means for You

If your district's buses do not have seat belts, ask why. The Ocala bus had none, and 53 unrestrained adults rolled in a field. Compartmentalization protects children in frontal crashes, not rollovers. If you drive a school bus or hire drivers, Rushville is what happens when a district has no safety policies and a driver is impaired on prescription medication: demand written fitness-for-duty and medication-disclosure rules before the crash, not after it. If your child rides a bus, the deadliest moment is not the ride, it is the curb at 7 a.m.: teach them to wait for the driver's boarding signal and stay 10 feet back. And never pass a stopped school bus with its stop arm out. The bus is the safest vehicle on the road. The traffic around it is not.
