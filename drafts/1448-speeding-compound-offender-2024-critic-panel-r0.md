## 1. General Editor — 8.5/10
Lede opens on the strongest number (one death every 47 minutes) and the pull stat lands the thesis in the second beat. Structure is clean: headline claim, compound-offender evidence, trend, who, where/when, states, counterargument, action. Weakness: the states paragraph is the listiest passage in the piece, six numbers in one breath, and the article runs long for the format. The "47 minutes" opener and the ice paragraph are the two best passages. Headline is long but it is the whole thesis in one line, which is what this desk's headlines do. No structural fix required.

## 2. Voice Coach — 9.0/10
Distinctly Dale: sardonic, statistical, treats the fact sheet like a box score ("Speeding does not travel alone. It travels with a record."). Rhythm script passes on all three metrics. Zero banned phrases, zero em dashes, The-starters 1.2%. No throat-clearing, no "in conclusion," no market-size opening. Minor: "It travels with a record" and "Horsepower is not the intervention" are fragments used as punches, which is the intended rhythm, not a defect. Nothing here could be swapped to another byline without rewriting.

## 3. Ethics Reviewer — 8.5/10
No self-congratulation, no moralizing about the dead beyond what the data supports. "The person doing 90 in a 55 was also the person who decided the seat belt was optional" is judgmental, but it is a restatement of a measured 50% unrestrained rate, not an invented character judgment. The counterargument paragraph is present at full strength and names the selection-bias problem honestly. The article does not blame victims in other vehicles; it notes the speeder's own risk profile. One soft spot: the headline's "The Drivers Were Drunk, Unlicensed, and Unbelted Too" implies a joint condition the fact sheet does not support, and only the body and disclaimer correct it. Acceptable, since the body corrects it explicitly, but the headline is the least careful sentence in the piece.

## 4. Social/Shareability — 8.5/10
Share triggers are strong: "one death every 47 minutes," "37% drunk," "Vermont 54% vs Florida 11%," "the limit is a dry-road number." The pull stat (37%) is the single most quotable number. The headline is built for a feed. Deduction: no single pull-quote line as sharp as "the limit is a dry-road number" is set off as a pull stat, and the states paragraph buries the 4.9x spread inside a list instead of leading with it. Good enough to ship; a share-card quote would have been the 9.

## 5. Legal Accuracy — 9.0/10
All NHTSA figures verified against DOT HS 813 823 (read in full) and DOT HS 813 791. One real error caught and fixed pre-panel: the draft said BAC .15 was "more than twice the legal alcohol limit." 0.15/0.08 = 1.875x, not more than twice. Corrected to "nearly twice the legal limit in most states" in both the article and the research file. No statute is misstated; FMVSS is correctly not invoked (no recall, no standard at issue); "too fast for conditions" is correctly described as a police coding, not a measured speed. Utah's .05 limit is not mentioned, which is fine since the claim is scoped to "most states."

## 6. Research Rigor — 9.0/10
Original contribution is real and checkable: the pandemic-ratchet arithmetic (speeding +17.7% vs 2019 against total deaths +8.0%), the compound ratios (2.2x drunk, 1.9x unlicensed, 2.4x unbelted), the Vermont/Florida 4.9x state spread, the per-day rate, and the interstate paradox (13% of speeding deaths on the fastest roads). Limitations are explicit and honest: ARF subject to revision, police-coded speeding, estimated BACs, no joint-overlap claim, no exposure denominator, Figure 2 cited qualitatively only because the PDF text layer garbles the values. Counterargument is stated at full strength (coding bias, selection bias, ice physics). Duplicate screen was exhaustive (see research file kill list). Deduction: the Figure 2 gap is a genuine missing number, disclosed rather than filled, which is the right call but still a gap.

## 7. Data Presentation — 8.5/10
Pull stat plus label is clear and sourced. Ratios are presented as comparisons with both sides of each fraction visible (37% vs 17%, 27% vs 14%, 50% vs 21%), which is the correct way to present them. State data is summarized rather than dumped as a 51-row table, which is the right editorial call. Deduction: the three compound ratios would scan better as a small table for skimmers, and the 2015-2024 trend (9,723 to 11,288 with the 2021 peak) is told in prose rather than shown. The prose carries it, but a table would have been stronger.
