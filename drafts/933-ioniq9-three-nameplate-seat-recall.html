A Toddler Died Under a Power Seat in March. Today Hyundai Recalled a Third SUV for the Same Seat.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 18, 2026

On March 7, a two-year-old girl died in Ohio when the second-row power seat of a 2026 Hyundai Palisade folded onto her.[5] This week, Hyundai recalled about 5,000 more vehicles because their seats have the same failure: power-folding logic that cannot reliably tell a child from an empty seat.[1]

66,661
Three-row SUVs across three nameplates recalled for power-seat contact-detection failures in six months

Count them. In March, NHTSA campaign 26V160 pulled 61,093 Palisades and Palisade Hybrids in Limited and Calligraphy trims, the two-year-old's death having escalated an investigation Hyundai had already opened the previous November.[3] Then came the 2027 Kia Telluride Hybrid: campaign 26V173, 568 vehicles built between February and March 2026, with Nidec Mobility Korea named as the seat control unit supplier.[4] And now the 2026 Hyundai Ioniq 9, NHTSA campaign 26V588000, Limited and Calligraphy trims, about 5,000 cars with seats that "may not respond to contact" during fold-and-stow.[1][2] Three badges. Two brands. One failure mode, worded nearly identically in all three filings.

Which brings me to the engineering question that should embarrass everyone involved: why does a folding seat get to be this dumb? Your power window cannot. Under FMVSS 118, any power window permitted to close unsupervised must reverse direction when it meets an obstruction, before the squeezing force reaches 100 newtons.[7] That rule was written because regulators recognized, three decades ago, that a child between a closing window and its frame is an unacceptable outcome. A seat back folding shut on a motorized track, strong enough to trap a child, faces no equivalent federal rule. Contact detection on these seats is voluntary engineering. The Palisade's version failed, with a fatal consequence, and the fix at the time was a software patch Hyundai admitted was only temporary.[6]

The fair counterargument: Hyundai deserves credit for the speed of the Ioniq 9 catch. The company found this one through its own in-house testing, not through a body count, and the fix ships over the air via Bluelink before most owners finish reading the recall notice.[1] Sensing a small, moving child in a folding seat is also genuinely harder than sensing an obstruction in a rigid window track, and two minor injuries across 5,000 vehicles is not the Palisade's record. But "we shipped a software update" is not a substitute for a standard. FMVSS 118 exists because one automaker's voluntary anti-pinch window was not enough. Power seats just got their second recall cycle in six months. Voluntary is not working.

What the filings do not say matters too. Hyundai has not named the seat-unit supplier for the Ioniq 9, only the Telluride filing names Nidec, so the three recalls share a documented failure mode, not a documented common part. The Ioniq 9's population figure and injury count come from Hyundai's notice via press summaries; the underlying Part 573 PDF had not been crawled as of publication. And nobody publishes seat fold-motor force, so don't let anyone tell you this failure is "minor": the only number that matters is the one from Ohio.

If you own a 2026 Palisade or Palisade Hybrid in Limited or Calligraphy trim, a 2027 Telluride Hybrid, or a 2026 Ioniq 9 in Limited, Calligraphy, or Calligraphy Design: keep every body part, child, and pet out of the fold zone before you touch that button. Accept the over-the-air update the moment it arrives. Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls; Ioniq 9 VINs became searchable September 12.[2] And if you're shopping a three-row SUV, ask one question the brochure won't answer: does the power seat actually know when something is in the way? No federal standard requires it to.
