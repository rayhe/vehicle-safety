A Bolt Was Loose on the R2's High-Voltage Battery. The Torque Station Passed It Anyway.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 6, 2026

An under-torqued fastener on the high-voltage battery can cut the R2's drive power with no warning at all, no dash light, no sound, nothing. Rivian filed the recall on September 29, with NHTSA listing 14 affected vehicles built between May 18 and August 27, 2026, at the Normal, Illinois plant.[1] And the only reason anyone knows is that a factory worker looked at a bolt the machine had already approved.

13 days
Between NHTSA receiving the R2's first recall filing and this one. Launch ramp math is not kind.

This is campaign 26V625, Rivian's internal number FSAM-1888. The subject bolt is an M6x1.0x24.5, part number SC00036202-D, connecting hardware inside the high-voltage battery system. NHTSA's component classification for it is ELECTRICAL SYSTEM:PROPULSION SYSTEM:CRITICAL FASTENERS.[1] Rivian estimates that 100 percent of the 14-vehicle recall population has the defect, which is recall-speak for: every single one we could find is bad.[5]

Here is the part that should bother you more than the loose bolt. On September 17, a manufacturing operator found an under-torqued fastener on an R2 unit. That fastener had already passed the plant's automated torque station. The station's whole job is to verify torque values, and it waved a bad one through. Rivian has since reprogrammed the station to reject fasteners that miss the torque spec. What the NHTSA filing does not explain is how the station got misprogrammed in the first place.[3]

Stop and think about what that means for the 14 cars. A torque station is the boring, reliable, unsexy last line of defense in a modern plant, and it exists so humans don't have to squint at every bolt. When that last line of defense silently degrades, the only thing standing between a driver and a loose high-voltage connection at highway speed is one operator who happened to be paying attention on September 17. That operator deserves a bonus, and the station deserves an audit.

Compare this with the R2's first recall, filed just 13 days earlier. That one, FSAM-1866, covered roughly 98,828 R1 and R2 vehicles for a backup-camera notification that could obscure the rearview image, and Rivian fixed it with an over-the-air software update.[2] This second one cannot be downloaded. It requires a service visit, an inspection, and possibly replacement of damaged components, which is the difference that counts: a software recall is an inconvenience, but a hardware recall with no driver warning is the kind you set a calendar reminder for.[4]

0
Customer complaints, injuries, or field incidents reported for this defect. The factory caught it first.

Now the full-strength counterargument, because it matters: fourteen cars, zero complaints, zero injuries, zero field failures. Rivian caught this on the factory floor, reprogrammed the station within days, and self-reported to NHTSA before a single customer experienced anything. That is the recall system working exactly as designed, and treating a contained 14-unit voluntary recall like a scandal would be innumerate alarmism. The honest version of this story might be: the human in the loop earned their paycheck.

But here is why it still deserves your attention. There is no warning for this failure condition. Rivian's filing is explicit: none identified.[3] If one of those 14 fasteners loosens further on the road, the driver gets no heads-up before the power cuts. And every R2 on the road went through the same torque station. Rivian scoped this to 14 units built in a three-month window, but the uncomfortable question is how long the station was passing under-torqued fasteners before September 17, and what else it passed.

If you have a 2027 R2, here is what to do: check your VIN on NHTSA's recall site starting November 28, 2026, when affected VINs become searchable, or call Rivian customer service at 1-888-748-4261.[1] Owner notification letters go out the same day. There is no do-not-drive order and no park-outside advisory on this one, which tracks with a contained 14-unit population. Do not wait for a symptom to act, because the filing is clear that there is no symptom to wait for. If you are R2 shopping instead of R2 owning, ask the dealer for the vehicle's service history on FSAM-1888 before you sign anything. Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this.

Limitations

What this article did not prove: NHTSA's API was rate-limiting during research, so the cross-make count of fastener/torque recalls could not be independently tallied for this piece. The Part 573 defect report itself was not directly readable; the “100 percent of recall population” estimate and the bolt part number come via trade-press reporting on the filing, not the filing document. No R2 production-volume figures for May–August 2026 were available, so no defect rate against total output can be computed. FARS covers only fatal crashes, and this defect has produced zero reported incidents, so no fatality statistics apply. Rivian's recall population of 14 is the manufacturer's own scoping; an independent audit of the torque station's history has not been published.
