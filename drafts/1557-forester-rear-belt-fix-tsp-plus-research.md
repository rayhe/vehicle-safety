## Story in one line
Subaru changed the rear seat belts on the 2026 Forester and the car went from Acceptable to Good in the IIHS test that puts a dummy in the back seat, earning its 20th straight safety award, while the Honda CR-V, the Toyota RAV4, and the Audi Q3 still fail the same test, and the road data refuses to line up neatly behind any of it.

## Verified facts
1. **2026 Subaru Forester, IIHS updated moderate overlap front test, Test ID CEF2566.** Tested vehicle: 2026 Forester Premium 4-door 4wd; rating applies to 2026 models. Overall evaluation: **Good**. Structure and safety cage: Good. Driver injury measures: head/neck, chest, thigh/hip, leg/foot all Good. Driver restraints and dummy kinematics: Good. Rear passenger injury measures: head/neck, chest, thigh all Good. Rear passenger restraints and dummy kinematics: **Good**. Shoulder belt tension 4.5 kN. Source: IIHS ratings page for the 2026 Forester (primary, read directly).
2. **The fix was a belt revision, not a redesign.** IIHS: "The Subaru Forester was redesigned for the 2025 model year. Beginning with 2026 models, Subaru made changes to the rear seat belts to improve rear occupant safety in moderate overlap front crashes." The 2025 Forester earned Acceptable in the same test. One model year, one belt change, one grade step. Source: IIHS ratings page; West Palm Beach Subaru blog (IIHS ratings reviewed November 17, 2026).
3. **The award and the streak.** Subaru announced March 24, 2026 that the 2026 Forester, Ascent, and Outback earned Top Safety Pick+. The Forester's award is its 20th consecutive Top Safety Pick honor and its 10th Top Safety Pick+. Under 2026 criteria, a Good rating in the updated moderate overlap front test is required for either award (last year, Acceptable sufficed for the base award). EyeSight driver assist is standard on every trim. Forester starts at $29,995 MSRP. Source: Subaru of America press release, March 24, 2026.
4. **Same test, same segment, different grades.** IIHS lists Good for the 2026 Forester, Acceptable for the 2026 Toyota RAV4 Hybrid, and Poor for the 2026 Honda CR-V in the updated moderate overlap front test (West Palm Beach Subaru blog, ratings reviewed November 17, 2026). The RAV4 Plug-In Hybrid scored Marginal, with rear-seat restraints and dummy kinematics Poor (Jalopnik). The Audi Q3 scored Poor, with the rear lap belt moving from the pelvis to the abdomen (Jalopnik). The Chevrolet Trailblazer fell short of Good in the same test (AutoGuide). The 2026-2027 Nissan Kicks earned Good after Nissan revised the rear seat belts on vehicles built after June 2026; the 2025 Kicks had scored Poor (SlashGear, AutoGuide). The 2027 Volkswagen Jetta earned Good (Jalopnik).
5. **What the test is.** IIHS updated the moderate overlap front test in 2022 to add a second dummy behind the driver, sized like a small woman or a 12-year-old child. The crash is a 40 mph, 40-percent-offset frontal impact against a deformable barrier. The rear dummy measures whether the belt stays on the pelvis and off the neck, and whether the occupant submarines under the lap belt. A Good rating in this test is required for any IIHS Top Safety Pick award under 2026 criteria. Source: IIHS test description; #1392 research file.

## Original contribution: the lab grade and the road rate disagree, in both directions
- **Forester vs. CR-V:** 0.26 vs. 0.53. The car that passed the rear-seat test kills at **half the rate** of the car that failed it (0.53 / 0.26 = 2.04x). The lab grade and the road rate agree here, and the gap is not small.
- **RAV4 breaks the pattern:** 0.19, the lowest rate of the group, on a car that scored Acceptable (hybrid) and Marginal (plug-in hybrid) and won no award. If the rear-seat grade predicted road deaths, the RAV4 would sit near the top of the death table. It sits at the bottom. Something else, buyer demographics, driving exposure, or the rest of the safety package, is doing more work than the rear belt.
- **Kicks breaks it the other way:** 0.69, higher than the Forester (0.26) and the CR-V (0.53), on a car that now holds a Good grade and a Top Safety Pick award. The Kicks is the cheapest car in the group and the smallest; its buyers skew younger and its fleet is the smallest (393,750), so the rate carries more noise, but the direction is the point: a Good grade does not buy a low death rate.
- **Impairment does not explain the gaps.** Any-impaired shares across the group run 17.6% (CR-V) to 22.1% (Trailblazer), a 4.5-point spread, while the death rates span 0.14 to 2.83, a 20x spread. The Trailblazer's 2.83 is the outlier that dwarfs the rest of the table, and its impairment share (22.1%) is only modestly above the group.
- **The belt-fix club has two members.** Kicks (built after June 2026) and Forester (2026 models) both went from failing to Good on a rear seat belt revision alone. The fix is cheap, targeted, and proven twice in one year. The four models that still fail the test in the same round, RAV4, RAV4 PHEV, Q3, Trailblazer, have not done it yet. Toyota could, as SlashGear noted of the RAV4; the part exists, the test is public, and the fix is a belt.
- **The award got harder and the Forester kept winning.** Twenty consecutive years of Top Safety Pick or better, ten Top Safety Pick+ awards, under criteria that keep tightening: the 2026 rules made Good in this test mandatory for either award, and the Forester met the new bar in the same year it took effect. That is the longest documented safety-award streak in the compact SUV segment.

## What this article must NOT claim
- That the Forester's Good grade caused its 0.26 death rate, or that the CR-V's Poor grade caused its 0.53. The FARS window (2014-2023) covers earlier generations than the IIHS test vehicles (2026 models), and the lab test measures one crash configuration for one seating position.
- That the RAV4 is "safer" than the Forester because its rate is lower. Rates blend vehicle, driver, and exposure; the RAV4's buyer population differs from the Forester's, and the lab test isolates the vehicle in a way the road data cannot.
- That the CR-V is unsafe overall. It earns Good in small overlap front and side tests, Good structure, and Good driver protection in this very test. The failure is rear-occupant-specific, in one test.
- A specific Trailblazer letter grade. Contemporary reporting disagrees (AutoGuide: Marginal; Jalopnik: Poor). The article says only that it fell short of Good.
- That the Forester's side rating is IIHS-tested. One secondary source notes the Side 2.0 result was assigned from a manufacturer-conducted test; the article does not lean on the side rating.

## Strongest counterargument (state at full strength)
The whole premise, that a belt revision is the story, can be read as a lab artifact chasing a lab artifact. The updated moderate overlap test is four years old, the rear dummy is one body size, and the belt geometry it rewards may or may not map to real rear-seat injuries at real crash angles. The road data in this very article refuses to cooperate: the RAV4 misses the award and has the lowest death rate, the Kicks earns the award and has a higher rate than the CR-V that failed. If the test predicted survival, those two facts would be reversed. The Forester's streak is real, but it is a streak of passing a test whose criteria the test-maker keeps rewriting, and passing a rewritten test is not the same as saving a life. The honest version of this story is narrower than the headline: Subaru fixed a specific belt behavior in a specific crash, the fix is cheap and proven, and buyers who carry kids in the back seat should care about exactly that, no more and no less.

## Limitations
- FARS coverage is 2014-2023; the IIHS test vehicles are 2026 models (2023-27 CR-V, 2025-26 Forester). Generations differ. No causal claim is made from rate to grade.
- The Kicks fleet (393,750) is small; its 0.69 rate carries wider uncertainty than the CR-V's or RAV4's.
- The Trailblazer grade is reported inconsistently in secondary coverage; the article does not assign it a letter.
- The Forester's Side 2.0 rating was reportedly assigned from a manufacturer-conducted test; the article does not rely on it.
- NHTSA five-star ratings are not compared here; NHTSA does not run a rear-seat offset-frontal test of this kind, which is why the two systems can disagree.
- No recall or defect is alleged against any model in this story. The CR-V, RAV4, Q3, and Trailblazer are legal to sell and carry five-star NHTSA ratings in several cases; the IIHS grade is a stricter, narrower measure.

## What to do (actionable)
- Shopping for a compact SUV and you carry kids or adults in the back seat: check the IIHS updated moderate overlap front rating for the exact model year, not the overall award and not the NHTSA stars. The grade that matters for your rear passengers is one line on the IIHS ratings page.
- Buying a Nissan Kicks: only 2026-2027 models built after June 2026 carry the revised belts and the Good grade. Ask for the build date on the door jamb sticker; a 2025 Kicks and an early 2026 Kicks are different cars in this test.
- Buying a 2026 Forester: the Good grade applies to 2026 models only. A 2025 Forester is Acceptable in the same test, same platform, different belts.
- Owning a CR-V (2023-2027), RAV4, Q3, or Trailblazer: the Poor/Acceptable grade is rear-occupant-specific. If your back seat is usually occupied, belt fit matters more than it used to: a lap belt riding up onto the abdomen is the failure mode. Keep kids in the right restraint for their size, and check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because a future belt revision would arrive as a service action, not a recall.
