Three Cheap Crossovers Walk Into IIHS. Only One Walks Out With an Award.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 4, 2026

Only one of three popular subcompact crossovers earned an IIHS safety award for 2026. Hyundai's Kona swept all three crash tests with Good ratings and took home Top Safety Pick+, the institute's highest honor, while Toyota's Corolla Cross and Kia's Seltos each walked away with nothing.[1]

1 of 3
Subcompact crossovers in this comparison earning an IIHS award for 2026

Line up the scorecards and the pattern jumps out: Kona scored Good, Good, Good, while Corolla Cross scored Good, Acceptable, Good and Seltos scored Good, Acceptable, Marginal. Small overlap, the 40 mph test where a car clips a barrier with a quarter of its front end, was aced by all three. Moderate overlap, the test with a dummy riding behind the driver, dropped both losers to Acceptable, with IIHS marking down the Corolla Cross for rear passenger restraints and dummy kinetics. Side impact, the SUV-shaped rig T-boning the car at 37 mph, is where Kia fell off the table, with a Marginal rating, a driver pelvis injury risk IIHS called especially high, and a safety cage that did not hold up as expected.[1]

Crash prevention tells the same story with a second act. Kona scored Good on vehicle-to-vehicle and pedestrian prevention alike. Corolla Cross scored Good on pedestrian prevention and was not tested on vehicle-to-vehicle, which is missing data, not a failure, and this article will not pretend otherwise. Seltos scored Poor on vehicle-to-vehicle prevention, failing to avoid a passenger car and warning late on motorcycle targets, with headlights grading Acceptable or Poor depending on trim. Count the problem cells across both grids and Kia owns three of them, Toyota owns one soft cell and one blank, and Hyundai owns none.[1]

Context makes the shutout sting. For 2026, IIHS requires a Good in the updated moderate overlap test for either award, a bar raised from Acceptable for the base award a year earlier, so an Acceptable there is no longer a near-miss with a consolation prize attached; it is a shutout. IIHS handed its top award to just three mainstream subcompact crossovers this year, Kona, Honda HR-V, and Mazda CX-30, leaving every other nameplate in the class, including two of the three tested here, outside looking in.[1][2]

What this analysis does not prove: lab results are not body counts, and the 2026 Kona has not been on the road long enough to accumulate meaningful fatal-crash data, so today's gold star is a prediction, not a track record. IIHS itself warns that bigger, heavier vehicles generally protect better than smaller ones. And the Seltos verdict covers the outgoing generation only, since the redesigned 2027 Seltos is unevaluated, so anyone buying the new one is buying an untested car.[1]

Strongest counterargument: a skeptic could fairly call the whole exercise a lab artifact, since real crashes are not 40 mph barrier tests, weight still wins when a small car meets a big truck, and one of the three cars in this comparison is already replaced by a redesign the lab has not touched, which is fair and worth sitting with. But the artifact theory has a hole in it, because all three cars faced identical barriers at identical speeds, and identical tests do not produce different results unless the cars differ. When the test is the same and the score is not, the difference is the car.

Before you sign that lease, shop the award list first: Kona, HR-V, CX-30. If you buy a Corolla Cross anyway, check rear belt fit for your actual back-seat passengers, especially smaller adults and kids in boosters, since that is where Toyota lost its award. If you buy an outgoing Seltos, go in knowing about the side-impact and crash-prevention gaps, and do not let a dealer tell you the 2027 redesign's safety is proven, because IIHS has not tested it. Whatever you buy, run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before money changes hands, since no crash-test award says anything about recall status.[1][5]
