America's Druggiest Nameplates: An Oldsmobile, a Buick, and a BMW M5

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
October 3, 2026

I ran the numbers, then ran them again, and they did not get better. Across 307 nameplates and 490,736 drivers in a decade of federal fatal-crash toxicology, the highest drug-positive rate belongs to the Oldsmobile Alero at 18.4 percent, a car whose manufacturer has been dead for 22 years. Second: the Buick Park Avenue at 16.6 percent. Third, and this is why I checked twice: the BMW M5 at 15.6 percent, the only six-figure car anywhere near the top of the list. One in six M5 drivers in a fatal crash had drugs in their system.[1]

BMW M5 drivers in fatal crashes testing drug-positive, 3rd highest of 307 nameplates (fleet average: 8.7%)

Here is the rest of the top ten, because the company the M5 keeps is the story. Fourth through tenth: Buick Verano 13.1 percent, Ford Five Hundred 13.0, Suzuki Grand Vitara 12.7, Nissan NV200 12.7, Isuzu Rodeo 12.4, Mercedes-Benz GLK-Class 12.2, Jeep Commander 12.1. Read that list again. It is nine beaters, orphans, and workhorses, plus one 617-horsepower Bavarian super-sedan that costs more than most of the other nine combined. I fit a regression to price tier expecting a clean downward slope, rich car equals sober driver, and the data laughed.[1]

Zoom out from drugs to any impairment, alcohol included, and the shape gets clearer. Take the twelve most-impaired nameplates in America, every one with at least 100 toxicology reports: Park Avenue 31.7 percent, Alero 29.1, Chevrolet C/K pickup 28.0, Audi A3 27.1, Chevrolet Astro van 27.0, Five Hundred 26.4, Infiniti QX56 26.3, Corvette 26.2, BMW 7 Series 26.1, Cadillac CTS 25.9, Hyundai Tiburon 25.9, Infiniti FX35 25.9. Mean: 27.1 percent, split evenly between cheap old metal and luxury or performance cars. Now the middle, eighteen high-volume mainstream nameplates, Camry 19.2, Accord 20.0, Civic 20.4, Corolla 19.2, CR-V 17.6, RAV4 18.4, F-150 18.9, Silverado 20.6. Mean: 19.3 percent, which is what makes impairment U-shaped. It concentrates at the price extremes and bottoms out in the Camry middle, nearly eight points below the top. Your safest statistical company on the road is a midsize crossover driven by someone going to Costco.[1][2]

Two more findings fell out of the cross-tab. First: of all 307 nameplates, exactly two have drug-positive rates exceeding alcohol-positive rates, the Suzuki Grand Vitara (12.7 vs 11.4) and the Nissan NV200 (12.7 vs 11.8). Everywhere else, alcohol still wins, usually by a lot. Second, the polydrug overlap is enormous: eleven of the M5's seventeen drug-positive drivers were also alcohol-positive, and twenty-four of the Park Avenue's forty-three were too. Pure-drug-only cases are the minority in every nameplate I checked, which means the drug leaderboard is mostly measuring people who were already drinking and added something on top.[1]

Now the asterisk column, because this dataset has sharp edges. Drug-positive does not mean drug-impaired: THC is detectable for days or weeks after use, and FARS records presence, not impairment, so some of these drivers were sober with old metabolites. These percentages are conditional on being in a fatal crash, not population prevalence, and states test unevenly, with alcohol tested far more consistently than drugs. The M5 sample is 109 drivers, wide error bars, and testing bias is real: a young man who wraps an M5 around a pole at 2 a.m. gets the full panel, while a grandmother who dies in a Camry at noon may never get drug-tested at all. Part of my U-curve could be who gets tested, not who uses, stated at full strength. Even discounted, the gap between 27.1 and 19.3 is too large to be pure testing artifact, and the M5's company on the drug leaderboard remains genuinely strange.[1][3]

What to do: If you are shopping the bottom of the used market, know that $3,000 early-2000s GM sedans statistically keep the worst toxicology company in America; that is a data point, not destiny, but pair it with an IIHS rating check before handing the keys to a teenager. If you drive performance metal, the M5 finding is a reminder that capability is not a designated driver; the car's limits are higher than yours, and the toxicology desk does not grade on horsepower. Fleet managers running NV200-class vans should note the drug-over-alcohol inversion in their safety programs. And the policy ask: NHTSA tracks alcohol deaths with the rigor of a metronome and has no equivalent drug-death surveillance, a gap that makes every number in this article fuzzier than it should be, and standardized toxicology reporting would fix that. Until then, I ran the numbers twice, and the U-curve held.[3][4]
