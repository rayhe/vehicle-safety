By The Numbers

America's Record-Low Death Toll Has Nine Asterisks

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
September 3, 2026

I spent a week with NHTSA's April 2026 early estimate report, the one that announced 36,640 traffic deaths in 2025 and 15 consecutive quarters of decline.[1] Buried on page five is a table that nobody quoted in the press release coverage, breaking the national total into state-level projections. Thirty-nine states improved, two held flat, and nine went backward.

+26.5%
Hawaii's projected increase in traffic fatalities from 2024 to 2025, the largest spike in the nation during a year NHTSA called historic.

Hawaii jumped from 102 deaths to 129, and Wyoming climbed 15.9% from 107 to 124. New Mexico added 49 deaths for a 12% increase, pushing its fatality rate to 1.59 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled.[1] Kansas rose 8.3%, while Idaho, the fastest-growing state by population, added 6.7% and Colorado tacked on 26 deaths. Louisiana, Vermont, and New Hampshire rounded out the list with single-digit gains that still represent real people in real cars.

Add the columns across all nine and you get roughly 309 additional fatalities over their 2024 baselines. The other 41 states plus DC and Puerto Rico collectively dropped by about 2,923, meaning the national improvement required their gains to overwhelm nine states' losses by nearly ten to one.

What do the nine share? Five of them post highway speed limits of 75 or 80 mph, where kinetic energy at impact is roughly double what it would be at 55.[2] Three rank among the fastest-growing states in their region, adding VMT before road infrastructure catches up.[3] One of them, New Hampshire, is the only state in America with no adult seat belt law, and its fatality rate ticked up from 0.97 to 0.99 while the national rate dropped to 1.10.

Contrast this with the biggest improvers: DC plunged 46.8%, Iowa dropped 27%, Minnesota fell 22%, and Maryland declined 16.4%.[1] Each has either a major urban core benefiting from lower speeds and higher enforcement density or recently overhauled impaired-driving laws. The pattern maps onto a familiar axis: 20% of the population lives in rural areas but accounts for 41% of all traffic fatalities.[4]

Limitations, honestly stated: These state estimates are statistical projections, not final FARS counts, and will shift when ARF data arrives in early 2027. Small states amplify noise, because Hawaii's entire increase is 27 deaths that three multi-fatality crashes could produce in a single month, and Wyoming's spike could be explained entirely by one bad winter weekend on I-80 rather than any systemic trend.

Strongest counterargument: If small-number volatility explains the increases, it also explains the decreases. Rhode Island dropped 28.8%, from 52 to 37, a swing of 15 deaths that nobody would call statistically robust in a clinical trial. You cannot celebrate 39 states improving and dismiss nine states worsening as noise, because either all state-level single-year comparisons are meaningful or none of them are. NHTSA chose to publish them, so I chose to read all of them, not just the ones that supported the headline.
