The Vistiq’s Third-Row Seat Folds Onto Whatever’s in the Way. GM Recalled Every One Ever Built.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
November 27, 2026

Let’s talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds after a power-folding seatback meets resistance, because in the Cadillac Vistiq, the answer is: nothing. A power-folding seatback is a machine that applies force to a space a child can occupy, and its entire safety case is one behavior — it must reverse when it meets an obstruction. Cadillac’s newest three-row electric SUV shipped without that behavior, and GM recalled all 14,540 Vistiqs ever built, every 2026 and every 2027, because the third-row seatback folds onto whatever is in the way and stays there.[1]

14,540
Vistiqs recalled under campaign 26V394: 100 percent of the nameplate’s production to date.

One press does it. A single press-and-release of the “down” button in the cargo area or on the pillar beside the seat commands the third-row seatback to power-fold into its stowed position, and nobody has to hold the button while the seat travels, which means nobody is watching the seat travel either. GM’s own bulletin states the failure plainly: the seatback “stops and does not automatically reverse if it encounters an obstruction in the seat,” and smaller occupants could become trapped under it.[1] Engineers tested the seat with a box weighing 33 to 40 pounds, roughly the weight of a three-year-old, ran the fold cycle, and watched the seatback fold, stop, and hold the box so it could not be removed without manually reversing the seat.[3] That is not an edge case. That is the designed behavior, demonstrated with a box.

Credit where it is due: once GM found it, the company moved fast. Its Safety Field Action Decision Authority approved the recall on June 11, 2026, NHTSA campaign 26V394 was filed June 18, and dealer inventory went on stop-delivery the same day, with an order that is unusually blunt for a recall bulletin: until replacement parts are available, vehicles in dealer inventory “MUST have the third-row folding feature disabled,” not delivered, not traded, not used for demonstration.[1] Owner letters went out August 6, and the remedy for cars already sold is a free software reprogram of the folding seat module.[2] Disabling a feature on unsold cars is an admission that the software fix could not be trusted to arrive before more cars were sold with a working trap, and that admission is the most honest sentence in the whole file.

Five months after filing, about 35 percent of the recalled Vistiqs have had the fix, according to the manufacturer’s latest quarterly report, which leaves roughly two in three still able to fold onto a child.[4] No injuries and no deaths have been linked to the defect, and that matters, but it is also the reason the repair rate is what it is: nobody rushes to a dealer for a seat feature they may never use. Meanwhile the Vistiq is collecting recalls the way a new nameplate collects press clips. It also appears in GM’s 67,380-vehicle EV brake-software recall, campaign 26V717, and in a FMVSS 225 child-restraint anchorage noncompliance, campaign 25V819, over a radio that was not set to download the electronic owner’s manual during production.[5] Three recalls in year one, on a car whose entire reason for existing is carrying children safely in three rows, and one of the three is about the seats that hold those children.

Fairness demands the other side, stated at full strength. This recall worked the way recalls are supposed to work: GM caught the defect in its own evaluation, approved the fix in a week, stopped deliveries the same day, disabled the feature on unsold cars, and mailed owners within seven weeks, all before anyone was hurt. A 35 percent repair rate five months in is unremarkable for a recall with no crash attached, and the Vistiq’s other two recalls are a software brake update and a manual-download glitch, not a pattern of the car falling apart. All of that is true, and none of it changes the engineering fact at the center of the file, which is that a luxury family SUV shipped with a seat that applies force to a child-sized space and no reversal when it meets resistance, and the fix for the cars already sold is a dealer visit most owners have not made.

What is not proven here deserves saying plainly. FARS data has nothing to say about this defect, because nobody has crashed over it; every number in this article is a recall-population number, not a fatality number. GM’s 33-to-40-pound box test is reported through a secondary account of the company’s evaluation, not quoted from the Part 573 report itself, though the NHTSA bulletin confirms the underlying failure mode in GM’s own words. And the 35 percent repair figure is a quarterly snapshot that will move as more cars are serviced, not a live count.

What do you do with this? If you own a 2026 or 2027 Vistiq, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls for campaign 26V394; the fix is a free software reprogram, and until it is done, do not let a child sit in or climb through the third row while anyone is folding the seats, and do not use the cargo-area or pillar fold button with a child anywhere near the third row. If you are shopping for a used Vistiq, the recall covers every one built, roughly two in three have not had the fix, and only a VIN lookup settles whether the car in front of you is one of them. If you are shopping any three-row SUV with power-folding seats, test the anti-pinch yourself: put a firm object on the seat, press fold, and watch whether the seatback reverses on contact. A seat that stops and holds is a seat that can trap a child, and the Vistiq proves that a six-figure luxury badge is no guarantee the test was ever run.[2]
