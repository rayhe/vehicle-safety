NHTSA Asked Tesla's Robotaxis to Slow Down for a School Zone. Four Videos Say They Didn't.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
August 29, 2027

On Friday, federal regulators did something they had never done to a driverless car: they opened a file on it for breaking a traffic law without crashing into anything. NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation opened Defect Petition DP26010 on October 9, 2026, after a petitioner alleged that Tesla's robotaxis exceed posted speed limits in active school zones while the Automated Driving System is engaged.[1] The evidence file is four incidents and three social-media videos. Zero crashes. Zero injuries. And a subject population of an estimated 1,000 model-year 2026 Tesla Model Y and Cybercab robotaxis, the first hard federal number on a fleet Tesla has never publicly quantified.[2]

Let's talk about what has to happen in the first 150 milliseconds of a school zone approach, because this is the part that genuinely annoys me. A school zone speed limit is conditional. It exists only during posted hours, only on school days, and it is announced by some combination of a static sign, a flashing amber beacon, and paint on the asphalt. A camera-first driving stack has to detect the beacon (is it flashing or just lit?), read the sign (which of the three panels applies?), know what day it is, know what time it is, and fuse all of that into a speed decision before the crosswalk. That is four perception problems stacked on a scheduling problem, and the machine has to solve all five correctly on every single approach or it sails through a 20-mph zone at 35. Humans do this with a glance and a calendar in their heads. The robot has to do it with code, and the code flinched.

Now the irony, because the data demands it: humans are catastrophically worse at this. A national survey of 16,714 vehicles across 63 school zones found 65% of drivers exceeding the posted limit, a quarter of them by 10 mph or more.[3] A Florida survey of 25,000 vehicles found over 70% of drivers speeding 20+ mph over the limit during morning drop-off and afternoon pickup.[4] A 2026 University of Nebraska study of 378,506 vehicles across 18 sites found 85th-percentile speeds routinely over the posted limit, with the worst compliance exactly where the limit drops the most.[5] The entire pitch for full self-driving has been that machines don't get impatient, don't get distracted, don't do 45 past the elementary school because they're running late. DP26010 says the machine does the human thing. Four videos. Same sin.

This is also the third federal action against Tesla's driverless fleet in about five weeks, and the pattern is what makes it a story rather than a file number:

What the file doesn't tell you matters as much as what it does. NHTSA's resume names no cities, no speeds, no dates, and doesn't say which videos were submitted.[1] We don't know whether the alleged speeding was 1 mph over or 20 over, or whether the zones were actually active when filmed. A defect petition is the first rung of NHTSA's ladder, not a Preliminary Evaluation and not a finding; the agency explicitly says it opened the file "to evaluate the allegations and determine whether to grant or deny the petition." The petitioner's identity is unknown, which means three cherry-picked videos of one bad software build can't be ruled out. And the 120-day clock can end in a denial, at which point this whole episode becomes a footnote about a robot that drove slightly too fast past a school on a Tuesday.

The strongest case for the defense deserves its full weight. School-transportation crashes kill an average of 107 people a year in the US, and virtually every one is human-caused; 70% of the dead are occupants of other vehicles, 15% are pedestrians.[6] Against that baseline, four videos of a robot going a few mph over, with zero crashes across an estimated 1,000 vehicles, might make Tesla's fleet the most school-zone-compliant driver on the road. Punishing the robot for a human-normal sin while human drivers keep killing kids would be regulatory theater. If the zones in those videos weren't active, there is no violation at all, just a car driving the posted limit past an empty school.

What you can do: if you live near Austin, Dallas, or Houston robotaxi service areas, the date that matters is not today but January 21, 2027, when the 120-day clock runs out and this becomes a granted investigation or a dismissal. If you're a parent, the human baseline is the real story, so walk your kid's drop-off route and watch the other parents, because 65 to 70% of them are speeding in the zone. And if you build or buy automated driving systems, treat this as notice: conditional speed limits, the time-of-day and school-day kind, are now a federal compliance surface. Beacon detection and map-data freshness belong in every safety case, because the most deterministic environment on public roads just became the hardest test.
