## Verified facts (3+ primary/primary-adjacent sources)
1. **Headline total — NHTSA early estimate (Apr 1, 2026):** an estimated 36,640 people died in motor-vehicle crashes in 2025, a 6.7% decrease from 39,254 in 2024. Fourth straight annual decline; Q4 2025 was the 15th consecutive quarterly decline (since Q2 2022). Fifth-largest annual percentage decrease in FARS recorded history. VMT rose ~0.9% (+29.8B miles) while deaths fell. Rate fell to 1.10 per 100M VMT (11.0 per billion), down from 1.19 in 2024, the second-lowest rate on record behind 1.08 in 2014. Fatalities fell in 39 states plus DC and Puerto Rico.
2. **Category breakdown — NHTSA July 2026 report:** speeding-related fatalities -11%; passenger-vehicle rollover -11%; single-vehicle -7%; roadway departure -9%; motorcyclist -8%; pedestrian -8%; large-truck-involved -7%; passenger-vehicle occupant -10%; urban interstates -10%; urban arterials -12%; nighttime -8%; weekend -7%; out-of-state travel -11%; newer vehicles (under 10 years old) -12%; unrestrained occupants -13%; ejected occupants -13%; drivers -5%, passengers -12%. The one riser named: cyclist fatalities +4% in 2025.
3. **Five-year divergence — TRIP (Sep 25, 2026), "Addressing America's Traffic Safety Crisis":** pedestrians + bicyclists = 21% of all 2025 traffic fatalities. 2020 to 2025: bicyclist deaths +29% (891 to 1,148); pedestrian deaths +5% (6,236 to 6,529); combined ped+bike +8%. Motorcyclists = 16% of all 2025 traffic deaths on under 1% of VMT. Work-zone crashes killed 4,481 people 2020-2024. Total societal harm from fatal and serious crashes in 2025: $1.75 trillion ($435B economic + $1.32T quality-of-life), per NHTSA's crash-cost methodology. 2025 total (36,640) is still 3% above 2015 (35,484).
4. **State spread (NHTSA 2025 data via Antiplanner summary):** fatality rates ranged from 4.7 per billion VMT in Rhode Island to 15.9 in New Mexico, a 3.4x gap. Other high-rate states: Mississippi 15.2, South Carolina 15.1, Arizona 15.0, Arkansas 14.3, Montana 14.3, West Virginia 14.6. Low-rate states include Massachusetts 5.7, Minnesota 6.2, New York 8.3, Maryland 8.4, North Dakota 8.4, Utah 8.5, Connecticut 8.6, Indiana 9.1, Wisconsin 8.0. Rural-road share tracks the gap: higher-rate states are more rural.
5. **Arithmetic the article may use (computed, flagged as computed):** 36,640 deaths is ~100 per day (100.4). The 2024-to-2025 drop is 2,614 fewer deaths. NM rate / RI rate = 15.9 / 4.7 = 3.38x. Cyclist share of 2025 deaths is not stated in sources; do NOT claim a precise cyclist count for 2025, only the +4% change and the 2020-2025 series (891 to 1,148).

## Kill-test notes / what NOT to claim
- Do NOT use GHSA's absolute pedestrian estimate (6,732 for 2025) alongside TRIP's 6,529: the two series disagree and mixing them manufactures a contradiction. This article uses the NHTSA/TRIP series only.
- Do NOT use the Richman Law table (claims pedestrian deaths rose 1.4% in 2025 to 7,251): it contradicts NHTSA's -8% and is excluded as an unreliable secondary compilation.
- 2025 figures are NHTSA early estimates, not final FARS counts; say so once, plainly.
- Cyclist +4% is a year-over-year change, not a count; the article gives no 2025 cyclist death count because no verified count was found in these sources.
- The "safest year in a decade" framing rests on the RATE (second-lowest ever, lowest since 2014), not the count: 2025's count (36,640) exceeds every year 2009-2015. Say both.

## Actionable insights (for article)
1. If you ride a bike: you are the one group the safety gains skipped. Separated lanes, daylight-visible clothing, and routes that avoid arterials are not lifestyle tips; arterials fell 12% for everyone else and still kill cyclists at rising rates. The data says the road got safer for people inside cars, not for people on bikes.
2. If you drive in a high-rate state (NM, MS, SC, AZ): your per-mile risk is over 3x Rhode Island's. Rural roads, higher speeds, longer EMS response. Slow down on rural two-lanes; that is where the state gap lives.
3. If you buy used: newer vehicles (under 10 years old) saw fatalities fall 12%, nearly double the national rate of improvement. A 10-year-old car is not just older; it is missing the safety gains.
4. Buckle up, every seat: unrestrained deaths fell 13% and ejected deaths fell 13%, the two largest drops in the breakdown. The belt is still the cheapest safety device ever sold.

## Limitations
- 2025 totals are early estimates; final FARS counts may revise them.
- Category percentages come from a secondary summary of NHTSA's July 2026 report; the underlying NHTSA research note was not read directly this run.
- No causal attribution is proven for any single decline (enforcement, vehicle tech, road design, post-crash care all plausibly contribute); the article must not credit one cause.
- Cyclist exposure (miles ridden) is not in these sources, so the +4% cannot be split into more riders vs more risk per rider. Say so.
