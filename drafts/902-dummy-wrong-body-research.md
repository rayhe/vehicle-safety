# Research — #902: The Newest Crash Test Dummy Is Still a 1970s Body

## Angle (1-2 sentences)
After 15 years of development, NHTSA finally has an advanced female crash test dummy — and it still represents a 4'11", 108-pound woman. Meanwhile NHTSA's own new injury study says BMI of 30+ means significantly higher injury risk in frontal crashes, and the average American woman is 5'3.5", 172 lbs (BMI 30.0). The new dummy was built for a body almost nobody has; nobody built one for the body at highest risk.

## Original contribution (novel calculation)
BMI cross-comparison nobody in the coverage did:
- THOR-05F / HIII-05F: 108 lbs, 4'11" = BMI ~21.8 (healthy).
- Average American woman (Smithsonian): 172 lbs, 5'3.5" = BMI ~30.0 — exactly the CDC obesity threshold, the category NHTSA's own new study flags for significantly higher injury risk.
- 50th-percentile male dummy: 5'9"/171 lbs per Smithsonian (1970s man) vs modern average man 5'9"/193 lbs per Freep — dummy is ~30 lbs too light, same structural problem for men.
- Zero: the number of federally regulated dummies at BMI 30+. The highest-risk body per the agency's own 2026 data is untestable in the federal regime.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
NHTSA's own injury study found the male-female fatality gap already collapsed from 18% to 2.9% for 2015-2020 vehicles — modern airbags, belt pretensioners, and load limiters largely closed the gender gap without any new dummy. The IIHS argues resources are better spent on virtual human-body models that can be scaled to any body shape cheaply, rather than another two decades of physical dummy development. Automakers note the THOR family is genuinely still developmental — 500+ validation tests were needed just to fix durability issues, and rushing an immature test device into FMVSS could lock in bad injury criteria. A heavier dummy is not automatically a better dummy: biofidelity is about how the dummy's flesh and bones respond, not just its scale weight.

## Limitations
- FARS is fatal-only and does not record occupant BMI, so we cannot directly measure the BMI-fatality link in our own data; the BMI-injury-risk claim comes from NHTSA's NASS-CDS/CISS study, not our calculation.
- Injury-risk elevation for BMI>=30 is moderate-to-severe/fatal injury, not deaths alone; the "significantly higher" phrasing is NHTSA's.
- The 2.9% residual gender gap in 2015-20 vehicles suggests much of the injury gap is restraint-geometry/vehicle-age driven, not dummy-driven.
- We did not verify what share of 2014-2023 FARS female deaths were in vehicles lacking modern restraints.

## Actionable insight
If you're a larger occupant: the lap belt must sit flat and low across the hips, never the belly — submarining (sliding under the belt) is the specific obese-occupant failure mode NHTSA's biomechanics work flags, and aftermarket belt adjusters or padding can make restraint geometry worse, not better. Shop 2020+ model years where the gender injury gap has measurably narrowed, and check IIHS's moderate overlap and side ratings, which are the tests most sensitive to female-body injury risk. And the policy lever: NHTSA will propose a rule adding THOR-05F to 49 CFR part 572 after the 500-test validation round — public comment on that rulemaking is the only input mechanism that exists.
