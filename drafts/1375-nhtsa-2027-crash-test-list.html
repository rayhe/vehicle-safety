NHTSA Will Crash-Test 23 Cars for 2027. Only Three Get All Three Tests.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 3, 2026

Before you trust a window sticker, read the schedule behind it. On October 15, 2026, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration named the vehicles it will test for model year 2027: 23 for crash testing, 18 for pedestrian protection, and 10 for driver assistance.[1] Only three models appear on all three lists; every other name gets a partial exam, and none of them has a single new result yet.

Models on all three 2027 test lists: Tesla Model 3, Volkswagen Atlas, Kia Telluride

Start with the crash list, because that is the test shoppers know. NHTSA will run frontal, side, and rollover evaluations on the BMW X5, Chevrolet Silverado 1500, Tahoe, and Trailblazer, Dodge Charger, Honda Civic Hatchback Type R, Hyundai Elantra and Tucson, Infiniti QX65, Jeep Cherokee, Kia EV3, Niro, Seltos, and Telluride, Lexus NX 350h, Nissan Frontier Crew Cab, Pathfinder, Rogue, and Rogue e-Power, the Slate pickup, Tesla Model 3, Toyota Sienna Hybrid, and Volkswagen Atlas.[2] One name stands out: the Slate pickup, from a maker most shoppers have never seen, gets the full federal battery alongside the Silverado.

Cross-checking the three published lists leaves exactly three models on all of them: the Tesla Model 3, the Volkswagen Atlas, and the Kia Telluride.[2] Kia EV3 and Nissan Frontier Crew Cab make crash and pedestrian but skip driver assistance. BMW X5, Silverado 1500, Elantra, Cherokee, NX 350h, and Sienna Hybrid make crash and driver assistance but skip the pedestrian check. A car can ace the tests it was given while never facing the one that measures what it does to a person outside it.

Pedestrian protection is not stars: NHTSA's newest assessment grades three impacts: headform strikes for children and adults on the hood and windshield area, upper-leg impacts on the hood's leading edge, and lower-limb impacts on the bumper.[2] Results show up as a yellow pass, fail, pending, or not-submitted box beside the star ratings. Eighteen vehicles face that check, including the Toyota Tundra and the gas-powered Ford Explorer, the only American-branded vehicle on the list.[3] A five-star occupant score and a pedestrian fail can sit on the same page.

Driver assistance covers 10 vehicles across five named systems: automatic emergency braking, pedestrian automatic emergency braking, lane keeping assist, blind spot warning, and blind spot intervention.[1] Rivian R2 joins that list. Silverado 1500, all-new for 2027, is the only GM vehicle on it, and the Trailblazer arrives refreshed with new safety features.[3] Compare those systems by name, not by marketing term. A model can carry one and lack another, and the brochure will not volunteer the difference.

Two traps could cost a buyer real money. First, selection is not a rating. NHTSA says its ratings cover 87% of new vehicles, a figure that only works if ratings from earlier testing of unchanged models carry over, and the agency has set no publication date for any 2027 result, so nothing here has new stars today.[1] Second, absence from a list is not a verdict. No Ford F-150, Toyota Camry, Honda Accord, Toyota Corolla, Honda CR-V, or Toyota Tacoma appears on the 2027 crash list, checked against all 23 entries, but that means not selected for fresh testing, not unrated.[2] A dealer waving an older five-star sticker at a buyer of a redesigned car is selling last year's test as this year's car. Ask which model year the stars belong to.

For scale, Silverados accounted for 9,591 deaths in FARS data from 2014 through 2023, at 1.25 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, a figure describing past generations, not the redesigned 2027 truck about to be tested.[4] That distinction is precisely why the test exists: a new generation earns its own numbers.

What to do, concretely. Search the exact model and model year at NHTSA.gov/ratings before negotiating, because ratings differ by trim and the badge on the fender is not enough.[2] If the model sits on the 2027 pedestrian list, ask the dealer for its pedestrian status specifically, since that box will not appear on older printed materials. Check the window sticker, where crash-test stars are required to appear, and treat any rating without a model year attached as an unanswered question. NHTSA Administrator Jonathan Morrison put the pitch simply: "For more than 30 years, NHTSA's 5-Star Safety Ratings have helped families choose the vehicle that's right for them."[1]

What this analysis does not prove. No 2027 result exists yet for any listed vehicle, so nothing here crowns or condemns a single car. September 2026 saw a separate action, the inaugural pedestrian-protection results in which 102 models met the criteria; that round is not this October selection list. Full-list names come from NHTSA's release as republished with the complete lists, since the agency's press page did not render its tables in the copy reviewed for this article.

Strongest counterargument. A testing schedule is bureaucracy, not a safety finding, and a rotating selection is efficient rather than negligent: unchanged models keep earlier ratings precisely so the agency can spend its crash sleds on redesigns like the 2027 Silverado. On that view, the thesis narrows. This list matters not because it judges any car, but because shoppers mistake a schedule for a scorecard and old stars for new ones.
