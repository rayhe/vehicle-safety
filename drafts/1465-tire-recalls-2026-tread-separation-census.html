12,643 Tires Were Recalled in 2026. Every Single One Was Coming Apart.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
November 10, 2026

Six tire recalls reached NHTSA's files in 2026, covering 12,643 tires from six brands. All told, Kumho accounted for 11,169 of them, 88 percent of the year's recalled rubber.[1]

Toyo recalled 567 Open Country A/T IIIs,[4] VeeRubber 144 Taiga ATs,[2] Goodyear 69 Eagle F1 Supersports in the US,[7] and Otani 84 WM1000s,[3] while Yokohama recalled 610 Geolandar M/T G003s last week.[5] Six brands, one defect: tread, belt, or sidewall separation, the tire coming apart at speed.

Two of the six recalls exist only because a lab bought a tire. NHTSA's contracted laboratory tested two Kumho Solus TA11s against the federal endurance standard, and both failed.[1] Kumho told regulators the precise reason the two tires failed "remains uncertain," and it knew of no accidents or injuries.[1] VeeRubber's recall started the same way: a random market sample failed the endurance test in January, and NHTSA emailed the company on January 14.[2] Nobody crashed, and nobody complained before the lab results came back. A machine in a lab found the defect first. That is the compliance system working as designed: if the lab had not bought those tires, they would still be on the road.

A recall only works if the owner finds out. Vehicle recalls find owners through registrations and VINs. Tire recalls have neither. Toyo's October 2025 recall covered 36,919 tires, and by April 2026 only about 4,000 had been replaced, roughly 11 percent.[8] Another 5,106 owners were unreachable in a single quarter, because no system can find a tire.[8] Only the owner can, by reading the DOT code molded into the sidewall. Those last four digits are the birth date: week, then year. A code ending in 2925 was born in week 29 of 2025. Match that code against the recall list at nhtsa.gov or the tire industry's database, and check the spare too, because Toyo's own recall notice says so.[4] Your spare is the tire nobody checks, and it is usually the oldest tire on the car.

None of the six recalls reports a crash, an injury, or a death. A skeptic can fairly call this a quality-control footnote. Counts are tiny, and that is the good news. That failure mode has not changed in 25 years; what changed is the catching. Goodyear's G159 motorhome tires came apart the same way, 173,237 of them.[9] This year's census is 7 percent of that one recall. FARS cannot see this failure mode: a tire failure shows up as a vehicle crash, so no death toll can be attached to these recalls. Go read yours: it takes thirty seconds, and it is the only inspection in the entire recall system that you have to do yourself.
