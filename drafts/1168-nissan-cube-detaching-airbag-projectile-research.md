## Kill test
- Genuinely newsworthy? YES. NHTSA closed its defect investigation on September 11, 2026 (33 days ago) after a single real-world crash in which the driver's airbag module detached from the steering wheel, struck the driver, and was found resting in the back seat. An airbag that converts itself into a projectile aimed at your chest is the single darkest possible failure mode of the single most universal safety device in the car.
- Novel angle? YES, three of them: (1) This is Takata-adjacent but NOT the Takata story — Nissan explicitly ruled out propellant degradation (the root cause behind the largest recall in automotive history) and instead found a bad weld at the inflator base. The inflator didn't explode with shrapnel; the whole module just... let go. (2) The zombie-supplier angle: Takata is bankrupt; its assets went to Key Safety Systems (rebranded Joyson Safety Systems), but "TK" legal entities kept operating for years to manage recall logistics — and TK Services Inc. of Romeo, Michigan built the exact inflator in this recall. Takata is striking from beyond the corporate grave. (3) The "exactly one" math: Nissan estimates 0.002% of the 47,928 recalled vehicles are affected. Multiply it out: Nissan recalled nearly 48,000 sixteen-year-old cars to find approximately ONE defective airbag.
- Not covered before? Grep across stories/ and all *-research.md files: zero Cube coverage. The closest recall-investigation pieces (#1149 Sierra grille, #1148 ProMaster steering, #1071 VW steering bolt) cover different vehicles and different failure physics. Untouched.

## Core facts

- **What:** NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation (ODI) closed Preliminary Evaluation **PE25005** on/around September 11, 2026, based on Nissan's recall action.
- **Investigation opened:** June 13, 2025 — after ODI was alerted to a single-vehicle crash involving a 2009 Nissan Cube. The driver's airbag module detached completely from the steering wheel during deployment, struck the driver, and hurtled into the back seat, where investigators found it. The driver was injured. (carscoops, June 2025; carcomplaints PE25005 summary)
- **Root cause (Nissan's finding):** Improper body weld seal at the base of the Takata PSDI-X driver airbag inflator (Nissan P/N 98560-7991C), made by **TK Services Inc., Romeo, Michigan**, within one specific lot. Nissan and Takata (now New Leaf LLC) analyzed the rupture and ruled out the propellant-degradation root cause of the big Takata recall — this is a welding defect, not a chemistry defect.
- **Recall:** **26V230** (Nissan internal: R26A1), filed April 9, 2026. Population: **47,928** vehicles — 2009–2010 Nissan Cube, built **October 10, 2008 – September 25, 2010**. Covers the 28,388 2009 MY vehicles from the investigation's opening resume, expanded to 2010 MY.
- **Remedy:** Dealers inspect the inflator serial number; if the inflator is from the affected lot, it is removed and replaced **with an inflator from a different manufacturer**, free of charge. VINs searchable on nhtsa.gov from April 23, 2026. Owner notification letters mailed May 27, 2026. Nissan customer service: 800-647-7261.
- **Defect rate estimate:** Nissan estimates **0.002%** of the recalled population is affected (per Jalopnik, citing recall docs). 47,928 × 0.00002 = 0.96 — about **one vehicle**.
- **The car:** Z12-series Cube, sold in the US for model years 2009–2014, boxy upright design, Nissan Versa drivetrain. Discontinued here over a decade ago; a surprising number survive as cheap used cars (Nissan's CPO program has even been putting used Cubes back on lots, per carscoops).
- **Scale context:** The Cube is a tiny footprint, but it shares a platform with the Versa — no other Nissan or INFINITI vehicles carry this inflator (USA Today, citing NHTSA notice).

## Original contribution for this article

1. **The "exactly one" calculation:** 47,928 recalled vehicles × Nissan's 0.002% estimate = 0.96 defective airbags. Nissan is running a full federal recall campaign — Part 573 filing, serial-number inspection protocol, owner mailings, dealer training — to remove approximately one airbag. This is the purest illustration of how recall economics works: lot-level traceability is so poor that the only safe move is to recall the entire haystack. The headline stat writes itself and the math is checkable.
2. **Takata's afterlife:** Takata Kabushiki Gaisha is dead — bankrupt, assets bought by Key Safety Systems, rebranded Joyson Safety Systems. But TK Services Inc. of Romeo, Michigan kept making inflators, and its PSDI-X unit is the one with the bad weld. This is not "the Takata recall came back"; it is a distinct manufacturing defect from a zombie supplier entity that outlived its parent. The two stories keep getting conflated (including, at first, by NHTSA's own probe coverage); separating them is a genuine clarifying contribution.
3. **The failure-mode taxonomy angle:** Every prior airbag defect is either "it didn't deploy" or "it exploded with shrapnel." This is a third category: the airbag deployed correctly, the cushion and inflator stayed attached to the housing — and then the entire module detached from the steering wheel and became a heavy projectile traveling through the cabin at deployment speed. Nobody catalogs this failure mode because it's nearly unprecedented. The article introduces the taxonomy.
4. **Strongest counterargument (stated at full strength):** At 0.002%, the expected defect count is one car in 47,928, and the real-world evidence is a single crash. One could argue this is recall theater — a lot-tracking failure being solved with a sledgehammer, imposing inspection costs on 48,000 owners and dealer time on a 16-year-old car for a risk that has produced one injury. But: the precautionary principle was made for exactly this case. The defect turns the driver's primary safety device into a projectile aimed at their torso. A serial-number inspection is a 20-minute dealer visit and the replacement is free. The downside of over-recall is inconvenience; the downside of under-recall is the airbag killing the person it was installed to save.
5. **Limitations:** We cannot independently verify Nissan's 0.002% estimate or the serial-number range of the affected lot (not published). The single triggering crash is the entire empirical basis; there may be unreported incidents, or there may genuinely be only one defective unit. The 26V230 documents we cite are via secondary reporting (carcomplaints/USA Today quoting NHTSA); the Part 573 filing itself wasn't directly reviewed. FARS 2014–2023 does not isolate Cube airbag-module injuries, so no independent body-count cross-tab was possible.

## Actionable takeaways (required)

- If you own or drive a 2009–2010 Nissan Cube (including a CPO one): enter your 17-digit VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. This is a serial-number inspection, not a blanket part replacement — most Cubes will be cleared in one dealer visit. If yours has the affected inflator, the replacement is free and comes from a different manufacturer.
- Don't skip it because "the defect rate is 0.002%." The whole point of the recall is that Nissan can't tell which of the 47,928 cars has the bad weld without looking. If yours is the one, the failure mode is your airbag detaching mid-deployment.
- Nissan customer service: 800-647-7261, reference recall R26A1.
