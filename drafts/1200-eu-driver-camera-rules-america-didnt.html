Europe Wrote the Law for the Camera Watching Your Eyes. America Wrote Nothing.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2027

Before you sign that lease, you might want to see this. Since July 7, 2024, every new car registered in the EU has carried an infrared camera aimed at the driver’s face, and yours has one too.[1] Europe wrote, in statute, what it may remember. America wrote nothing.

4.8 million
Driver-monitoring-equipped cars on the road at one supplier alone, up 67% in a year

Its name is Advanced Driver Distraction Warning. Above 50 km/h your gaze may drift more than 30 degrees below your eye point for 3.5 seconds, six between 20 and 50, then the car must speak up, escalating until you look back.[1]

Those thresholds are written in seconds, not feet, and seconds do not scale. At 31 mph, 3.5 seconds is 160 feet of blind road. At 70 it is 360 feet. At 81, with the extension, 590 feet pass before the car must speak.

It works at night by design. 940-nanometer infrared emitters light the cabin, a wavelength chosen because sunlight and sunglasses struggle to defeat it, the red glow engineered out.[1]

Europe wrote the data rules into the same law: no continuous recording, nothing retained beyond its purpose, no third party ever gets it, immediate deletion, and no reliance on biometric personal data. It may watch your eyes, but it may not know whose they are.[1]

America has no federal standard for driver monitoring. Congress ordered NHTSA to write an impaired-driving prevention rule, due November 15, 2024. The agency took 18,367 comments, filed its report explaining the miss, and published no proposed rule.[2] So the governing law is the manufacturer’s privacy policy, plus state biometric statutes. Exactly one has real enforcement weight: Illinois’s Biometric Information Privacy Act.[1]

Even at 99.9 percent accuracy, the agency warned, the technology could wrongly stop drivers millions to tens of millions of times per year.[4] The rough arithmetic: 290 million registered vehicles, two starts a day, 365 days, a 0.1 percent error rate, equals roughly 211 million false interlocks a year. An illustration on stated assumptions, not the agency’s number. It frames NHTSA’s range as conservative, and it shows why the agency fears mandating a machine that bars sober drivers.

The strongest case against my complaint is that the cameras already work here. Super Cruise and BlueCruise demand eyes on the road and disengage when you look away, federal standard or not. BIPA’s statutory damages per violation may be a sharper stick than European type approval. And Europe’s mandate has an escape hatch: drivers may deactivate it, and it reactivates each ignition cycle, so a nagging system gets switched off every morning.[1]

Read the driver-monitoring section of your privacy policy: does footage leave the car, how long is it stored, who buys it. In Illinois, BIPA gives you a private right of action, so read the consent screen instead of tapping through. If the warnings cry wolf, recalibrate them instead of training yourself to ignore every chime. Do not shop expecting a federal alcohol-interlock standard anytime soon.[3]

What this piece did not prove: I have not pulled the EU regulation text, so the thresholds are as reported by The Auto Wire. The 4.8-million and 211-million figures are company-reported and my arithmetic, respectively. Sunglasses degradation is inferred, not measured.
