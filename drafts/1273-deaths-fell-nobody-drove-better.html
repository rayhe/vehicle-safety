Traffic Deaths Fell for Three Straight Years. Nobody Drove Better.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2026

Good news first, because the good news is real. Between 2021 and 2024, the US motor vehicle mortality rate fell from 13.0 deaths per 100,000 people to 11.5, three straight years of decline from the pandemic peak, across 166,230 deaths counted in NHTSA's Fatality Analysis Reporting System.[1] Americans also drove more over the same stretch, from 3.13 trillion miles to 3.29 trillion, so this was not a story about empty roads. Per-mile risk fell hard enough to swamp the extra miles. Somebody should get credit. According to a Stanford-led team publishing in JAMA Network Open on October 8, 2026, it was not American drivers.

51%
Share of the per-mile mortality risk decline accounted for by fatalities involving an unrestrained occupant, alone or combined with speeding or alcohol

Here is the decomposition, and it reads like a riddle. Researchers split the 1.81-per-100,000 drop in per-mile risk into 11 mutually exclusive categories. Unrestrained-only deaths fell 0.42 per 100,000, the single largest contribution at 23% of the decline. Add in unrestrained deaths combined with speeding (down 0.21) and with alcohol (down 0.19), and the unrestrained-involved group accounts for half the improvement.[1] Meanwhile seat belt use sat stable near 91% for the whole period, and self-reported speeding sat stable from 2019 through 2024. Read that twice. Deaths among people who do not buckle up fell sharply, while buckling up did not rise. Deaths involving speeding fell, while speeding did not fall. A morality tale would be simpler. Reality declined to cooperate.

One group got nothing at all. Pedestrian deaths fell 0.23 per 100,000, a genuine improvement on top of a grim baseline: IIHS counts 7,080 pedestrian deaths in 2024, 18% of all crash fatalities, up 72% since the 2009 low.[3] Bicyclist deaths, by contrast, did not move. Their contribution to the decomposition was 0.02 per 100,000 with an uncertainty interval crossing zero, which is statistics for "flat."[1] Authors flag this as perpetuating a long-standing gap relative to peer countries, and they are being polite. Cyclists watched three years of national improvement pass them by entirely, while sharing roads with the same heavy vehicles that kill pedestrians at record-adjacent rates.

Geography adds a second puzzle. Vehicle miles rose in 44 of 51 jurisdictions, yet no state suffered a statistically significant mortality increase. Louisiana led the improvement at minus 4.62 deaths per 100,000, followed by Georgia at minus 4.22 and South Carolina at minus 4.11.[1] Thirteen states posted a statistically significant decline in at least one behavioral risk category. A uniform national story, like a federal campaign or a single model year, would not produce that map. Something structural moved, unevenly, and nobody has yet named it with confidence: newer fleets, better crash structures, shifting enforcement, or the pandemic cohort simply aging out of its worst years are all hypotheses, not findings, and the study is honest about that distinction.

What to do with a mystery like this: buckle up anyway, because the single largest slice of the decline came from the exact deaths a belt prevents, and a flat 91% belt-use rate means roughly one driver in eleven still gambles on every trip. Cyclists should assume the national improvement does not cover them, since the data says precisely that, and ride like the exception they are. Policymakers chasing the next decline should note where the last one actually came from: not from persuasion, which demonstrably did not move behavior, but from whatever changed the lethality of crashes that still happened. Find that mechanism and fund it. Keep preaching at drivers if you must, but do not confuse a sermon with a result.

What this does not prove: decomposition is accounting, not causation, and it cannot say why unrestrained deaths fell while belt use stayed flat. FARS 2024 data remain preliminary and subject to revision. State-level VMT and FARS quality vary, and uncertainty intervals capture sampling noise, not systematic bias. Researchers could not split the decline by age, race, or ethnicity because VMT is not stratified that way, so whether pandemic-era disparities narrowed is unknown. NHTSA early estimates put 2025 deaths at 36,640, down 6.7% from 2024, suggesting the decline continued, but the mechanism split for 2025 has not been computed.[2]

Strongest case against this story, stated fairly: 2021 was an abnormally lethal year, and some of any fall from a spike is just the spike unwinding, not a structural improvement. Grant every word of that, because mean reversion is real. Still, reversion should shrink all categories roughly proportionally, and this decline did not. Unrestrained-involved deaths drove half of it while cyclist deaths did not move at all. Size of the decline might be luck. Shape of the decline is a finding, and its shape says the road got safer in a way that had nothing to do with drivers getting better.
