Older Pedestrians Don't Get Hit More Often. Their Bodies Just Can't Take the Hit.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 27, 2026

Here's a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. In 2023, nearly 8,200 pedestrians age 65 or older were injured by motor vehicles, and they were not hit at a higher rate than younger pedestrians. Same injury rate, and then the scoreboard diverged: more than 1,500 of them died, their death rate above the all-ages average.[1] You can get hit the same number of times and still lose more often. The crash is survivable. The body doing the crashing is not.

17% vs 22%
Share of the U.S. population age 65 and older, versus their share of all pedestrian deaths, per CDC data.

Two mechanisms, and only one of them is about the street. Older adults cross more slowly, hear and see less, and react later. The killing part is what happens after contact. "The consequences of injuries are stratospherically different the older you get," said Charles DiMaggio, an injury epidemiologist at NYU Grossman. A hip fracture at 45 is a bad month; at 75 it can start a cascade of surgery, immobility, and complications. Meredith Melville, 73, was in a crosswalk on Piedmont Avenue in Oakland when a Toyota sedan found her. She got a new hip, a repaired elbow, and a future knee replacement, and she calls herself lucky. She's right. It often goes the other way.[1]

Now the part that should embarrass everyone in the business. After pedestrian deaths fell for all ages from the mid-1970s through the mid-2000s, the rate for pedestrians 70 and older simply stopped improving. IIHS data, read back by Columbia's Andrew Rundle: from about 2008 through 2024 the fatality rate for the oldest pedestrians stayed above average and "stayed stubbornly flat." Meanwhile the overall system got safer: NHTSA's early estimate for 2025 put total traffic deaths at 36,640, down 6.7 percent, at a rate of 1.10 per 100 million miles, the second-lowest ever recorded, with driving up.[2][3] Eighteen years of progress everywhere else, and for your grandparents, flatline. If safety were a treatment, this one would have lost its trial a decade ago.

Three things are pushing against them, and none of them are fixable with a "look both ways" lecture. Older adults are walking more, not less, up from 60 percent reporting walking for exercise in 2011 to 65 percent now.[1] Then the vehicles changed: a New York Times investigation in June documented how taller hoods and larger blind zones on SUVs and trucks contribute to rising pedestrian deaths, so the physics of a collision got worse exactly when the frailest bodies entered the crosswalk.[4] And the geography is almost cruel: older pedestrians die most often near hospitals, pharmacies, and senior and community centers. We built the sidewalks to the pharmacy and did not make the walk survivable.

So here is the heresy the data demands. We spent twenty years teaching old people to cross better, and the problem was never primarily the crossing. It was the body, the hood height, and the street. "Our job is to make the system safer for all ages and capacities," said Stephen Mooney, an injury epidemiologist at the University of Washington, adding that safety should not rely on a pedestrian noticing a car coming and jumping out of the way.[1] Structure beats behavior lectures here: pedestrian islands, curb extensions, leading pedestrian intervals that give walkers a head start over turning cars, lower speed limits. Spend that money on the three blocks around the pharmacy, not on a brochure.[5] You cannot out-walk a blind spot with brittle bones. The crosswalk does not grade on a curve, and you will age into this dataset. All of us will.
