## Key data (verified)
- Compliance dates: Sept 1, 2029 (standard OEMs); Sept 1, 2030 (small-volume/final-stage/alterers). Proposed extension: +2 years -> Sept 1, 2031 / Sept 1, 2032.
- NHTSA projections: >=360 lives/year, >=24,000 injuries/year at full implementation. Rule cost: ~$82/vehicle (~$354M/year industry-wide).
- The "challenging aspects" (from rule text + AAI petition): (a) no-contact with lead vehicle at up to 100 km/h (62 mph); (b) pedestrian detection in darkness; (c) false-activation limits (braking must not exceed specified amounts when no threat); (d) no driver off-switch (deactivation prohibition, except tow mode/low-range 4WD, resets at ignition).
- Statutory root: Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, 49 U.S.C. § 30129 (Nov 2021) — Congress *ordered* the rule; NHTSA didn't volunteer it.
- Delay-cost arithmetic (original): 2 years x 360 lives = ~720 lives; 2 x 24,000 = ~48,000 injuries of forgone steady-state benefit. See Limitations for the fleet-turnover caveat.
- Timeline: May 9, 2024 final rule (89 FR 39686) -> June 2024 petitions -> Nov 26, 2024 partial grant/denial (89 FR 93199) -> Jan 17, 2025 lawsuit -> Feb 2025 abeyance -> March 23, 2026 status report (2-yr extension NPRM in prep) -> Sept 2026: NPRM still unpublished; Sept 2029 deadline technically still in place.

## Original contribution (novel analysis)
1. **Delay-cost arithmetic:** nobody has priced the two-year slip in lives: ~720 lives and ~48,000 injuries of forgone steady-state benefit, per NHTSA's own projections. Presented with the fleet-turnover caveat (below), this is the article's headline number.
2. **Litigation timeline reconstruction:** the full chain from petition to abeyance to the March 2026 status report, assembled from FR documents, the court docket, and the CTA letter — the first plain-English account of *why* the 2029 date is fiction.
3. **"Challenging aspects" inventory:** naming exactly what the industry says it can't do (62-mph no-contact, night pedestrian, false-positive limits) so readers can judge the excuse themselves.
4. **The abeyance trap:** the lawsuit is frozen, not dead; the extension NPRM is drafted, not published. Automakers get "continued uncertainty" (Nelson Mullins) while the deadline stands on paper — the worst of both worlds for planning, and the best of both worlds for delay.

## Limitations (honest)
- The 720-lives figure is NHTSA's own projection multiplied by two years of delay. It is a steady-state estimate: real benefits phase in over ~12 years of fleet turnover, so a two-year compliance delay shifts the whole benefit curve right by roughly two years rather than instantly costing 720 lives in 2029-2031. The cumulative forgone benefit is on that order, realized over the following decade.
- NHTSA's 360/24,000 projections are model-based estimates from 2024, not measured outcomes; they assume the systems perform as specified.
- The March 2026 status report says DOT is *preparing* an NPRM; it has not been published as of this writing, and the September 2029 deadline technically remains in force. The extension is proposed, not final.
- The case (Auto Innovators v. NHTSA, D.C. Cir. #25-1026) remains in abeyance; its outcome is unknown and could moot or reshape the rulemaking.
- IIHS's 22% flunk rate (from #986) measures performance against IIHS's test, not FMVSS 127's — related but not identical protocols.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
The industry's engineering complaint is not pure theater. Our own site's #986 found that 22% of currently rated systems score Poor/Marginal on IIHS's harder front crash prevention test, Jeep fails at 61%, and the failure mode that matters most — pedestrian at night, at speed — is exactly what FMVSS 127 demands by 2029. Teaching a car to slam its own brakes at 62 mph with zero contact while *never* phantom-braking on a highway overpass shadow is genuinely one of the hardest perception problems in production cars, and false positives at highway speed can themselves cause rear-end crashes — which is precisely what Bozzella warned about in his June 2024 letter to Congress. NHTSA itself has now conceded the point in the only language that matters: it is drafting a two-year extension for the "challenging aspects of the standard." A mandate the regulator admits needs more time is a mandate whose original deadline was, at least in part, aspirational. The honest version of this story is not "evil lobby kills safety rule" — it's that the rule's bar is real, the industry is partly behind it, and the delay is the price of a bar nobody can fake.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- Buying new in 2029-2031? Do not assume the car has FMVSS-127-grade AEB. The mandate date on paper is September 2029; the working date is September 2031. Ask specifically about pedestrian detection *in darkness* and lead-vehicle avoidance speed — those are the two requirements most of today's systems fail.
- Shopping used: the IIHS front crash prevention rating for the *exact model year* (iihs.org/ratings) is the only public proxy for how close a car is to the federal bar. Two model years of the same car can differ more than two brands.
- The $82/vehicle cost figure is NHTSA's — if a dealer tells you compliant AEB "isn't available at this price point," that number is your answer.
- The extension NPRM, when published, will have a public comment period. That is the last lever before 2031 is locked in.
