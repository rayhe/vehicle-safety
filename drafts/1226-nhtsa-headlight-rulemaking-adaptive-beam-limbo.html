Existential Dread

The Cure for Blinding Headlights Has Existed Since 2012. America Outlawed It, Legalized It, Then Ruled It Into Irrelevance.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
November 20, 2027

Fourteen years ago, Europe solved the most hated thing on your evening commute. America read the solution, admired it, kept it off American roads, then legalized it. Then it wrote the rule so carefully that hardly anyone can use it. That last sentence is not a joke. It is the current state of Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 108, which NHTSA announced on October 6 it will finally modernize, through its first negotiated lighting rulemaking in more than 25 years. Public comments are due November 9.[1]

60% vs. 0.2%
Drivers who call headlight glare a problem after dark, versus the share of nighttime crashes where police coded glare as a factor. Both numbers are true. Neither is comforting.

Start with the outrage, because the outrage is real. An AAA survey of 1,092 adults this February found 60 percent call headlight glare a problem after dark, and 73 percent of the annoyed say it has gotten worse over the past decade. Women report it more than men. Glasses wearers more than the clear-eyed. Pickup drivers, perched highest, complain least. Then the IIHS walked in with 24 million police-reported crashes and found glare coded as a factor in one or two of every thousand nighttime crashes. Usually it was a lane departure in an old car, and most "glare" crashes happened in daylight, courtesy of the sun. Your eyes are not lying to you. The crash census just does not measure what does not crash.[1]

Call it solved, and call it 2012. Adaptive driving beams carve shadows around oncoming cars while keeping the rest of the road lit, and Europe has sold them since then. Toyota petitioned Washington to allow them in 2013. Washington needed until 2022. Then came the fine print: industry voices argued the final rule held adaptive beams to a stricter glare standard than the conventional lights they were replacing, and demanded new lamp designs instead of switching on hardware already sitting in some imported luxury cars. Automakers asked the agency to reconsider. The IIHS says American requirements still restrict how adaptive beams function and may blunt their effect. Today only a few manufacturers sell them here. BMW and Audi ship physically identical hardware to Europe and America, with the clever part turned off in the American cars. Read that again, slowly.[2]

Washington, meanwhile, has been busy policing brightness at the other end. In July, NHTSA denied Tesla's request to avoid a recall of nearly 20,000 Model 3 and Y vehicles whose headlights may exceed maximum lighting levels, rejecting the claim that the glare risk was inconsequential. In 2022, it turned down a similar bid from General Motors covering some 820,000 vehicles. The same rulebook defines no glare limits in lux for conventional headlights, the kind blinding you right now, even though adaptive beams must meet one. Ten states require headlight alignment checks at inspection. Ten. Everywhere else, your truck's lamps point wherever physics and laziness decide.[1]

So the rulemaking matters. NHTSA listed five work areas: glare from modern headlights, the LED and auto-high-beam rules, headlamp height and aim, plastic-optic test procedures, and harmonizing with international standards, which is bureaucrat for "catch up with Europe." Secretary Duffy appoints up to 18 stakeholders. You can nominate someone, or tell the committee what your retinas think, at regulations.gov under docket NHTSA-2026-1981 before November 9. Until then, check your headlight aim, because only ten states do, and confirm high-beam assist is actually enabled, since it resets on some trims and resets you into a dark curve. Fourteen years. The light already knows how to bend around an oncoming car. We just need the paperwork to say so.
