The Chart Says the Trailblazer Is America's Deadliest SUV. The Chart Is Counting the Wrong Truck.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
June 13, 2027

I ran the numbers on SUV death rates, then I ran them again. At 2.83 deaths per 100 million miles, the Chevrolet Trailblazer came out with 2.83 deaths per 100 million miles, the highest rate of any SUV in the FARS 2014–2023 data: 14.9 times the Toyota RAV4’s 0.19, worse than the Tahoe’s 2.49 and the Yukon’s 2.55.[1] That looked wrong, because the Trailblazer is a small crossover and those are three-ton trucks. It was wrong, just not in the way I expected.

98.7%
Share of Trailblazer deaths in FARS 2014–2023 from model years 2009 or older; the rebooted 2021+ crossover accounts for 31.

That 2.83 is not the small crossover’s number. It belongs to the 2002–2009 body-on-frame Trailblazer, a truck that shares nothing with the current one but the badge. Cross the model-level rate against the model-year data and the split is stark: 2,426 of 2,457 tracked deaths are model year 2009 or older, while the rebooted 2021–2023 crossover contributes just 31.[1][2] The headline rate divides a discontinued truck’s body count by a fleet estimate that includes the new crossover. That is not a measurement; it is an accounting error wearing a lab coat.

This is not a one-nameplate bug, so I ran the census. Ford’s Ranger posts 2.91, the highest pickup rate in the dataset, on 98.3% pre-2019 deaths; the 2019+ truck is a different platform on a different continent’s engineering. Chevrolet’s Blazer shows the artifact cuts both ways at 0.56: 89.9% of its deaths are the old body-on-frame Blazer, so a crossover is currently wearing a low number built by a truck that died two decades ago. Any “deadliest cars” ranking that treats a nameplate as one continuous vehicle is doing arithmetic on a ghost.

To be fair to the chart, part of the pattern is real, and the vintage quartiles prove it. For every high-rate nameplate, the middle half of deaths lands in roughly 1997–2015: Mustang, Accord, Impala, Ranger, all of them. Old vehicles are genuinely more dangerous per mile. They were built with weaker structures, they predate the 2012 ESC mandate, and by the time they’re cheap enough to be third-owner beaters they attract the riskiest drivers.[3] The nameplate-reboot artifact is a second distortion stacked on top of a true phenomenon, which is exactly why it survives review. Half right is the hardest kind of wrong to catch.

Steel-manning the chart: the old Trailblazer earned its number and nobody is misreading it. Early-2000s body-on-frame SUVs were rollover-prone and genuinely dangerous, and a stat built from 2,426 of their deaths is a fair obituary for that truck. Agreed, with one amendment: it is a fair obituary, and obituaries should not be stapled to the living. Applying a GMT360’s record to a 2024 unibody crossover is like grading a student on their grandparent’s transcript.

What this analysis cannot tell you: whether the current Trailblazer is safe. Thirty-one deaths is a numerator without a denominator, far too small for a credible rate, which is why IIHS driver-death-rate reports demand millions of registered vehicle years before they’ll print a number.[3] The fleet and VMT estimates behind the 2.83 carry roughly ±15% uncertainty for low-volume models, FARS records fatal crashes only, and the surviving old Trailblazers skew toward the least-maintained examples with the riskiest owners. Every one of those caveats widens the error bars; none of them rescue the nameplate rate.

Here is the actionable version: when any “deadliest cars by model” chart names a rebooted nameplate, check the model-year mix before you believe the number. You cannot judge the 2024 Trailblazer by a rate built from 2002 GMT360s, and neither can you the new Ranger or Blazer. Your reliable signal is the IIHS rating for the exact model year you are buying: lab-tested, generation-specific, no ghosts.[4] And the meta-lesson, the one I will be applying to my own future columns: a nameplate is a marketing decision, not a vehicle, and the spreadsheet should never forget that.
