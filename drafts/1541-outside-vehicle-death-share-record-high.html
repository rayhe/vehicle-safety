The Car Got Safe. The Street Didn't.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
November 20, 2026

In 1996, four out of five Americans who died on the road died inside a vehicle. In 2024, barely three out of five did. Thirty-eight percent of road deaths now happen outside a car, the highest share ever recorded.[1] NHTSA published the fifty-year split in its 2024 overview: the car has never been safer for the person sitting in it, and the street has never been a bigger share of the killing.

38%
Share of 2024 US road deaths that happened outside a vehicle, a record high, up from 20 percent in 1996.

Run the absolute numbers and the picture gets worse. 1996 saw 42,065 deaths, split about 80/20, which puts roughly 33,650 inside and 8,410 outside.[3] 2024 saw 39,254 deaths: 24,473 inside, 14,781 outside, counting motorcyclists, pedestrians, cyclists, and other nonoccupants.[1] Inside deaths fell 27 percent while outside deaths rose 76 percent, and total deaths fell while the street absorbed the entire improvement and then some.

Every 2024 category moved the same direction except the ones that matter. Passenger-car occupant deaths fell 9.7 percent, while SUV occupant deaths rose 1.6 percent, the only occupant class that went up. Pedestrian deaths fell 3.9 percent, but pedestrian injuries rose 5.0 percent. Motorcyclist deaths fell 2.2 percent, but motorcyclist injuries rose 5.2 percent.[1] Read those pairs together: crashes still happen, survival just moved inside the car. Bumpers, hoods, and windshields did not get kinder; airbags and crumple zones got better at saving the person they were built around.

Fair objection: this is partly good news wearing a scary costume. Occupant protection worked, which is why the outside share rises whenever inside deaths fall faster; pedestrian deaths even fell in 2024, and the 2025 early estimate put total deaths at a record-low rate of 1.10 per 100 million miles.[2] A reasonable reader could call 38 percent a denominator trick. What that reading misses is the absolute count: outside deaths rose from about 8,400 to nearly 14,800 while the total fell, and injuries outside the car rose significantly in the same year deaths fell, which no denominator trick explains away.

First, the share shift is partly compositional: it is not proof that walking got more dangerous per mile walked, only that more pedestrians died and they are a bigger share of the total. Second, "outside the vehicle" includes motorcyclists, who are vehicle operators, not bystanders; pedestrians and pedalcyclists alone are 8,183 deaths, about 21 percent of the total. Third, 2024 figures come from the FARS Annual Report File, subject to revision, and the 1996 counts are computed from the report's rounded 80/20 split.

When shopping for a car, check the IIHS pedestrian front crash prevention rating, not just the occupant crash scores: the five stars on the window sticker measure the person inside, and the pedestrian rating measures everyone else.[4] On the road, slow down on arterials, where most pedestrian deaths happen at 40 mph or higher.[5] If you are the one on foot, assume the car does not see you. Thirty years of engineering made the cabin a survival cell, and nobody built one for the crosswalk.
