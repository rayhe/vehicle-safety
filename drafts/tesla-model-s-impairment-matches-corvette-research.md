# Research: Tesla Impairment Paradox — The "Safest Car" Has a Bar-Car Problem

## Angle
FARS toxicology data reveals Tesla's most expensive model — the Model S — has a 24% impairment rate in fatal crashes. That's higher than the national sedan average (~18-19%) and comparable to the Corvette (26.2%) and BMW 7 Series (26.1%). More importantly: within Tesla's own lineup, impairment rates track price. The more expensive the Tesla, the drunker the driver who died in it.

### Comparable Impairment Rates (sedans/sports cars)
- Corvette: 26.2% (1,147 drivers) — classic bar-car
- BMW 7 Series: 26.1% (230 drivers)
- Cadillac CTS: 25.9% (931 drivers)
- **Tesla Model S: 24.0%** (204 drivers)
- Infiniti G37: 25.0% (959 drivers)
- BMW 3 Series: 22.1% (sedan benchmark)
- Toyota Prius: 16.0% (1,746 drivers) — the sober EV comparison

### Price-Impairment Hierarchy (Tesla only)
- Model S (~$80K-$100K+): 24.0% impaired
- Model X (~$80K-$100K+): 21.3% impaired
- Model 3 (~$35K-$50K): 20.1% impaired
- Model Y (~$45K-$55K): 17.6% impaired

The pattern mirrors the broader FARS finding: more expensive performance vehicles attract higher impairment rates. Tesla is no exception.

## Limitations
- Model S toxicology sample (204 drivers) is smaller than ideal; Model 3 (765) and Model Y (455) are more robust
- FARS rate calculation uses VMT estimates, not odometer data (±15% uncertainty for lower-volume models)
- Impairment ≠ impaired driving — some positive-tox drivers may not have been the at-fault party
- Tesla demographics (income, age) likely confound the price-impairment correlation

## Strongest Counterargument
Tesla's low death rates (Model 3: 0.05, Model Y: 0.03 per 100M VMT) suggest the vehicles DO protect occupants well in crashes. The impairment rate reflects who drives these cars, not how safe they are. A 20% impairment rate in a vehicle with a 0.05 death rate means the absolute number of impaired-driver deaths is tiny. But the rate-vs-rate comparison is still newsworthy because it punctures the halo effect.
