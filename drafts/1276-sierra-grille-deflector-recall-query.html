Existential Dread

GM Recalled 70,768 Sierras for a Flying Grille. NHTSA's Audit Covers 535,639.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 25, 2026

On October 9, federal regulators opened a file that GM probably wishes did not exist. NHTSA's Office of Defects Investigation launched recall query RQ26003 into the GMC Sierra 1500's upper grille deflector, after receiving 118 complaints of the trim bar detaching at speed.[1] Twenty-one of those complaints come from trucks that already had the recall repair. That audit covers an estimated 535,639 pickups, which is 7.6 times the number GM recalled in the first place.

7.6×
Audit population (535,639 trucks) versus original recall population (70,768 trucks) for the same grille deflector

A recall query is not a defect investigation and it is not a new recall. It is NHTSA auditing a recall it already approved, asking two questions: did the recall cover the right trucks, and did the fix hold? Possible endings include an expanded recall, a revised remedy, or nothing at all. Think of it as the agency grading its own homework, in public, with the manufacturer's original answers attached.

GM filed the original campaign, recall 25V-060, on February 6, 2025, covering 70,768 model-year 2022 Sierra 1500s built with a chrome grille deflector between November 2021 and September 2022.[2] Eight attachment points hold the deflector on. Those points can crack over time, and if all eight let go, a long rigid bar leaves the front of a full-size pickup at highway speed. GM's own estimate put the defect rate at 3%. By the end of 2024 the company had counted 1,225 complaints and four accident allegations, with no known injuries. Dealers needed about an hour for the remedy: reinforce the existing clips, and do not replace the grille unless necessary.[4]

Worse is the paper trail. A GM employee flagged a detached deflector through the company's internal Speak Up for Safety program on January 19, 2024. GM opened an investigation five weeks later, then closed it on May 23, 2024, after finding no defect trend. Warranty claims kept climbing, so GM reopened the file on December 11, 2024, and filed the recall two months later. Twelve and a half months from first flag to recall, including six and a half months in a closed file.

Run the division the recall query invites. 535,639 divided by 70,768 is 7.57, which means the original recall covered 13.2% of the trucks now under scrutiny. Left outside: 464,871 trucks, non-chrome 2022s plus every 2023 and 2024. And 21 of 118 complaints, 17.8%, allege detachment after the repair. Complaints are not a rate, and ODI has published no denominator for repaired trucks, so nobody can say what fraction of fixes failed. But a remedy that shows up in the failure file at all has a durability problem, not just a scope problem. Separately, GM Authority reported a repair rate near 64% as of January 2026, leaving roughly 25,500 trucks inside even the narrow recall still unfixed.[3]

On paper a grille deflector is cosmetic. At 70 mph it is a rigid bar at bumper-to-windshield height, departing one of America's best-selling trucks into traffic. ODI lists the people at risk as the truck itself, nearby vehicles, motorcyclists, and pedestrians, and describes detachment as happening "without detectability." Complaints mention cracked windshields and drivers swerving. Most exposed: the motorcyclist behind a Sierra at highway speed, with the least protection and the least warning.

Steel-man GM before burying it. GM followed the process its own system prescribes: flag, investigate, close on the evidence available, reopen when warranty data moved, recall within two months of reopening. Its 2023-2024 exclusion rested on a supplier suspect-window analysis, not a guess. And 118 complaints across 535,639 trucks is 0.02%, a rate so low most of the query population will never experience this. If the query ends in no action, GM's original scope call will have been right. Fair. But none of that explains the 21. A scope dispute is a paperwork argument. A repaired truck shedding the same part is a hardware argument, and it is the one GM has to answer.

What this article did not prove, stated plainly. An RQ is not a defect finding and not a new recall; NHTSA has declared nothing defective here. Those 21 post-repair complaints are allegations in a complaint file, not confirmed remedy failures, and repair status in complaints can be self-reported. No incident rate can be computed because ODI published no denominator. GM's suspect-window claim for later trucks is untested by the query, not refuted by it. No crashes, injuries, or deaths are attributed in the opening summary. If GM's 3% defect estimate held across the full query population, the implied count would be about 16,000 trucks, but that extrapolation is an illustration, not a finding, because the 3% figure was estimated for the chrome-deflector subset only.

So what do you do? If you own a 2022-2024 Sierra 1500, walk around the front and push gently on the top grille bar. Movement, buzz, or rattle over bumps means get it looked at now, not when a letter arrives. Already had 25V-060 done? You are exactly who ODI is asking about: if the deflector loosens or comes off again, file a complaint at nhtsa.gov, because owner complaints are what opened this query.[5] Own a 2023-2024 or a non-chrome 2022? No recall covers you yet, so report a loose deflector anyway and keep repair receipts. Shopping used? Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and check the bar for movement before you buy. And if the query ends in an expanded recall, expect stop-delivery orders on dealer inventory, just as 25V-060 imposed on the original trucks.
