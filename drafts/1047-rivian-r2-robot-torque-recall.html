A Robot Forgot to Tighten 14 Battery Bolts. A Human Caught It.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 2, 2026

Somewhere on the R2 line in Normal, Illinois, there is an automated station whose entire job is torquing high-voltage battery fasteners. This summer it failed at that job on 14 vehicles, and nobody on the line noticed until September 17, when a manufacturing operator spotted the under-torqued fastener and said something. Rivian is now recalling 14 model-year 2027 R2s because a loose high-voltage connection can cut drive power without warning.[1]

14
R2s recalled this week — a tiny footprint for the scariest failure mode on the recall board

Rivian reprogrammed the station so it now rejects improperly torqued fasteners, which sounds like progress until you realize the station was happily passing bad ones before a person intervened. The remedy for the cars is satisfyingly old-school: a technician with a torque wrench inspects each of the 14 and either tightens the connection or replaces damaged high-voltage components, free of charge.[2] No over-the-air patch for this one. You cannot download torque.

Now the part that should make you check your VIN. This is Rivian's second R2 recall in six weeks, and the two are mirror images of each other. Recall one covered 98,828 vehicles and fixed itself through a software update most owners already had: a pop-up that could overlap the backup camera image, an FMVSS 111 paperwork problem.[4] Recall two covers 14 vehicles and requires opening a high-voltage battery pack. The defect population is four orders of magnitude smaller, and it is the one where the car can go dark at highway speed. Scale and danger ran in opposite directions, which is worth remembering the next time a headline tells you a recall's size instead of its failure mode.

Veterans of this beat will feel a flicker of recognition. In October 2022, Rivian recalled 12,212 vehicles because the nut joining the front upper control arm to the steering knuckle "may not have been sufficiently torqued."[3] Same failure class, four years later, on a new vehicle and a new line, at 1/870th the volume. That is either a quality system catching things earlier or a torque specification that keeps getting lost between the design office and the assembly line. Rivian's filing does not say which, and it is the kind of question that decides whether this is a story about one robot or about a company.

Rivian says it is not aware of any crashes or injuries, which is the outcome you get when a human on the line pays attention.[1] Some owners were told to stop driving pending inspection, and at least one R2 was towed home on a flatbed.[5] Fourteen flatbed rides is the price of the operator in Normal doing their job.

Two caveats, because fairness is a torque spec too. First: we have press summaries of the NHTSA filing, not the filing itself, so there are no torque values and no per-pack fastener count to check. Second: a 14-car recall is arguably the safety system working exactly as designed, and punishing Rivian for filing federal paperwork on 14 cars would teach every automaker to bury the small ones. File the paperwork, tighten the bolts, reprogram the robot. That is what happened here.

If you own an R2 built between May 18 and August 27, 2026: VINs become searchable at nhtsa.gov/recalls on November 28, and you can call Rivian at 1-888-748-4261 and reference recall FSAM-1888.[2] Broader lesson for anyone shopping a brand-new model's first six months of production: treat it like a beta program, because on the assembly line, in some very literal sense, it is one.
