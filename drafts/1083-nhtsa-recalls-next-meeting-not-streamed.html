NHTSA Wants Your Ideas on Recalls. It Just Won't Let You Watch.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
October 7, 2026

On November 5, the federal government will hold a public meeting about the millions of Americans who never get their recalled cars fixed. You cannot watch it. Near the very end of the notice NHTSA published in the Federal Register on September 28, in plain language, sits this sentence: the event will not be live streamed or recorded.[1] In person only. Washington, D.C. On a Thursday.

29.2 million
Vehicles covered by the nearly 900 safety recalls NHTSA administered in 2025, per the agency's own meeting notice

The meeting is called Recalls Next: Technological Innovations and Safety Recalls, signed by Administrator Jonathan Morrison under FR Doc. 2026-19712, with a public docket (NHTSA-2026-2014) open for comments through December 5.[1] It exists because the agency knows its recall system leaks. It administered nearly 900 safety recalls in 2025 covering more than 29.2 million vehicles, and the notice names three problems it wants solved: reaching owners of used vehicles who never bought them new, contact information that is incomplete or out of date, and completion gaps between different groups of vehicles.

Keynotes, industry panels, and what the notice calls facilitated workshop tables are on the agenda. Topics include how automakers reach owners, how they explain risk, and how they get people to book the repair. The tools under discussion: digital communications, connected-vehicle technology, mobile repair, and over-the-air software updates, plus whatever new recall questions automated vehicles are raising.[1] All of that is reasonable. Now weigh it against the guest list the problem implies.

NHTSA says it cannot reach used-car owners, people whose addresses changed, and communities with lower completion rates. Meanwhile, the people spending a Thursday in the DOT atrium will be automakers, dealer groups, suppliers, and trade associations.[1] A meeting about the unreachable, unreachable to the unreachable. You could set your watch by this kind of symmetry.

There is a second asymmetry buried in the procedure section. Companies may file comments as confidential business information under 49 CFR part 512, sending NHTSA a redacted public copy and an unredacted one to the Office of Chief Counsel. Individual commenters get a different warning: everything they submit posts without change to regulations.gov, personal information included.[1] So the institution gets the sealed envelope and the citizen gets the photocopier.

Do the fleet math: roughly 282 million vehicles are registered in the United States,[4] which means about one in ten was covered by a recall in 2025 alone. Completion rates vary by campaign, and the agency's own notice admits the letters often go to the previous owner, naming completion gaps between vehicle groups as one of the three problems on the agenda. At a 20 percent non-completion rate, that is on the order of 5.8 million vehicles rolling around with an open safety defect. An illustrative figure, not a measured one, but the direction is not in doubt.

The closed room has one honest defense, and it is candor: facilitated workshops need it, cameras inhibit it, and the docket is genuinely open to everyone for 30 days after the meeting. NHTSA routinely posts meeting summaries, so absence of a promise to publish is not proof nothing will be published, and the stale-address problem is a data-pipeline problem, not a transparency problem. The October 28 versus October 29 registration mismatch between the event page and the Federal Register is, most likely, a one-day typo rather than a conspiracy.[1]

Limitations are worth stating plainly: the 900-recall and 29.2-million figures are NHTSA's own, not independently audited, and they sweep in equipment and tire campaigns alongside vehicle recalls. Completion rates vary wildly by campaign type, and a meeting that is not streamed is not the same as a meeting whose conclusions are never published. The 5.8-million estimate is arithmetic on an assumption, offered to size the gap, not to assert a count.

Three things you can do anyway: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, free, no letter required, and it shows open recalls whether or not anyone ever mailed you about them.[3] Bought used? Register the car with the manufacturer so the next notice finds you instead of the stranger who sold it to you. And if you want a say in recall policy, file a comment on docket NHTSA-2026-2014 at regulations.gov before December 5, and strip out anything personal before you hit submit, because NHTSA will post it verbatim.[1]
