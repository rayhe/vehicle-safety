Ford Wrote the Fix for Its Leaking EcoBoost Engines in 2019. 499,603 Edges Are Still Waiting for the Recall.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
September 20, 2026

On August 5, a defect petition asked NHTSA to investigate the 2.0-liter EcoBoost engine in half a million Ford Edges. The agency opened Defect Petition DP26008 on September 3.[1] I have read a lot of federal paperwork. This one made me set my coffee down: Ford documented this exact defect, and the exact repair, in a December 2019 technical service bulletin.[2]

499,603
2015–2018 Ford Edge SUVs with the 2.0L EcoBoost now under federal review in DP26008

The failure mode reads like a horror short. Coolant seeps into the cylinders through cracks between them, and the motor starts drinking its own cooling system. Drivers get an “Engine Coolant Over Temperature” warning and a sudden, significant loss of power, then a rough restart and a misfire code. If you are doing 70 in the left lane when the warning arrives, the next few seconds get interesting.[1]

1 in 320
Documented ODI incidents per vehicle in the petition population (1,563 ÷ 499,603), a floor, not a ceiling

TSB 19-2346 (December 2019) covers the 2015–2018 Edge and its 2.0-liter EcoBoost siblings: low coolant, white exhaust smoke, rough running, misfire codes P0300 through P0304. Ford's prescribed correction is unambiguous: replace the long block engine assembly.[2] Not a gasket, not a sensor, just the whole long block. That is a manufacturer writing “the engine is the problem” in a document it hoped only dealers would read.

A class action has been grinding through a California federal court since 2020 over this defect. Miller v. Ford alleges the company knew about the coolant intrusion before the first affected Edge was sold, concealed it, and handed owners what the complaint calls “superficial stopgap, ‘Band-Aid’ remedies such as installing coolant level sensors.”[3] A sensor tells you the coolant is gone; it does not put it back. Owners outside warranty paid for the long block themselves, and the suit alleges the replacement engines can fail the same way, which means some people paid twice.

Enter the petition: a defect petition is the federal government's suggestion box with teeth. Any person can ask NHTSA to investigate; the agency aims to rule within about four months.[5] DP26008 names 499,603 vehicles and 1,563 documented incidents. And here is the number that smirks: zero crashes, zero fires, zero injuries in ODI's summary.[1] The safety case rests on physics, not a body count: motive power evaporating at highway speed, backed by a paper trail showing Ford has known the cure since 2019.

Ford's defense, stated at full strength: these are 8-to-11-year-old vehicles, and a 0.31% documented incident rate with zero crashes reads like an aging-fleet durability problem, not a safety defect. The TSB proves Ford supported the repair through dealers for years, and the defect announces itself with warnings, white smoke, and rough idle, giving drivers a chance to pull over. If NHTSA denies this petition, that paragraph is the reason.

What this piece did not prove: the true failure rate. ODI complaints are self-reported, unverified, and undercounted, so 0.31% is a floor and I cannot tell you the ceiling. FARS captures only fatal crashes, so no fatality rate attaches to this defect. If you own a 2015–2018 Edge 2.0L, watch the coolant reservoir like it owes you money and get a pressure test at the first white smoke or rough idle. If it strands you anyway, file at NHTSA.gov; petitions run on complaint volume. If you are shopping used, ask for engine-replacement records. NHTSA's decision should land around January 2027. I will be reading it with coffee.
