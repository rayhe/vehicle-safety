GM Killed the Impala in 2020. It Was Already the Deadliest American Sedan on the Road.

Rex Driverton • Clautomotive Desk
October 9, 2026

On February 27, 2020, a Cajun Red Impala Premier rolled off the line at Detroit-Hamtramck, and General Motors put the nameplate down. The plant was retooled for electric Hummers; obituaries called it the end of the full-size American sedan.[4] What they did not mention: the car they were burying had been killing its occupants at the worst rate of any American sedan in the federal database.

3,774
Impala occupant deaths in FARS, 2014–2023 — rate 5.0 per 100M miles

Read that rate twice. 5.0 deaths per 100 million miles driven. Across the dataset, sedans average near 2.0. A Kia Forte, the safest sedan on the books, is at 0.40.[1] Do the arithmetic your actuary would: the Impala killed at twelve and a half times the rate of the safest car in its class.

Before you file this under bad drivers, look at the toxicology. Only 21.4 percent of Impala drivers in fatal crashes had any impairment on board.[1] This was not a drunk-driver story. It was a sober-people-in-a-car story. Which means the car is the story.

The years that killed

Split the toll by model year and the picture sharpens into a mugshot. Deadliest years on record: 2006 (355 deaths), 2007 (381), and 2008 (401). Add the whole 2006-to-2014 span and you get 2,328 of the 3,732 model-year deaths. Sixty-two percent of the Impala's decade of carnage came from exactly those model years.[1]

GM recalled the 2006-2014 Impala on June 23, 2014. The defect, per the recall filing: a heavy key ring or a hard bump could knock the ignition switch out of the run position, killing the engine, power steering, power brakes, and the airbags. 3.14 million cars. NHTSA campaign 14V355000.[2] The deadliest cars were, to the year, the recalled cars.

62.4%
of Impala model-year deaths came from the 2006–2014 ignition-switch recall years

Washington's official accounting says the switch defect killed 124 people across every GM model. GM paid $900 million to the Department of Justice in September 2015, took a deferred prosecution deal on conspiracy and wire-fraud charges, and CEO Mary Barra told employees, "We didn't do our jobs."[3] The corporation survived. Cars it could not fix kept driving.

The demographic defense

GM's strongest defense is demographic and it deserves a fair hearing. The Impala was cheap, big, comfortable, and old. It sold to fleet buyers and retirees and young drivers priced out of safer iron. FARS rates cannot separate the vehicle from the people steering it, and early-2000s sedans carried less crash prevention than anything sold today. Some of that 5.0 is the machine. Some of it is the meat behind the wheel.

But the model-year distribution will not let the defense rest. Fleet buyers and demographics do not explain why deaths concentrate, to the model year, inside the recall window and fade after it. A different, quieter tenth generation arrived in 2014 and reads cleaner in this data. Still out there in bulk: the middle years. The switch years.

What this means for you

The Impala is discontinued, not extinct. Hundreds of thousands of 2006-2014 models are still on the road, on used-car lots, in family driveways. Older recalled cars are the least likely to have ever had the recall performed. If you own one, or your teenager just bought one for $4,500 cash: go to nhtsa.gov/recalls, type the VIN, and confirm recall 14299 (campaign 14V355000) is closed on that specific car. The fix takes an hour and involves a key ring insert.[2]

Shopping full-size sedans used? 2014-and-later tenth-gen cars live outside the worst of this. 2006-2013 cars are the ones FARS is still counting. Price accordingly. Or better: shop the segment's safer end and check the rate before you check the price.

Limitations: FARS fatality rates use estimated VMT and fleet data, not odometer readings, with roughly ±15% uncertainty on individual models. Rates combine vehicle crashworthiness with driver demographics and cannot separate the two. The model-year series sums to 3,732, not 3,774, because 42 deaths lack model-year attribution in the FARS records. The 124-death ignition-switch attribution is the DOJ figure; the 3,774 is the Impala's total FARS toll, not an attributed count. Recall completion rates for 2006–2014 Impalas are estimates; NHTSA does not publish per-VIN completion nationally.
