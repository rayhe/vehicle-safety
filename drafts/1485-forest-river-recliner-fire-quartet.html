The RV Recliner Fire Trio Was a Quartet. The Fourth One Is the Biggest.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
November 12, 2026

We counted three; the count was four. In October, four RV makers filed recalls with the identical NHTSA sentence about a power-seat cord fuse that cannot be trusted to stop a fire, and the biggest filer was the one nobody's story included. Forest River recalled 6,275 trailers, more than the other three makers combined, and the count we published missed it entirely.[1][2]

10,738
Trailers recalled across four makers for the same power-seat cord fuse, filed October 16–28, 2026. Forest River alone is 6,275 of them.

Here is the quartet. Alliance RV filed first, October 16, with 3,686 Valor and Delta trailers under campaign 26V680. Forest River filed October 22, with 6,275 units across ten model lines under 26V704. Heartland filed October 27, with 518 fifth wheels under 26V710. Ember filed October 28, with 259 E-Series trailers under 26V714. Twelve days, four competing manufacturers, one component family, one failure mode. Every filing carries the same defect sentence, word for word: the power cord fuse may not provide adequate circuit protection. Every filing carries the same consequence: in the event of a short circuit, inadequate protection increases the risk of a fire.[1][3][4]

Forest River is not a footnote to this count; it is 58 percent of it. Its recall spans six model years and ten lines: 2022–2027 Cedar Creek, 2023–2027 FR3, 2023–2026 Palomino, 2024 Cardinal, Wildcat, and Shasta Oasis, 2024–2026 Hardside and Palomino Real-Lite Camper, plus 2026 Puma and Puma Tracks. A defect that wide is not a bad week at one plant. It is a component family running through the industry's largest RV maker, and it sat in the public file for three weeks while the trio story, ours included, counted the other three.[1][2]

Now the part that should bother you is the fuse, which has exactly one job: interrupt a short before it becomes a fire. These fuses cannot be counted on to do it, which makes the safety device the defect, and the room it sits in is a bedroom. People sleep in these boxes, far from a fire station, with one narrow door out, and the cord runs through the furniture they sleep on. A car-seat fuse is a nuisance. An RV power-seat fuse is the only thing between a warm wire and a fire in a room full of bedding.[4]

Forest River owners also wait longest. Their recall was filed October 22, no remedy is available yet, and owner letters do not go out until December 21. Sixty days with a known fire risk in the fuse of the seat they sit on. Ember owners heard November 23, Heartland November 30, Alliance December 7. Across all four makers, the letter dates run in the order the paperwork cleared, not in the order of risk, which means the last owners to learn about this are the most numerous ones.[2][3]

Give the other side its due, because it has a real case. Nobody was hurt, and no fire, injury, or crash is listed in the NHTSA summaries for any of the four campaigns, which makes all four preventive recalls filed before the fuse failed in the field. Four manufacturers found the same marginal part, filed within twelve days, and fixed it free. Identical wording could reflect NHTSA's standardized defect language rather than a shared supplier, and staggered letters could reflect mailing logistics rather than indifference. That is the recall system working, and the praise is earned. The reply is narrower than the praise: the system still left the largest group waiting longest, and a fuse whose only job is stopping fires should not need four recalls to get right.[1]

What this story does not prove matters too. The filings do not name a shared seat or cord supplier, and we are not guessing one. Identical wording strongly suggests a shared component family, but that is inference from the filings, not a finding in them. The 10,738 total counts potentially affected units, not fuses that will actually fail. And the December 21 letter date is per the November filing summary; the remedy program may have started since, so check the VIN instead of assuming the date still holds.[1]

What to do with this: own a 2022–2027 Cedar Creek, a 2023–2027 FR3, a 2023–2026 Palomino, a 2024 Cardinal, Wildcat, or Shasta Oasis, a 2024–2026 Hardside or Palomino Real-Lite Camper, or a 2026 Puma or Puma Tracks? Run the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now, referencing campaign 26V704 or Forest River 51-2109, and do not wait for the December letter. Own an Alliance Valor or Delta, a Heartland Torque, Bighorn, Cyclone, Gravity, or Road Warrior, or an Ember E-Series? Same check, campaigns 26V680, 26V710, and 26V714. The fix is free at the dealer in every case, cord replacement everywhere and the harness too for Heartland, so get it done before the next trip where anyone sleeps in the trailer. And if you are buying a used RV from any of these model years, the VIN check is the only reliable screen. A fuse is not visible in a walkthrough, and a seller has no reason to mention a recall letter they never opened.[5]
