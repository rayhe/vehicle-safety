One Bad Capacitor, Nine Recalls, and a Notification System That Can't Reach You

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 7, 2026

Grech Motors recalled 134 luxury shuttle buses last week because of a circuit board that can overheat and catch fire.[1] Sounds like a Grech problem, but it isn't. It is the ninth recall for the same board, in the same fan, from the same supplier, filed under nine different campaign numbers.[3]

~3,044
of 5,021 recalled fan boards still in service with the original board (39.35% complete as of late August)[3]

Let's talk about what happens in the first hundred milliseconds of a thermal event on that board. The defect lives on a field-effect transistor, and in the language the coachbuilders filed with NHTSA, relaying supplier Airxcel's own diagnosis: the capacitance on the FET is insufficient to handle the current flow, so the device fails and heat builds up. The filings add a warning sign with the gentleness of a defibrillator manual: smoke will be present if the board is experiencing a thermal event.[3] For a sealed rooftop fan sitting inches from your RV's headliner, smoke is not an early warning. Smoke is the report card.

To their enormous credit, this one was caught ahead of any fire: not one fire appears in any filing. Grand Design and Foretravel both told NHTSA they had seen zero thermal events in the field as of June 30, 2026.[3] This is the recall system working exactly as designed: supplier-initiated, incident-free, fixed in production by June 24.

So why does the math refuse to close? The parent equipment recall, 26E036000, covers 5,021 fans.[3] Add up the nine downstream vehicle campaigns (Airstream, Tiffin, Grand Design, Winnebago, Foretravel, Forest River, Rossmonster, Kibbi, Grech) and you get about 2,100 vehicles.[1][2][3] Thousands of boards sit outside any VIN-keyed notification list, because the defect was recalled as equipment, not as a vehicle.

Here is where I get judgmental. Under 49 CFR 577.7, an equipment manufacturer must notify only the most recent purchaser it knows about. There is no owner-registration rule for roof fans the way there is for tires and child seats. No public notice happens unless the Administrator orders it.[3] MaxxAir published no recall page, and Airxcel's brand site is silent. The only public inspection procedure exists because Grand Design attached it to a federal filing. Somebody who bought a bare N-series fan from a retailer and screwed it into their Transit roof is unreachable by the entire chain, and nobody told them that.

Which brings me to the actionable part, because this is an engineering story with a ten-second fix. Pull the interior screen on your MaxxFan and twist the four lock-in tabs. Read the label underneath, formatted ####-####N-####. If the middle segment has no N, you are clear. If it shows N or NX, call Airxcel at 574-247-9235, option 1, and get the free replacement board (part 810930). Grand Design's own letter says: do not operate the fan until it's inspected.[3] No tools, no VIN, no excuse.

Federal law guarantees the free remedy for 15 years and makes it illegal to sell the recalled part.[3] That is the one part of this system that works for everyone, invisible buyers included, so use it.
