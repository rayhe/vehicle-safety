The Steering Bolt Recall Was 208,724 Cars. The Real Number Is 4 Million.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
October 1, 2026

Two weeks ago, the number was 208,724, and now it is four million. Same bolt.[1][2] On September 18, NHTSA announced a US recall of the 2018 Tiguan, 2018-2019 Atlas, and 2019-2021 Audi Q3 over a steering-rack mounting bolt that corrodes, snaps, and can take your steering with it. This week, Germany's Handelsblatt reported the true scope: close to four million vehicles worldwide, spanning VW, Skoda, Audi, and Seat. Volkswagen's largest recall since Dieselgate is one part number failing everywhere at once.

Let's talk about what this bolt does in the first 150 milliseconds after it fails. The right-side steering rack mounting bolt, part N.105.524.02, holds the steering rack to the front subframe.[3] Road salt gets to it, and the head snaps off. The rack hangs from a single remaining mount, the aluminum housing cracks under the load, and directional control is gone. Brakes get dual hydraulic circuits, and throttle gets brake override. Steering gets one rack, and when its mounting fails, there is no backup system, no warning light with time to spare, no graceful degradation. That is a design choice, and it is a bad one.

The reason one bolt can menace four million cars is the MQB platform. Volkswagen's modular architecture shares components across the Golf, Golf Variant, Tiguan, Touran, and Caddy, plus more than a million Skodas, roughly 700,000 Audis (the Q3 alone), and 200,000 Seats, including Ateca and Tarraco models built from April 2016 through September 2024.[2] Platform engineering is the industry's greatest efficiency trick: design one part well, install it in everything. The corollary is brutal: design one part badly, with one coating spec that cannot survive road salt, and you have installed the failure in everything. Somewhere in Wolfsburg, a fastener engineer is having the worst month of their career.

Now the honest calibration, because four million sounds apocalyptic and mostly is not. VW estimates about 1% of the recalled US vehicles actually carry the defect, and zero crashes or injuries have been reported in the United States.[1][3] The remedy is a bolt swap, not a buyback; Dieselgate cost VW tens of billions and a generation of trust, while this costs a tube of anti-corrosion coating and a very long dealer schedule. Corrosion is also a geography problem, not a uniform one. A 2024 Golf in Arizona that has never seen road salt is not the risk profile here. A 2018 Tiguan that has endured eight salted Ohio winters is. The recall is the biggest logistics exercise of VW's post-Dieselgate era, and the danger concentrates exactly where the salt does.

There is one more number worth sitting with. In our FARS data, the US models in this recall are among the safest vehicles per mile on the road: the Tiguan at 0.14 deaths per 100 million VMT, the Atlas at 0.06, the Golf at 0.18, against a Honda Accord at 3.07.[4] This recall is preventive, not reactive. Nobody died to trigger it, which is how the system is supposed to work, and it is worth saying so on a site that mostly documents the times it did not.

If you drive a 2018 Tiguan, a 2018-2019 Atlas, or a 2019-2021 Q3 in the US, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls and get the free bolt replacement. It is a steering part, so do not postpone it behind an oil change.[1] If you drive a 2016-2024 MQB Volkswagen, Skoda, or Seat in Europe, watch for notices from your national authority; Germany's KBA has the data, and the corrosion clock is ticking loudest on the oldest, saltiest cars.
