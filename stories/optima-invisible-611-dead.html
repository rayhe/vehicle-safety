Nobody Writes About the Kia Optima. 611 People Died in It Anyway.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
August 10, 2026

The Kia Optima does not appear in car magazines. It does not star in chase scenes. Nobody puts one on a poster. Between 2014 and 2023, 611 people died in fatal crashes involving one, and the collective response from the automotive press has been roughly equivalent to a shrug emoji.[1]

110
Deaths in model year 2015 Optimas alone. Nearly matching the Camaro.

Dig into the model year data and a single vintage jumps off the page. Model year 2015 accounts for 110 of those 611 deaths, or one in every five and a half. That is nearly the same body count as the 2015 Chevrolet Camaro (115 deaths), a 426-horsepower muscle car purpose-built for the kind of driving that kills people.[1] The Optima made 185 horsepower and came with cloth seats.

For perspective: the deadliest single model year of the Corvette produced 24 deaths. Its worst year produced 4.6 times that. Challenger's peak? 69 deaths. Optima beat it by 59%.[1]

Model year 2013 Optimas killed 75 people. Model year 2014 killed 36. Then 2015 tripled the previous year. It was the final year of the third-generation Optima, the one Peter Schreyer designed when Kia decided it wanted to stop being a punchline. It also shipped without an engine immobilizer on most trims, a decision that would later spawn TikTok theft tutorials and a class-action lawsuit.[3]

Impairment data offers no relief. One in five drivers in fatal Optima crashes tested positive for alcohol or drugs (22%), slightly above the sedan class average of 21%.[1] Not a uniquely reckless demographic. An average one, dying at an average rate, which for a car sold on the promise of sensible transportation is its own kind of indictment.

Kia discontinued the Optima nameplate in 2020 and replaced it with the K5. All 611 deaths stayed behind with the old badge. Roughly one million Optimas still registered in the United States did not get an immobilizer retrofit. They got older, cheaper, and more likely to end up in the hands of a buyer for whom a $4,000 used sedan is the only option.[2]

A fair objection: the MY 2015 spike may reflect nothing more than fleet volume. Kia sold about 159,000 Optimas in 2015, the model's sales peak. More cars, more crashes. Per-vehicle, the death rate of 0.58 per 100 million VMT is unremarkable for a midsize sedan. This is not a structurally dangerous car. It is a statistically ordinary one, produced in extraordinary volume, accumulating a body count that nobody reads because it arrives one anonymous crash at a time.

If you drive a 2011 through 2021 Kia Optima, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. Kia has issued multiple recall campaigns for engine fire risk. Also lock your car. Not because the lock will stop anyone with YouTube, but because it is technically a prerequisite for your insurance claim.
