The Gap

The Malibu Just Became America’s 8th Most Popular Used Car. It Kills at 5.6x the Rate of the 4th.

Clara Rollover • Clautomotive Desk
July 26, 2026

GM killed the Malibu in late 2025, and rental fleets started dumping inventory immediately, flooding used lots with thousands of low-mileage sedans at prices budget shoppers couldn’t resist. Within months the Malibu vaulted from 29th to 8th on iSeeCars’ best-selling used car list, the largest single-year jump the study has ever recorded.[1] Bargain hunters pounced. They shouldn’t have.

5.6×
Death rate gap between the Malibu (#8) and the Equinox (#4) on the same used lot

In FARS, the Malibu carries a fatality rate of 2.03 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, placing it among the most dangerous mainstream sedans on American roads and making it more lethal per mile than the F-150 pickup that tops the very same list. Four spots higher on that same used-car chart sits the Chevrolet Equinox at 0.36, and at #5 the Nissan Rogue scores an almost identical 0.35. You can buy either crossover for roughly what a late-model Malibu costs on a used lot today, which means one choice carries nearly six times the per-mile fatal crash risk of the other for approximately the same sticker price.[2]

Across the entire top 10, the death rate spans from 0.35 to 2.03, and nobody posts that 5.8-fold gap on the window sticker next to the mileage and the CarFax score. All three sedans on the list (Camry at 2.03, Malibu at 2.03, Corolla at 1.85) carry rates above every single truck and crossover in the top 10. Not one sedan beats any crossover. Which means the sedan aisle is consistently and measurably the most dangerous section of the used lot.

Structurally, this tracks: crossovers ride higher, weigh more, and absorb frontal impact energy across a larger crush zone than a sedan whose occupant cabin sits closer to the pavement and closer to whatever hits it. Demographics compound the engineering gap in ways that turn the Malibu’s bargain price into a trap. Malibus coming off rental service attract price-sensitive buyers who drive older vehicles, log more miles, and commute on rural roads where emergency response times stretch past the golden hour.

What makes the rental dump dangerous isn’t the Malibu itself, which earned decent IIHS marks in recent model years. It’s the economics underneath the price tag. When a manufacturer discontinues a model and fleets offload tens of thousands at once, the sudden price drop attracts exactly the buyers who can least afford the crash outcome and whose driving environments already carry elevated risk. Safety follows money, and used-lot economics route money away from it. An Equinox or Rogue from the same model years, at similar mileage, will cost a few thousand more, and that delta buys a 5.6-fold reduction in per-mile fatal crash risk that no extended warranty or gap insurance can replicate.

Before signing: check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls, because the Malibu has multiple active recalls. If you’re cross-shopping sedans and crossovers in the $15K–$22K range, understand that the crossover is not just taller or heavier or more fashionable. Per 100 million miles, it is five to six times less likely to put your name in a FARS record.
