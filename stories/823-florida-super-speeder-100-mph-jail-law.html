Florida Made 100 MPH a Jailable Offense. The Physics Had Already Sentenced You.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
September 4, 2026

Here is a fun fact that will ruin your morning commute. Until July of last year, doing 100 miles per hour on a Florida highway was a civil infraction. A fine. Some points. Drive home and tell the story at dinner. The state looked at people hurtling down I-95 at speeds the road was never designed to forgive, and its legal response was, essentially, a parking ticket with ambition.[1]

Florida has now invented a crime for it. House Bill 351 created "dangerous excessive speeding": exceed the limit by 50 mph or more, or drive 100 mph or more in a manner that threatens people or property, and you are no longer a motorist with a citation but a criminal defendant. First conviction buys up to 30 days in jail, a $500 fine, or both; do it again and the numbers double to 90 days and $1,000. Do it twice within five years and the state takes your license for six months to a year. And you cannot pay this one online anymore. Fifty over means a mandatory court appearance, in person, explaining yourself to a judge.[2]

The kinetic energy of a car at 100 mph versus 70 mph. Thirty extra mph, twice the killing energy.

Fourteen months in, the arrests read like a police blotter written by an action movie. A woman in Bonita Springs clocked at 123 mph told deputies she thought it was fine because she was driving a Supra, as though the badge came with a physics exemption.[5] A motorcyclist in Sarasota was photographed doing 116 in a 40-mph zone, the radar gun's reading preserved like a trophy.[6] A driver doing 111 across the Bayside Bridge was pulled from the car in handcuffs on Florida Highway Patrol video. Two street racers in Ocoee learned the new statute's name the hard way at 4 a.m. on Colonial Drive.[8] Twenty-six motorists were apprehended in the law's first month alone.

None of these people were deterred by the old fine schedule, which is precisely the point. The legislature's vote tells you everything: 37-0 in the Senate, 75-38 in the House. Near-unanimous agreement that the existing framework was a joke at triple digits.[2] This law is a confession. The state tried money, money failed, and now it is trying cages.

Physics, meanwhile, had already rendered its verdict, and physics does not offer plea deals. Kinetic energy scales with the square of velocity, so the math is brutal and simple. A car at 100 mph carries 2.04 times the energy of the same car at 70, the speed of the traffic around it. The Sarasota motorcyclist at 116 in a 40 was carrying 8.41 times the energy of every law-abiding driver on that road. Stopping distance obeys the same square law: the 100-mph driver needs twice the pavement to stop as the 70-mph driver, and at 100 mph you are covering 147 feet every second, which means a perfectly average 1.5-second reaction time consumes 220 feet of road before your foot even finds the brake pedal. Every one of those numbers is a death sentence with the serial numbers filed off.

10,035
Projected U.S. speeding-related deaths in 2025, down 11% from 11,288 in 2024. The curve was already bending before the jail cells arrived.

All of which raises an awkward question, because NHTSA's data shows speeding killed 11,288 Americans in 2024, nearly a third of all traffic deaths, but the early 2025 estimates project an 11 percent decline, back to pre-pandemic levels.[3] The first quarter of 2026 posted a 0.99 fatality rate per 100 million miles, the second-lowest ever recorded.[4] Enforcement campaigns and high-visibility policing were bending the curve without a single new criminal statute. Florida may have built a gallows for a problem that was already shrinking.

And here is the part nobody at the bill signing wanted to say out loud. Virginia has treated 85 mph as criminal reckless driving, a misdemeanor carrying up to a year in jail, for years — the threshold was 80 mph for decades before a 2020 tweak raised it.[9] Its triple-digit problem persists anyway. Decades of the harshest speed-criminalization regime in the country did not end triple-digit driving, because the people doing 116 in a 40 are not performing cost-benefit analysis. They are, almost by definition, undeterrable by statute. The deterrence literature keeps delivering the same unfashionable finding: certainty of getting caught beats severity of punishment, every time. Florida's genuinely interesting innovation might not be the jail time at all. It might be the mandatory court appearance, the end of pay-online-and-forget, the one part of the law that increases the certainty that something unpleasant will actually happen to you.

What to do with all this, if you drive in Florida, is straightforward. Understand the new arithmetic: triple digits or 50-over is an arrest now, not a ticket, with a court date you must attend in person, so set the cruise control and mean it. If someone blows past you at triple digits, give them the entire lane and then some, because their crash is already written and you do not want a co-author credit. And support the unsexy stuff, the speed cameras and the road diets and the traffic calming, because those catch the driver doing 12 over on your street, and that driver, multiplied by millions, kills far more people than the Supra doing 123 ever will. The super speeder is spectacular, but the ordinary speeder is the epidemic.

What this analysis does not prove is whether fourteen months of enforcement changed behavior or saved lives; no before-and-after study from Florida's highway safety office exists yet, and arrest anecdotes are vivid, not statistical. The energy and stopping-distance math is illustrative physics for an identical vehicle under identical conditions; real outcomes vary with mass, tires, road surface, and whatever gets hit. NHTSA's 2025 figures are early estimates subject to revision, and "speeding-related" in federal data means speed was a factor, not necessarily the cause. This piece also does not examine Georgia's 2010 Super Speeder law, the naming predecessor that added a $200 state fee, which deserves its own autopsy.
