The 2026 Nissan Kicks Is Two Different Cars. Check the Door Jamb Before You Buy.

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
February 17, 2028

Let's talk about what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a moderate overlap frontal crash, because on October 22, 2026, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety told us something remarkable: a 2026 Nissan Kicks built in May is a materially different safety proposition than a 2026 Nissan Kicks built in July. Same model year, same badge, same window sticker. Different rear seat belts, and different crash kinematics behind them.[1]

June 2026
Build month dividing the Poor-rated Kicks from the Top Safety Pick Kicks

Nissan redesigned the Kicks for the 2025 model year, and the redesigned car scored Poor in IIHS's updated moderate overlap front test, the rear-seat-focused test the Institute tightened in 2024.[2] In that test, the rear dummy's lap belt rode up off the pelvis, which in a real crash means the belt loads the abdomen instead of the hip bones. Nissan revised the rear seat belts and their load limiters; post-June-2026 cars retested Good across all three crashworthiness tests and took the Top Safety Pick. Cars built before the cutoff kept the old hardware.

Audi tried the same trick on the Q3 and failed the exam. Audi revised the Q3's rear belts on cars built after May 2026, and the belt still slipped off the pelvis in testing: Poor, even though the Q3 earned Good everywhere else. One sliding belt killed the whole award.[3] Toyota's redesigned RAV4 stumbled into the same ditch: Acceptable for the standard hybrid, Marginal for the plug-in hybrid, the heavier battery car performing worse in rear-seat kinematics than its lighter sibling on the same platform.[3] The Chevrolet Trailblazer managed Marginal in moderate overlap with enough other middling scores to miss both awards, and the 2027 Volkswagen Jetta, the lone sedan in the round, qualified for neither award either. Six vehicles tested, one award, and the winner is the cheapest car in the group, which proves that price is not a safety feature.

Model year used to be the unit of safety. You looked up "2026 Kicks" on the IIHS ratings page and got an answer. As of this release, that lookup returns two cars: the rating table lists the qualifying entry as "2026-27 Nissan Kicks Small SUV Built after June 2026," with the build cutoff baked into the model name.[1] The used-car market does not think this way. Neither do dealers, insurers, or parents buying a teenager's first car in three years. Only one place prints the truth: the safety certification label on the driver's door frame or sill, the one with the month and year of production that nobody reads until a recall letter arrives.[4]

Now the uncomfortable arithmetic from the FARS data, which I ran because the lab result needs a reality check. The Kicks fleet in our data, the 2018-2022 generation, dies at 0.69 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles. The Toyota RAV4 fleet dies at 0.19, one of the lowest rates in the entire dataset: a 3.6-to-1 real-world gap in the RAV4's favor, while the IIHS trophy sits on the Kicks's shelf. The counterargument deserves full strength: the moderate overlap test is one 40-mph frontal scenario, and a Good there does not make the Kicks safer than the RAV4 in any whole-vehicle sense. If you are strapping a kid into the second row today, the FARS data says RAV4, and it is not close. The IIHS result is a signal about the hardware, not a verdict about the car.

If you are shopping a 2026 Kicks, open the driver's door and read the certification label. After June 2026 means revised belts and a Top Safety Pick; before that, you are buying the Poor car. Cross-shopping the RAV4, Q3, or Trailblazer? None earned an award in this round, and the plug-in RAV4's Marginal should give pause to anyone buying it as the family hauler. Model year is no longer a complete description of the car. The door jamb is.
