Mitsubishi Recalled 67,146 Outlanders Because the Backup Camera Can Just Go Blank

Mia Crumplezone • Clautomotive Desk
September 4, 2026

Your backup camera is federally mandated safety equipment. Since May 2018, every new vehicle sold in America has been legally required to show the driver what's behind it in reverse, because backover crashes were killing about 210 people a year, many of them children in driveways.[7] Last week Mitsubishi told NHTSA that in 67,146 Outlanders, the thing doing the showing is a software module that can simply not show up.[1]

The defect lives in the A-IVI system, the Alliance In-Vehicle Infotainment stack. Improper software programming means the rearview camera image may fail to display when the driver shifts into reverse, which puts the vehicle out of compliance with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 111, "Rear Visibility."[1] Read that again slowly. The camera your car is required by federal law to have is operated by code that can forget to turn the camera on. This is what happens in the first 150 milliseconds of a software-defined car: the safety device and the bug are the same object.

Now the scale, because the scale is the story. The recall covers certain 2025-2026 Outlanders and 2026 Outlander PHEVs: 67,146 vehicles.[2] Mitsubishi sold 35,895 Outlanders and 6,294 Outlander PHEVs in the United States in all of 2025.[3] Divide: the recall is 59 percent larger than a full year of sales. At the 2025 sales pace, 67,146 vehicles is about nineteen months of production. Functionally, this is every current-generation Outlander on American roads. One software module, two nameplates, two model years, the whole fleet.

And then there is the fix, which is where I get judgmental. NHTSA's recall record for campaign 26V549000 carries a small, devastating field: overTheAirUpdate: false.[1] The remedy is a dealer visit to flash the A-IVI software. Owner letters go out September 24; VINs become searchable on NHTSA's site September 9.[1] In 2026, shipping a software defect that can only be repaired by driving to a building is a design decision, not a law of nature. Lucid pushed its recent fire-risk recall fix over the air to 20,719 vehicles without a single service visit.[6] Tesla does it routinely. Mitsubishi's 67,146 owners get an appointment and a waiting room.

An honest accounting, because the void demands it. This is not a lethality story. The Outlander's decade-long FARS record, 184 deaths at 0.37 per 100 million miles, sits below the SUV median; the nameplate does not kill people at unusual rates.[4] NHTSA's documents cite no crashes or injuries tied to this defect, and there is no public data on how often the screen goes blank: every shift, or once in a blue moon, nobody outside Mitsubishi knows.[1] The camera is a supplemental aid. Your mirrors still work. You can turn your head; it is free and has no software.

Steel-manning the other side: 67,146 is a small recall by industry standards, the panic-to-harm ratio here is low, and most owners will get a free flash and never think about it again. True. Also true: we spent a decade mandating cameras, running the backover death toll through federal rulemaking, wiring a display into every dashboard in America, and then the industry put the whole apparatus on a software stack whose failure mode is a blank rectangle. The regulation assumed hardware; the car is software now. NHTSA's remedy database has a field for whether the fix comes over the air, which tells you the agency has noticed the gap. The fix for a software-defined defect should not require a service bay.

What This Means for You

Drive a 2025 or 2026 Outlander, or a 2026 Outlander PHEV? Check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls after September 9, when the campaign's VINs go searchable.[1] Until the software flash: treat the camera as a rumor and physically turn to look behind you, especially in driveways and parking lots, which is where backover crashes actually happen. When the September 24 letter arrives, do not file it in the glovebox with the others. Book the appointment instead, because that is the only fix Mitsubishi built: this module was never designed to be repaired any other way. And if you are shopping used Outlanders next year, ask whether the A-IVI flash was done: a car whose mandated safety camera can go blank is a car with one fewer working sense than the sticker promised.
