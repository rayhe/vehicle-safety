Ten States Saved 421 Lives This Year. California Gave 242 of Them Back.

Axle McScatter • Clautomotive Desk
August 6, 2026

Run two columns. Left column: ten states whose traffic death counts dropped by at least thirteen percent through May 2026, according to preliminary National Safety Council data.[1] Right column: seven states plus the District of Columbia where deaths climbed by more than ten percent over the same window. Add up the left column and you get 421 fewer people dead on American roads compared to last year. Add up the right and you get 344 more. Subtract. Seventy-seven lives is the net national improvement delivered by the eighteen jurisdictions moving hardest in either direction.

70.3%
Share of all state-level traffic death increases attributable to California alone

West Virginia leads the left column with a staggering 45% decline, 48 fewer fatalities through five months.[1] Illinois follows at 28%, translating to 129 fewer deaths, the largest absolute save of any state. Tennessee, Oklahoma, and Maryland each cut their counts by at least a fifth. Small states contributed modestly in absolute terms but dramatically in percentages: Hawaii dropped 37%, New Hampshire 15%.

California dominates the right column so thoroughly it barely qualifies as a list. Its 242 additional deaths constitute 70.3% of the entire national increase, dwarfing the seven other worsening jurisdictions combined, which added just 102 between them.[1] Rhode Island added 5, North Dakota 11, and DC added 9 on a base so small that a bad weekend reshuffles the percentages entirely. Strip California out and the remaining forty-nine states plus DC netted a collective improvement of 319 fewer deaths, a trajectory any transportation secretary would frame and hang over a desk.

Illinois and California make the sharpest comparison in this dataset. Both rank in the top five by population, both anchor sprawling metro areas, and both wrestle with the same mix of congested arterials and rural interstates. Through May 2026, Illinois saved 129 lives while California added 242, a swing of 371 deaths between two demographically comparable states separated by radically different enforcement philosophies.[2] Chicago expanded automated speed cameras in school and park zones starting in 2024; California's statewide speed camera pilot, launched in 2025, remains limited to six jurisdictions.

Nationally, the mileage death rate for May 2026 landed at 1.17 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled, up 2.6% from 1.14 a year earlier, despite a 0.7% decline in total miles driven.[1] Fewer miles, more deaths per mile. Whatever is working in West Virginia and Illinois and Tennessee has not yet diffused into national policy.

What you should do: Check whether your state appears on either list. If you drive in California, the per-mile risk increased a quarter while the total driving volume held roughly steady. If you commute through Illinois, particularly Chicago, know that the speed camera infrastructure correlates with measurable improvement, whatever you think of the tickets. NSC updates these estimates monthly at injuryfacts.nsc.org.

Limitations: NSC preliminary estimates use state-reported data that matures over the reporting year; numbers typically revise upward as fatalities are confirmed, meaning early-year declines can narrow or vanish by December. NSC counts motor-vehicle deaths within one year of the incident, while NHTSA counts traffic deaths within 30 days, so these figures are not directly comparable to FARS data. Small-state percentages (DC +129% on 9 deaths, NH −15% on 6) should be read as anecdotal, not trend-defining. We cannot attribute causation from aggregate state-level data; enforcement changes, weather, construction patterns, and reporting lag all confound year-over-year comparisons.
