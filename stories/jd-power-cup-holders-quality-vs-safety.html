Cup Holders Are the Biggest Improvement in American Car Quality. 37,810 People Died.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
August 1, 2026

JD Power just declared the best year-over-year improvement in new-vehicle quality since 1997.[1] Problems per 100 vehicles dropped from 192 to 175. Big number. Huge progress. The single biggest contributor to that improvement? Cup holders.

175 PP100
JD Power's 2026 quality score. Best since 1997. Led by cup holders.

Not airbags. Not crash avoidance tuning. Cup holders. Their "more accessible location and capacity to hold a variety of sizes of cups/water bottles" moved the needle more than any other change in American automobiles this year.

A different number: 37,810. Americans killed in motor vehicle crashes in 2025.[2]

These two metrics have precisely nothing to do with each other.

JD Power's Initial Quality Study surveys 78,514 buyers about their first 90 days of ownership, asks 227 questions across 10 categories, and measures whether the infotainment paired with your phone, whether the seats squeaked, whether the paint looked right in direct sunlight, and whether your Stanley tumbler fit in the center console without wobbling.[1] Zero of those 227 questions ask whether the vehicle protected its occupant in a crash. Zero cross-reference a VIN against NHTSA's fatality database.

Automakers know this. Ford grabbed the No. 1 mass-market spot at 152 PP100, then launched a July 4 ad campaign: "We put quality first. Now Ford is first in quality."[3] Consumers hear "quality" and picture structural integrity at 70 mph. Ford means cup holders and Bluetooth pairing speed.

That same July, Ford recalled close to two million vehicles. Sixty recalls in 2026, leading every manufacturer. The Bronco recall alone covered 565,691 SUVs whose wiring harnesses could short-circuit and set the engine bay on fire.[4]

0
JD Power IQS questions that measure crash survival.

JD Power does include a "driving assistance" category. Sounds safety-adjacent. It is not. It measures whether ADAS features annoy the driver. A lane-keeping system that aggressively corrects your steering? Complaint. A forward-collision warning that fires three car lengths early? Complaint. An AEB system that phantom-brakes twice before stopping you from rear-ending a semi? Two complaints, one save. IQS counts the complaints.

Perverse incentive, stated plainly: to score well on JD Power, tune your safety systems to intervene less. Soften the warnings. Widen the thresholds. Make the car quieter, more pleasant, and slightly more likely to kill someone.

FARS tells you what actually happens. Ten years of crash data. The Mazda CX-5 posts a death rate of 0.12 per 100 million VMT. The Hyundai Veloster posts 8.54. A 71-to-1 gap.[5] JD Power cannot distinguish between them. A Veloster with excellent cup holders outscores a CX-5 whose infotainment drops Bluetooth once.

IQS measures what it claims to measure: first-ownership annoyance. Fair enough. But "quality" does something in a buyer's brain that no cup holder methodology can support. It implies durability. It implies the thing you bought won't kill you. Cup holders cannot carry that weight.

What to do: Ignore JD Power when shopping for safety. Check IIHS ratings at iihs.org/ratings. Check NHTSA's 5-star program. Enter your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls. A car that won a JD Power award and has an active fire recall is exactly as dangerous as it sounds.
