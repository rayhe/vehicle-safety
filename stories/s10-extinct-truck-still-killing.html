The Chevy S-10 Was Discontinued in 2004. It Still Kills 143 People a Year.

Vin Wreckage • Clautomotive Desk
July 24, 2026

Somewhere in rural America right now, a Chevrolet S-10 with 230,000 miles on the odometer is hauling a load of firewood down a two-lane road at dusk, no electronic stability control, no side airbags, its crumple zones designed by engineers who retired before the iPhone existed. Between 2014 and 2023, vehicles like it appeared in 1,890 fatal crashes that killed 1,427 people. General Motors stopped building the S-10 twenty-two years ago. The truck does not care.

4.83
S-10 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled — 17× its replacement, the Colorado (0.28)

The FARS database is a decade-long ledger of every fatal crash on American roads, and inside it the S-10 occupies a peculiar position: a vehicle that no longer exists as a product but continues to exist as a body count. Its death rate of 4.83 per 100 million VMT ranks it among the deadliest vehicles still tracked, higher than the Ford Mustang (6.02 if you want a sports car, sure, but the Mustang is sold as dangerous), higher than the Nissan Altima (2.88), the Honda Accord (3.07), every sedan and crossover on the road. At under 3,000 pounds and built before federal crash standards caught up with the physics of offset barriers, the S-10 is a compact pickup designed for an era when "safety" meant "wear your seatbelt and hope."

GM introduced a successor in 2004. Colorado: same showroom slot, same buyer, same corporate ecosystem. Colorado's FARS death rate is 0.28 per 100 million VMT, which is not a typo. One generation of engineering, one ESC mandate, one set of side airbags, one modern crash structure later, and the same company's version of the same truck kills at one-seventeenth the rate.

Wreckage does not normally do math in public, but this one deserves it: 4.83 divided by 0.28 is 17.25, a 94.2% reduction in fatality rate from one model generation to the next, within a single manufacturer, within a single market segment. Nobody published that number because nobody thought to divide a dead truck by a living one.

Peak model year for S-10 fatalities in the FARS window is 2000, which contributed 178 deaths between 2014 and 2023. A truck built in 2000 that killed someone in 2020 had been on the road for two decades, accumulating rust, deferred brake jobs, bald tires, and owners who could not afford anything newer. Model year deaths peak between 1997 and 2003, meaning the fatal fleet consists overwhelmingly of trucks that were 11 to 26 years old at the time of the crashes, vehicles that survived past the average fleet age of 12.6 years precisely because pickup trucks are mechanically durable, which is the worst kind of compliment a safety analyst can pay a vehicle: it lasts long enough to keep killing.

And it was not alone in death. An entire generation of compact pickups went extinct in the mid-2000s, and together they constitute a rolling graveyard that no recall can reach. Ford's pre-2019 Ranger carries a 2.91 rate and 3,089 deaths. Dodge Dakota: 2.62 rate, 1,237 dead. GMC Sonoma, the S-10's platform twin wearing a different grille: 2.13 rate, 378 dead. Added together, these four discontinued compact pickups killed 6,131 people in the FARS window, and every single one of them lacked stability control, lacked side curtain airbags, and lacked the structural engineering that makes a modern Colorado or Tacoma (rate: 0.80) survivable in the kinds of crashes that routinely kill S-10 occupants.

Wreckage will write the counterargument first, since it writes itself: the comparison is unfair. S-10s still on the road are old, worn, owned by people who cannot afford maintenance, driven in rural counties where the nearest trauma center is forty minutes by helicopter. Colorado drivers are newer-car owners, better-maintained, suburban dads with comprehensive insurance. Fleet composition distorts the raw rate comparison, and 17x overstates the pure engineering delta. True enough, but the IIHS published peer-reviewed evidence that ESC alone cuts single-vehicle fatal crashes by roughly 50 percent[1]. Side curtain airbags reduce near-side fatalities by approximately 30 percent[2]. Neither technology existed in the S-10. Stack those two improvements and you already account for a 65% reduction before you touch crash structure, materials, or occupant geometry, so while the 17x number carries fleet-age noise, the engineering improvement underneath it is not a statistical artifact. It is the reason people walk away from crashes in a Colorado that would have killed them in an S-10.

No fix exists for the remaining S-10 fleet. No recall will add electronic stability control to a 2001 truck with a body-on-frame chassis that was never designed for it. No dealer campaign will retrofit side curtain airbags into headliners that have no mounting points. S-10s still on the road will keep aging, keep accumulating miles, keep showing up in FARS until the last one rusts through its frame rails or gets traded to a scrapyard for $400. NHTSA estimates that the average vehicle on U.S. roads is 12.6 years old, and the deadliest S-10s are twice that, older than some of the people driving them.

This is the part of vehicle safety that nobody markets. GM will tell you the 2026 Colorado earned a five-star NHTSA rating and comes standard with ten airbags and automatic emergency braking. What GM will not tell you is that its corporate ancestor, the truck it replaced, is still killing 143 people per year in the same FARS data that feeds those five-star ratings, and no engineering advancement can retroactively save them because the truck was built before the engineering existed. Safety is not a product feature; it is a fleet problem, and the fleet has a long, rusting tail.
