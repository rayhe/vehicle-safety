Takata's Ghost: A 2021 Lawsuit Predicted This Airbag Recall. GM Needed Three Tries.

Dale Impactor III • Clautomotive Desk
September 30, 2026

An airbag inflator is a pressure vessel that spends a decade sitting inches from your head, waiting for one violent fraction of a second of work. This week GM recalled 1,559 of them for the third time this year, because they can rupture without a crash and spray metal fragments through the cabin.[1] NHTSA campaign 26V611 covers 2016 and 2017 Chevrolet Silverado 1500, Silverado HD, GMC Sierra 1500, and Sierra HD trucks, crew cab only. Two earlier recalls of the same defect family, 26V166 and its expansion 26V325, were supposed to have handled this.

Roof-rail airbag recalls GM has issued for this defect in 2026 alone, across campaigns 26V166, 26V325, and now 26V611

What failed is a small, specific chain of chemistry. GM's defect report says the suspect inflators carry two manufacturing flaws that combine: a hairline crack in the canister introduced during production, and a small amount of water left inside after the washing process.[2] Water plus steel plus a decade of thermal cycling equals stress-corrosion cracking, which eats the canister from the inside out. When the wall finally lets go, the end cap separates or the sidewall splits, and a pressurized vessel designed to inflate a curtain becomes a fragmentation device positioned directly over the occupants.

GM found out the way automakers always find out. In late July, a customer reported that the left-side roof-rail airbag of a 2016 Silverado 1500 ruptured shortly after the truck started moving. Not in a crash. In motion. An inspection found a split inflator sidewall and damage to the headliner and roof. The occupants walked away with ringing in their ears.[3] GM traced the failure to supplier production lot B116B and opened its third recall of the defect.

The ghost in the supply chain

The suspect inflators were built by Joyson Safety Systems at a plant in Hermoso, Mexico.[3] Joyson Safety Systems is the company assembled from Takata's corpse. In April 2018, Key Safety Systems, owned by China's Ningbo Joyson Electronics, bought substantially all of Takata's global assets out of cross-border bankruptcy for about $1.6 billion, explicitly leaving the old airbag recall liabilities behind, and rebranded as Joyson Safety Systems.[4] The deal bought the factories, the engineers, and the customer contracts. It did not buy the debts. What it appears to have bought, alongside the assets, is the same failure mode: an inflator that ruptures and sends metal into the cabin.

And here is the part that makes the third recall the story, rather than the second or the first. In 2021, a proposed class action against GM, FCA, and Joyson described this exact defect in these exact trucks. An 88-page complaint alleged that Joyson's inflators were contaminated by moisture during manufacturing at the same Mexico facility that had made earlier Takata airbags, and that the resulting interior corrosion could make them explode spontaneously, spraying shrapnel into driver and passenger compartments.[5] It named the 2015 and 2016 Silverado and Sierra roof-rail airbags specifically. The plaintiffs argued the automakers knew or should have known, given Joyson's lineage. That lawsuit sat in the public record for five years. GM is now on its third recall of the same defect.

The year a class-action complaint named these trucks and this moisture-corrosion failure mode, five years before GM's third recall

The chemistry here deserves a moment, because it is the entire reason the recalls keep expanding. A crack you cannot see plus water you cannot see, sealed inside a pressure vessel you will never inspect, mounted where you will never look, with a failure that announces itself by tearing your headliner apart. Each recall covered the lots GM could trace at the time. 26V166 found some. 26V325 found more. 26V611 found lot B116B, which the first two missed. The corrosion does not care which recall you were in. It just keeps working.

The counterargument, at full strength

GM will say, correctly, that recalls are how the system is supposed to work. The first two campaigns covered the lots the company had identified through part traceability records at the time. The July rupture surfaced a new lot, B116B, and the company investigated, traced, and recalled within two months. Each recall was narrow because a narrow recall is precise, and a precise recall is fast. A sweeping "replace everything from that plant" campaign would have grounded hundreds of thousands of trucks for a defect confirmed in a single vehicle. You cannot recall on suspicion; you recall on evidence, and evidence arrives one ruptured inflator at a time.

That defense is honest as far as it goes. It just has a hole in it shaped like the 2021 lawsuit. Somebody wrote down the mechanism, the plant, the vehicle population, and the predicted outcome years before the first recall. The evidence arrived early. The recall arrived late. Three times.

What to do

If you drive a 2016 or 2017 Silverado or Sierra crew cab, check your VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls now. Do not wait for the letter; GM does not expect to mail owner notifications until November 9, and VINs have been searchable since September 24.[2] If your truck is in 26V611, the dealer replaces both roof-rail airbag modules free, and the recall number is N262569920 if the service desk asks. Regular cab and double cab models are not in this campaign, and trucks outside lot B116B are not either, at least as far as GM currently knows.

If you drive a 2015 Silverado or Sierra, or a 2015 through 2020 Ram 1500, 2500, or 3500 with side-curtain airbags from the same supplier, understand that the 2021 complaint named your trucks too, and no recall has yet covered them. Watch your recall inbox like it matters, because the corrosion keeps working whether the campaign arrives or not.

Limitations

The 2021 filing was a proposed class action, not a court finding of fact; its allegations about what GM and Joyson knew are claims, not verdicts. I have not read the Part 573 defect report itself, only NHTSA's campaign summary and press coverage of it, so the lot-tracing details come from GM's filings as reported. The full scope of affected production lots beyond B116B is unknown; GM says it traced the defect through supplier records, which is only as good as the records. And the injury count for this specific defect remains low, one ruptured inflator in July with no injuries beyond ringing ears, which is lucky, not safe.
